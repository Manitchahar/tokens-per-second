Everything in the last two lessons is a claim about a queue. This lab is where you check the claims.

You get a simulator of the engine loop — the one from *Long-prompt interference & chunked prefill* in Lesson 10.1 — and five knobs. Your job is to find the configuration that maximizes <Term t="goodput">goodput</Term>: the fraction of requests that meet an interactive SLO, not the one that maximizes raw tokens per second.

<Scrolly>
  <MixedWorkload client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. A mixed workload">
    Short chat prompts and long RAG prompts sharing one GPU. Thirty-five percent of requests are long, because that is roughly what a product with retrieval looks like, and long prompts want the whole GPU while short ones want a slice.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. Turn off everything">
    Unoptimized: no chunking, no prefix cache. A long prefill takes the GPU in one step and everyone behind it waits. Notice it is the **p99**, not the mean, that collapses. Long prompts are a minority, so they barely move the average and completely destroy the tail.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Chunked prefill only">
    Cut every prefill into chunks that fit one decode step. The median barely moves and total throughput barely moves, but the tail stops being a cliff. This is a fairness fix, and noticing that throughput is unchanged is the important part.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Add the prefix cache">
    Every request shares a system prompt and, for the long ones, retrieved context. Cache them and long prompts now cost only their unique tokens. Both latency and throughput improve — the only lever in this lab that moves both.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Push the rate">
    Raise arrivals and watch the trade-off. Throughput per user falls, total throughput rises, and goodput is the first thing to go. A saturated engine still produces plenty of tokens; it just produces most of them too late to be useful.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Pick the config">
    Chunk small enough for fairness, cache as much as the tree holds, and accept the throughput ceiling that physics sets. Then raise the rate as far as goodput allows — that number, not tokens per second, is your capacity.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Part 1 · On the real GPU: measure the two phase costs

Start on hardware. Everything above is arithmetic on paper; this part confirms the constants with your own measurements, which is what makes the rest of the lab real.

Use **Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct**. It has 0.49B parameters, so a 1,024-token prefill is a few milliseconds on a T4 — fast enough to sweep. The ratios you measure transfer to 8B even though the absolute numbers do not, because both scale linearly in parameter count.

The notebook times:

1. Prefill at 128, 256, 512, 1024 tokens, in FP16 and in FP32 — confirming prefill time scales with tokens and shrinks with precision.
2. Decode for batches of 1, 8, 32, 64 — confirming decode is memory-bound and nearly flat in batch size.
3. The **crossover chunk size**: the chunk at which prefill time equals one decode step. This is the chunk-size formula from the lesson, measured rather than assumed.
4. The interference penalty: time a decode step alone, then time a step that also does an 8,192-token prefill, and record the ratio.

Record `c_star`, `t_prefill_per_token`, and `t_decode_step` in your notes. You will use them in Part 2.

## Part 2 · Compute the interference by hand

Now use your measured constants to predict what the simulator will show, *before* you run it. For a service with 32 decoding users at 1,024 context and a long prompt of $n$ tokens arriving:

```
stall_ms          = t_prefill(n) - t_decode_step        (floored at 0)
other_users_tpot  = t_decode_step + stall_ms
speedup_factor    = t_decode_step / other_users_tpot
wasted_GPU_ms     = stall_ms                            (per long request)
```

Do this for prompt lengths of 2,000, 8,000 and 32,000 tokens and write the table down. The predicted `speedup_factor` at 8,000 tokens is the number that should make you wince.

## Part 4 · The deliverable

Write a one-page **tuning memo** for the service described in Part 1. It must contain:

- the chosen configuration (chunk size, cache on/off, and the rate you would deploy at);
- your measured p50 and p99 TTFT and mean TPOT at that configuration;
- the predicted capacity in requests per second, from the goodput-0.9 point;
- one sentence per knob explaining why it is set where it is;

## Part 5 · Take it to a real engine

If you have a GPU with more than 16 GB, run vLLM with the same workload and confirm the simulator's shape:

```bash
# Chunked prefill with a 2,048-token budget and prefix caching on
vllm serve Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \
  --max-model-len 16384 \
  --enable-prefix-caching \
  --max-num-batched-tokens 2048 \
  --disable-log-requests
```

Send a mix of 200-token and 8,000-token prompts at a controlled rate (the notebook has a small load generator) and compare your measured p99 TTFT with the simulator's. Expect the same *shape* and a different absolute number, because a real engine adds attention implementations, scheduling fairness and launch overhead the simulator omits. **Predicting the shape and then explaining the gap is the skill.**

<KeyNotes>

  1. Measure prefill and decode separately before modelling anything. Prefill time scales with tokens; decode time is nearly flat in batch size.
  2. Derive the crossover chunk size from your own measurements. It is the chunk whose prefill time equals one decode step.
  3. Interference is a **p99** problem, not a mean problem. Long prompts are a minority of traffic and completely dominate the tail.
  4. Chunking buys fairness at a small throughput cost. It bounds the worst wait; it does not reduce the arithmetic.
  5. Prefix caching is the only lever in this lab that improves latency *and* throughput, because it removes GPU work rather than reordering it.
  6. The chunk size is the fairness/throughput dial: smaller is fairer and slower, larger is faster and cliff-like.
  7. Goodput is the capacity number: find the arrival rate at which it crosses 0.9 and deploy below it.
  8. Recompute the chunk size when the hardware or model changes — it is a ratio of decode time to prefill time per token, and both terms move.
</KeyNotes>
