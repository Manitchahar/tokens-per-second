We now have two ingredients. An SFT model that answers in the right format (lesson 3C.2), and a reward model that scores any answer the way people would (lesson 3C.3). The question is how to use the second to improve the first.

The first idea, best-of-N, works but only at inference time and at N times the cost. We want the improvement *in the weights*, so that a single sample is already good. That calls for a different kind of training. There is no correct answer to imitate any more, only a score for whatever the model happens to write. The model has to **try things and learn from the grades**, which is what reinforcement learning is.

This lesson follows the paper that made the recipe famous: *Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback* by Ouyang et al. (2022), the InstructGPT paper. It is also where a problem appears that every later method has to deal with. Optimise hard enough against a learned grader and the model learns to fool the grader instead of pleasing people.

## The recipe in one picture

The InstructGPT recipe has three steps, each with its own data:

| Step | Data | Trains |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · SFT | 12,725 prompts with labeler demonstrations | the SFT policy, from GPT-3 |
| 2 · Reward model | rankings of 4–9 answers on 33,207 prompts | a 6B reward model, from the SFT model |
| 3 · RL with PPO | 31,144 prompts, *no labels at all* | the final policy, from the SFT policy |

Step 3 needs only prompts. The model writes the answers and the reward model grades them, so no human is in the loop. That is what makes it scale: a reward model can grade millions of answers, a person a few hundred a day. It is called <Term t="rlhf">reinforcement learning from human feedback</Term> (RLHF) because the feedback that trained the grader came from people. In RL vocabulary, the model being trained is the <Term t="policy">policy</Term>: it takes a situation (the prompt and the tokens so far) and chooses an action (the next token).

  <Step title="1. Sample, score, nudge">
    The policy, initialised as the SFT model, writes an answer to a prompt. The reward model scores it. The policy is nudged so that high-scoring answers become more likely. Repeat for every prompt, many times. No human is in this loop.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. The reward goes up">
    Early on, everything the reward model rewards is something people actually like. Answers become clearer and more specific, and the policy **drifts away from the SFT model** it started as. We measure the drift with KL divergence.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Keep pushing: it keeps going up">
    Optimise longer and the reward model's score keeps climbing. It has no ceiling, because the further the policy drifts, the stranger its answers are, and the less the reward model's training data says about them.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. True quality turns down">
    Now ask people. Their judgement **rises, peaks and falls**. Past the peak the policy is exploiting the reward model's mistakes: padding, flattery, the confident tone the grader happens to like. This is **reward hacking**.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. The KL leash">
    Subtract a penalty from the reward: β times the KL divergence between the policy and the SFT model. Drifting far now costs reward. Near the SFT model the reward model is on familiar ground, so its scores can be trusted.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Choose the strength β">
    Drag β. Too small and the policy overshoots into hacking; too large and it barely leaves SFT. In these toy units the best β puts the policy right at the peak. InstructGPT reported that values around **0.01–0.02** worked best for its rewards.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. PPO-ptx: keep the old skills">
    RL against the reward model also made GPT-3 worse at some standard benchmarks the reward model never checks: an **alignment tax**. Mixing ordinary pretraining gradients into every update, a variant called **PPO-ptx**, removed most of it.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. What it bought">
    Labelers preferred the **1.3B** InstructGPT to the **175B** GPT-3, over 100 times larger. The 175B InstructGPT won **85 ± 3%** of comparisons against GPT-3, and 71 ± 4% against GPT-3 with a carefully written few-shot prompt.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Then derive it properly

### The objective

For a prompt $x$ drawn from the RL prompt set, the policy $\pi_\phi^{\text{RL}}$ samples an answer $y$. We want high reward without straying far from the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. The InstructGPT objective writes exactly that:

$$
\text{objective}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{x,\; y \sim \pi_\phi^{\text{RL}}} \Big[ r_\theta(x, y) \;-\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi^{\text{RL}}(y \mid x)}{\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)} \Big] \;+\; \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D_{\text{pretrain}}} \big[ \log \pi_\phi^{\text{RL}}(x) \big] .
$$

Read it term by term.

- $r_\theta(x, y)$ is the reward model's score, the thing we want to push up.
- $\log \frac{\pi^{\text{RL}}(y \mid x)}{\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)}$ asks: how much more likely is this answer under the new policy than under the SFT model? Averaged over the policy's own samples, it is exactly the KL divergence $\mathrm{KL}(\pi^{\text{RL}} \,\|\, \pi^{\text{SFT}})$ from lesson 3C.1. Multiplied by $\beta$ it is the <Term t="kl-penalty">KL penalty</Term>, the leash.
- The last term, weighted by $\gamma$, is ordinary next-token log-likelihood on pretraining text. With $\gamma = 0$ the method is called **PPO**; with $\gamma > 0$ it is **PPO-ptx**. More on it below.

Because an answer's probability is the product of its token probabilities (the autoregressive chain from Module 03), its log-ratio is a **sum over tokens**. That makes the penalty cheap to compute: you already have the policy's log-probabilities from sampling, and one forward pass of the frozen SFT model over the same tokens gives the rest.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: a penalised reward">
  The policy writes a four-token answer. The reward model scores it $r = 1.8$. Token by token:

  | token | $\log \pi^{\text{RL}}$ | $\log \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ | log-ratio |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | −0.10 | −0.90 | 0.80 |
  | 2 | −0.40 | −0.60 | 0.20 |
  | 3 | −0.05 | −0.50 | 0.45 |
  | 4 | −0.30 | −1.10 | 0.80 |
  | **sum** | | | **2.25 nats** |

  The policy has made this answer $e^{2.25} = 9.5$ times more likely than the SFT model would have. With InstructGPT's order of magnitude, $\beta = 0.02$:

  $$
  r - \beta \cdot 2.25 = 1.8 - 0.045 = 1.755 .
  $$

  A small tax on one answer. But KL grows as training pushes the policy further, and it grows with answer length, since every token adds its log-ratio. At $\beta = 0.2$ the same answer would score ${}1.8 - 0.45 = 1.35$: a quarter of its reward gone. β sets the exchange rate between reward and drift.
</Callout>

### Why the leash: reward hacking

The reward model was trained on answers produced by the SFT model. It is a good judge of answers *like those*. Show it something far outside that distribution and its score is an extrapolation, and extrapolations are where models are confidently wrong.

An optimiser is very good at finding those spots. If the reward model slightly over-rewards enthusiasm, the policy becomes enthusiastic. If it over-rewards length, answers get longer. Each step raises the score; past some point none of them raises the quality. This is <Term t="reward-hacking">reward hacking</Term>, a case of Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured it carefully. They used a large "gold" reward model in place of people, trained smaller proxy reward models on its labels, and optimised policies against the proxies. The proxy score kept rising with the distance $d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$ from the starting policy, while the gold score rose, peaked and fell, in smooth curves whose peak moved further out as the proxy reward model got bigger. The visual uses toy curves of that shape. In a real run the curves are not visible: you find the peak by evaluating checkpoints with people or a trusted judge (Module B2).

The KL penalty is the standard defence. In the toy model the reward rises like $d$ and the penalty like $\beta d^2$, so the policy settles where $d - \beta d^2$ is largest, at $d^* = 1/(2\beta)$ in those units. Halve $\beta$ and the policy wanders twice as far. The real dynamics are messier, but the trade is the same: $\beta$ decides how much the policy may trust the reward model.

<Callout type="intuition" title="A grader with a narrow field of view">
  Picture a teacher who has only ever marked essays from one class. Within that range of styles they mark fairly. A student who writes something wildly different, very long, full of flattery, gets marks that mean nothing, high or low. The KL penalty tells the student: you may improve, but stay recognisably in the style the teacher has seen, because that is where their marks can be trusted.
</Callout>

### How the policy is nudged: PPO in plain words

The optimiser InstructGPT used is **PPO**, proximal policy optimisation (Schulman et al., 2017). You do not need its full derivation to work with RLHF, but you should know its three moving parts.

1. **Make good samples likelier.** The core of any policy-gradient method: after sampling $y$, increase $\log \pi(y \mid x)$ in proportion to how good $y$ turned out. Good answers get more probable, bad ones less.
2. **Compare with what was expected.** "How good" is measured relative to a baseline: the advantage $A = $ (penalised reward) − (the reward we expected for this prompt). A second network, the **value model**, predicts that expected reward; InstructGPT initialised it from the reward model. A mediocre answer to an easy prompt then counts as bad news, not good news.
3. **Take small steps.** PPO computes the ratio $\rho = \pi_{\text{new}}(y \mid x) / \pi_{\text{old}}(y \mid x)$ between the policy after and before the update, and clips it to $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$, with $\varepsilon = 0.2$ a common choice:

$$
L^{\text{CLIP}} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(\rho A,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho,\, 1 - \varepsilon,\, 1 + \varepsilon)\, A\big)\Big] .
$$

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: the clip">
  An answer has advantage $A = +0.5$. After a few gradient steps on the same batch, the policy already makes it 1.5 times as likely as before: $\rho = 1.5$. Unclipped the objective would be ${}1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75$, and it would keep rewarding further increases. Clipped, $\mathrm{clip}(1.5, 0.8, 1.2) \times 0.5 = 0.6$, and the minimum of 0.75 and 0.6 is 0.6, a constant: the gradient for pushing this answer higher is now zero. **One batch of samples cannot move the policy by more than about 20% per answer.** That is the "proximal" in the name.
</Callout>

So a PPO step for RLHF holds four models at once: the policy being trained, the frozen SFT reference (for the KL term), the reward model, and the value model. At InstructGPT's scale that is a 175B policy, a 175B reference, a 6B reward model and a 6B value model. In 16-bit weights alone:

$$
2\ \text{bytes} \times (175 + 175 + 6 + 6) \times 10^{9} = 724\ \text{GB}
$$

before any optimiser state or activations, at least ten 80 GB GPUs just to hold the weights. And every PPO iteration begins with **generation**: thousands of sampled answers, token by token. For an inference engineer, that is the punchline. RLHF training is, in wall-clock terms, dominated by inference, and the engines in Parts 2–5 of this course are what make it affordable. [Batch inference & RL rollouts](/learn/specialized-workloads/batch-rl-rollouts/) (Module 26) picks this up.

### PPO-ptx and the alignment tax

RL against the reward model made the policy better at what labelers rated and, at the same time, measurably worse at some public benchmarks: question answering (SQuAD, DROP), commonsense completion (HellaSwag) and French-to-English translation. Nothing in the reward model checks those skills, so nothing stops the policy drifting away from them. The authors call this cost an <Term t="alignment-tax">alignment tax</Term>, and an aligned model with a large tax is a hard sell.

Their fix is the $\gamma$ term. Alongside every RL batch, take a batch of *pretraining* text and add its ordinary next-token log-likelihood to the objective. The policy is pulled back toward everything pretraining taught, not just toward the SFT model. The paper reports that PPO-ptx removed most of the regressions while keeping labeler preference about as high, and that simply raising the KL coefficient did not achieve the same. The final InstructGPT models are PPO-ptx models.

### What it bought, and what it did not

The headline is in the visual. On prompts from the API, labelers preferred the **1.3B** InstructGPT to the **175B** GPT-3, a model with more than 100 times the parameters. The 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 **85 ± 3%** of the time, and to GPT-3 given a carefully written few-shot prompt **71 ± 4%** of the time (95% confidence intervals). Recall the compute from lesson 3C.1: this cost about 1.6% of GPT-3's pretraining.

The paper is just as clear about the limits, and you should be too:

- **Truthfulness improved.** On TruthfulQA, InstructGPT gave truthful and informative answers about twice as often as GPT-3, and on closed-domain tasks (summarise *this* text) it made up information not present in the input about half as often, roughly 21% versus 41%.
- **Toxicity improved only a little, and only when asked.** With a prompt asking for a respectful reply, InstructGPT produced about 25% fewer toxic outputs than GPT-3. Without that instruction there was no clear gain, and when explicitly asked to be toxic it was *more* toxic, because it followed instructions better.
- **Bias did not improve** on the benchmarks they used (Winogender, CrowS-Pairs).
- **It still made simple mistakes**: following false premises in a question, hedging on simple questions, and failing to follow instructions with several constraints.

Notice what "aligned" means here. The model is aligned to *the preferences of a few dozen labelers, following instructions written by OpenAI researchers, on prompts from early API customers*. The paper says so itself. Every preference dataset carries the values and blind spots of the people who produced it.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Reading RLHF results">
  "Preferred 85% of the time" is a win rate on a particular prompt distribution, judged by particular people. It is not 85% correct, and it does not transfer automatically to your prompts. When a model card says a model "beats" another on human preference, ask: whose preferences, on what prompts, and against which baseline prompting? Module B2 is about asking those questions systematically.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mentor" title="Where RLHF went next">
  At the time of writing, PPO-based RLHF is still used, but it is heavy: four models, a sampling loop, and many hyperparameters to tune. The next lesson derives DPO, which gets a very similar effect with one supervised-looking loss and no reward model at all. Many open models now use DPO or a relative of it for preference tuning, and keep online RL for tasks with checkable answers such as maths and code, where the "reward model" can be a test that cannot be hacked as easily.
</Callout>
