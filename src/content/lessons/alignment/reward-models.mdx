Supervised finetuning got us an assistant that answers in the right format and stops. Now look at its answers closely and you will find the limits from the end of the last lesson. Some answers are fine. Some are vague, some are padded, some are confidently wrong. And SFT has no way to hear about it: every demonstration said *"answer like this"*, and none said *"this answer is better than that one"*.

The fix that sounds obvious is to write more and better demonstrations. It hits a wall quickly. Writing an excellent answer to a hard prompt can take a skilled person many minutes. Two skilled people will write two different "ideal" answers, so the target itself is fuzzy. And the model can only learn to be as good as the writers' patience on a Tuesday afternoon.

This lesson swaps the question we ask people. Instead of *"write the best answer"*, ask *"which of these two answers is better?"*. Then turn the answers into a model that can make that judgement on its own, millions of times, without a person in the loop.

## Comparing is easier than creating

Think about how you judge a restaurant. You could not cook the dish, but you can taste two and say which you prefer in a second, and you will give the same verdict tomorrow. Judging is easier than producing, and that is true of text too: a reader can recognise the clearer explanation, the one without the factual slip, the one that actually followed the instruction, even if they could not have written it.

So the new kind of training data is <Term t="preference-data">preference data</Term>: the model generates several answers to a prompt, a person says which they prefer, and each record is a triple, *(prompt, chosen answer, rejected answer)*. In equations the chosen answer is written $y_w$ (for winner) and the rejected one $y_l$ (for loser).

Comparisons are also more *consistent*. In the InstructGPT project, labelers agreed with each other on which output was better roughly three times out of four. That sounds low until you remember the alternative: two people's free-written answers would essentially never match. Agreement around 73–77% is about the ceiling any model of human preference can hope to reach, and it is worth remembering when you measure one.

  </Step>

    Add the same constant to both scores and nothing changes. A reward has **no zero and no units**, only gaps. Remember this when someone shows you a reward of 3.7 and asks if it is good.

## Then derive it properly

### From two scores to a probability

We want a model that, given a prompt $x$ and two answers, predicts which one a person will prefer. The cleanest way to get there is to give every answer a single number, its <Term t="reward-model">reward</Term> $r(x, y)$, and let the *difference* decide.

The rule used everywhere is the <Term t="bradley-terry">Bradley-Terry model</Term>, published by Ralph Bradley and Milton Terry in 1952 for ranking things from paired comparisons:

$$
P(y_A \succ y_B \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_A) - r(x, y_B)\big) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_A - r_B)}} ,
$$

where $\succ$ reads "is preferred to" and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid you met as the S-shaped squashing function in Module 03. Rearranged, it says something tidier: the **odds** that A wins are $e^{r_A - r_B}$. Rewards live on a log-odds scale. If you know chess ratings, this is the same idea: the rating difference between two players sets the odds of who wins (Elo uses base 10 and a scale of 400, but the shape is identical). Module B2 uses exactly this model to rank chatbots from head-to-head votes.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: the probability and the odds">
  Take $r_A = 1.2$ and $r_B = 0.4$. The gap is $\Delta = 0.8$.

  $$
  \sigma(0.8) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.8}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.4493} = 0.6900 .
  $$

  The odds are $e^{0.8} = 2.23$: the model thinks A is preferred about 2.2 times as often as B, and indeed $0.690 / 0.310 = 2.23$.

  Two landmarks worth memorising: a gap of 0 gives exactly 0.5, and a gap of 2 gives $\sigma(2) = 0.881$. Every additional unit of gap multiplies the odds by $e = 2.72$.
</Callout>

### The loss is just maximum likelihood

We have a dataset of comparisons where people chose $y_w$ over $y_l$. A good reward model makes those choices likely. Maximising the probability of the observed choices, or equivalently minimising its negative log, gives the loss for one comparison:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\log \sigma\big(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l)\big) .
$$

If that looks familiar, it should. It is the binary cross-entropy from a yes/no classifier, where the "logit" being classified is the score gap. It is also exactly the cross-entropy idea from Module 3A: the loss is the surprise, in nats, at what actually happened.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: loss and gradient for one comparison">
  People chose A, so $r_w = 1.2$, $r_l = 0.4$:

  $$
  \mathcal{L} = -\ln \sigma(0.8) = -\ln 0.6900 = 0.371\ \text{nats}.
  $$

  Had the model got the order wrong, say $r_w = 0.4$ and $r_l = 1.2$, the loss would be $-\ln \sigma(-0.8) = -\ln 0.3100 = 1.171$ nats: more than three times larger.

  The gradient has a beautifully simple form. With $\Delta = r_w - r_l$,

  $$
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial r_w} = -\big(1 - \sigma(\Delta)\big) = -0.310, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial r_l} = +\big(1 - \sigma(\Delta)\big) = +0.310 .
  $$

  Gradient descent subtracts the gradient, so the winner's score goes up and the loser's goes down, each by a step proportional to $1 - \sigma(\Delta)$: the probability the model currently puts on the *wrong* outcome. When the model is already confident and right, $1 - \sigma(\Delta) \to 0$ and the pair stops teaching it.
</Callout>

In code, the whole objective is three lines once you have the scores:

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F

r_w = reward_model(chosen_ids)      # [batch] one score per chosen answer
r_l = reward_model(rejected_ids)    # [batch] one score per rejected answer
loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l).mean()
```

Use `logsigmoid` rather than `log(sigmoid(...))`: for a large negative gap the sigmoid underflows to 0 and the log becomes infinite, the same numerical-stability lesson as subtracting the max before a softmax (Module 01).

### Rankings: more comparisons for the same reading

Reading answers is the expensive part of a labeler's job. InstructGPT's interface showed between $K = 4$ and $K = 9$ answers to one prompt and asked for a ranking. A ranking of $K$ items implies an order for every pair:

$$
\binom{K}{2} = \frac{K(K-1)}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad K = 4: 6 \text{ pairs}, \qquad K = 9: 36 \text{ pairs}.
$$

The InstructGPT reward model was trained on rankings for **33,207 prompts** (6,623 written by labelers, 26,584 from API users). At 6 to 36 comparisons each, that is somewhere between 33,207 × 6 = 199,242 and 33,207 × 36 = 1,195,452 comparisons, from about 33 thousand ranking sessions.

There is a catch, and it is instructive. The pairs from one ranking are not independent: each answer appears in $K - 1$ of them. The InstructGPT authors found that shuffling all pairs into one big dataset made the reward model **overfit** within a single epoch, since every answer was seen many times. Their fix was to treat all $\binom{K}{2}$ comparisons from one prompt as a *single* batch element, averaging their losses:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\binom{K}{2}} \, \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \Big[ \log \sigma\big(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l)\big) \Big] .
$$

It is also much cheaper, and this is the kind of saving an inference engineer should spot immediately. Scoring pairs one by one needs two forward passes per pair; scoring each answer once and reusing the score needs $K$.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: forward passes per ranking">
  With $K = 9$: pair by pair costs $2 \times 36 = 72$ reward-model forward passes. Scoring each answer once costs **9**, then the 36 differences are free arithmetic. That is 8× less compute for an identical loss. With $K = 4$: 12 passes versus 4, a 3× saving.
</Callout>

### The reward model itself

Architecturally, a reward model is nothing new. Take a transformer (InstructGPT started from its supervised finetuned model), remove the final layer that maps the last hidden state to 50,000-odd vocabulary logits, and put in a layer that maps it to **one number**. Feed in the prompt followed by the answer, read the hidden state at the last token, and the scalar that comes out is $r(x, y)$. The last token is the natural place to read because, thanks to the causal mask (Module 03), it is the only position that has seen the whole answer.

Size matters, and not always in the obvious direction. InstructGPT used a single **6B** reward model for all of its policies. The authors report that 175B reward models could reach lower validation loss but trained less stably, and would have made the RL stage much more expensive, since the reward model runs on every sample the policy generates.

### Rewards have no zero

Look again at the loss: it depends only on $r_w - r_l$. Add 10 to every score and every loss is unchanged. So the training process fixes the *gaps* between answers to the same prompt, and nothing else. Two consequences:

- **Normalise before use.** InstructGPT shifted its reward model's output so that the labelers' own demonstrations scored 0 on average before using it for reinforcement learning.
- **Do not compare scores across prompts.** A reward of 2.1 on one prompt and 0.3 on another does not mean the first answer is better in any absolute sense. The model was only ever trained to order answers *to the same prompt*.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Thresholding a reward">
  A tempting production idea: "only show answers with reward above 1.0". Because rewards have no anchored zero and are only trained within a prompt, that threshold means different things for different prompts, and it shifts every time the reward model is retrained. If you must filter, compare candidates for the same prompt against each other, or calibrate a threshold on held-out data for each reward-model version.
</Callout>

### Measuring a reward model

The natural metric is <Term t="pairwise-accuracy">pairwise accuracy</Term>: on held-out comparisons, how often does the model give the chosen answer the higher score? Random guessing scores 50%. The ceiling is not 100%, because people disagree with each other: if labelers agree only about 73–77% of the time, no model can predict *a given labeler's* choice much more reliably than that. A reward model at 70% on hard, real comparisons is doing well; one at 95% probably has an easy or leaky test set.

Pairwise accuracy also hides what the model has learned to like. Reward models are commonly reported to favour longer answers, confident tone and certain formatting whether or not those make an answer better. Those biases are harmless in the dataset and dangerous once something optimises against them, which is the story of the next lesson.

### The simplest use: best-of-N

You do not need any more training to get value from a reward model. Generate $N$ answers to a prompt, score each, return the highest. This is <Term t="best-of-n">best-of-N</Term> reranking. It is a real inference-time technique and you will implement it in the lab.

Its cost is easy to state: $N$ times the decode work of one answer (the $N$ samples can share one prefill of the prompt, and batch nicely, which Module 05 onward will make precise), plus $N$ reward-model forward passes. Its weakness is also easy to state: the more candidates you take the maximum over, the more you select for whatever the reward model *mistakenly* likes. Best-of-N is the gentlest form of optimising against a reward model, and the next lesson shows what happens when you push much harder.

<Callout type="real" title="Public preference data">
  You can train a reward model today on open data. Anthropic's **HH-RLHF** set (Bai et al., 2022) holds about 160,800 training and 8,552 test comparisons between two assistant replies, rated for helpfulness and harmlessness; it is what the lab uses. Many later open sets use a strong model as the judge instead of people (AI feedback), which is cheaper and brings that judge's biases along. Module B2 covers AI judges and their biases in depth.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. **Comparing is easier than creating.** People judge which of two answers is better in seconds and agree with each other roughly 73–77% of the time; free-written ideal answers essentially never agree.
  2. A preference record is $(x, y_w, y_l)$: prompt, chosen answer, rejected answer, where both answers usually come from the model itself.
  3. Ranking $K$ answers yields $\binom{K}{2}$ comparisons: 6 for K = 4, 36 for K = 9. Train them together per prompt: it avoids overfitting on correlated pairs and needs K forward passes instead of 2·C(K,2).
  4. **Bradley-Terry:** $P(A \succ B) = \sigma(r_A - r_B)$, so rewards are log-odds. $r_A = 1.2$, $r_B = 0.4$ gives σ(0.8) = 0.690, odds 2.23.
  5. **Reward-model loss:** $-\log\sigma(r_w - r_l)$, binary cross-entropy on the score gap: 0.371 nats at a gap of 0.8, 1.171 nats if the order is wrong. Its gradient moves each score by $1 - \sigma(\Delta)$.
  6. A reward model is a transformer with the vocabulary head replaced by a scalar head, read at the last token. InstructGPT used one 6B reward model trained on rankings for 33,207 prompts.
  7. Rewards are **shift-invariant**: only gaps within a prompt mean anything. Normalise before use; never threshold raw scores across prompts.
  8. Measure with **pairwise accuracy** (ceiling near human agreement), and use the model at inference with **best-of-N**, which costs N generations plus N scoring passes.
</KeyNotes>
