Everything so far has been arithmetic on paper. This lab puts the two numbers of Module 02 on your own screen: how many bytes per second this GPU can read, and how many FLOP per second it can do. You will then build a roofline out of them, and every later module will refer back to the notes you write at the end.

<Colab nb="02-gpu.ipynb" />

## What you will do

### 1. Look at what you are running on

Print the device properties and run `nvidia-smi`. Predict the multiprocessor count and memory size **before** you run it. A T4 has 40 SMs and about 16 GB; the mismatch between what you guessed and what you got tells you something worth knowing.

### 2. Prove the asynchrony bug on your own machine

Time a matrix multiply with and without `torch.cuda.synchronize()`. The unsynchronized version measures *launches*, not work, and will be off by an order of magnitude or more. Then time the same thing with **CUDA events** and see that you get the honest number without the host-side overhead.

This is the measurement you will reuse for the rest of the course, so do not skip it.

### 3. Measure bandwidth

Copy a 1 GB tensor. You are reading 1 GB and writing 1 GB, so 2 GB have moved. Divide by the time and you have bytes per second.

Then repeat at 1, 4, 16, 64, 256 MB and 1 GB. The number at small sizes will disappoint you, and understanding why is worth more than the final figure.

### 4. Measure compute

Multiply large (8,192 square) FP16 matrices and convert to TFLOP/s. Then sweep the size to find where it saturates, and compare FP32, FP16 and BF16.

Record: `my_bw` and `my_peak`.

### 5. Derive the ridge point

Divide the two numbers you just measured, being careful with units. The T4 reference is about 320 GB/s and 65 TFLOP/s, giving roughly 203 FLOP/byte. Compare yours.

### 6. Sweep m from 1 to 2048

For each m, measure a `[m × 4096] · [4096 × 4096]` multiply in FP16 and report the measured time, the floor implied by your two limits, your efficiency as a percentage, TFLOP/s, GB/s, and arithmetic intensity.

Read the table before going further. The measured time should be almost **flat** from m = 1 to m = 32. That flatness is the empirical fact underneath the whole of Module 07: the first few dozen users of a batch cost you nearly nothing, because they are all reading the same weights at the same time.

### 7. Plot your roofline

One chart, log-log: your bandwidth floor, your compute ceiling, the ridge point, and one dot per row of the sweep. This is the picture you will keep.

### 8. Launch overhead, and the memory hierarchy

Time 1, 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 tiny kernels and divide. That per-call cost is why fusion exists. Then read the same tensor at three sizes to see the L1/L2/HBM ladder in your own numbers.

## Write these down

At the end, in **My notes**, four numbers:

- `my_bw` — your measured bandwidth, GB/s
- `my_peak` — your measured FP16 compute, TFLOP/s
- `my_ridge` — the ratio
- the GB/s at m = 1 from section 6

Every memory calculation from Module 05 onward uses these. If you do nothing else in this course, measure these four things on the hardware you will actually use.

<KeyNotes>
  1. Always warm up, always synchronize or use CUDA events, always average. A timing helper you cannot trust poisons every conclusion drawn from it.
  2. Bandwidth: copy a large tensor, count **read + write** bytes, divide by the time. The number is a floor you can never beat.
  3. Compute: multiply large FP16 matrices and convert FLOPs to TFLOP/s. Small matrices never reach peak; find the size where it saturates.
  4. Ridge point = peak compute ÷ peak bandwidth, in FLOP per byte. Everything left of it is memory-bound.
  5. Sweeping m shows measured time almost flat from 1 to 32 rows. Adding users is nearly free at first, and that is a measured fact, not a theory.
  6. Kernel launch costs single-digit microseconds, so 10,000 tiny operations can be pure overhead. Fuse them.
  7. The same read at three sizes gives three different bandwidths. That spread is the memory hierarchy, visible.
</KeyNotes>
