The last lesson gave you a mechanism. This one gives you a decision procedure, because a mechanism you cannot evaluate is a mechanism you cannot deploy.

Two numbers do all the work, and it pays to keep them strictly separate — the literature is sloppy about this and confuses them constantly.

$$
\underbrace{a}_{\text{acceptance rate}} = \Pr[\text{draft agrees with target}]
\qquad\qquad
\underbrace{\rho}_{\text{draft cost ratio}} = \frac{\text{time to draft one token}}{\text{time to decode one token}}
$$

**$a$ is about text.** It is the <Term t="acceptance-rate">acceptance rate</Term>: how often a drafted token is one the target accepts. A property of your model pair and your content.

**$\rho$ is about hardware.** It is the <Term t="draft-cost-ratio">draft cost ratio</Term>: what one drafted token costs relative to one target decode step. A property of your model sizes and your GPU.

Both appear in the literature under the name "alpha". Never let a paper's alpha slide between the two meanings without checking which one it means.

## Step one: how many tokens leave a block?

Recall the mechanics exactly. The draft model proposes **all $\gamma$ tokens**, one after another. Only then does the target score them, in one parallel pass, keep the drafts up to the first rejection, and add **one token of its own**: a corrected token at the first rejection, or a bonus token if every draft survived.

Draft $i$ is kept only if it **and every draft before it** are accepted. If each draft is accepted independently with probability $a$, that happens with probability $a^i$. So the expected number of kept drafts is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{kept}] = \sum_{i=1}^{\gamma} a^{i} = a + a^2 + \dots + a^{\gamma} = \frac{a\,(1 - a^{\gamma})}{1 - a}
$$

Add the target's one token and you have the block's <Term t="tokens-per-block">tokens per block</Term>:

$$
\boxed{\ \mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per block}] = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{\gamma} a^{i} = \frac{1 - a^{\gamma+1}}{1 - a}\ }
$$

The "1" is the target's own token, and it is why a block never returns nothing. As $\gamma$ grows the sum approaches $\frac{a}{1-a}$, so tokens per block can never pass $\frac{1}{1-a}$, however long the block: a ceiling set by how predictable the text is.

## Step two: what does the block cost?

Two parts. **Verification** is one forward pass over $\gamma+1$ positions, which we established costs about one decode step:

$$
t_{\text{verify}}(\gamma) \approx t_{\text{decode}} \cdot \left(1 + O\!\left(\frac{\gamma\, P}{F/\text{BW}}\right)\right)
$$

where the $O(\cdot)$ is the FLOPs term finally becoming visible — the $P$ is parameters, $F$ the FLOP/s, and $\mathrm{BW}$ the bandwidth. **Drafting** is $\rho$ of a decode step for every drafted token, and **every one of the $\gamma$ is drafted**, kept or not:

$$
t_{\text{draft}} = \gamma \cdot \rho \cdot t_{\text{decode}}
$$

Put in decode-step units, the cost of one block is

$$
\boxed{\ \text{cost} = 1 + \gamma\,\rho\ }
$$

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Drafting does not stop at the first rejection">
  It is tempting to think a rejected draft saves you the drafts after it. It does not. The draft model cannot know which guess the target will reject until the target has run, and the target runs only after all $\gamma$ drafts exist. So the drafts after the first rejection are **paid for and thrown away**. At $a = 0.72$ and $\gamma = 4$ a block keeps 1.88 drafts on average and wastes ${}4 - 1.88 = 2.12$. That waste is exactly why long blocks stop paying.
</Callout>

## Step three: the speedup, and the punchline

The honest baseline is one plain decode step per token. Write $K = \mathbb{E}[\text{kept}]$. Then

$$
\text{speedup} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per block}]}{\text{cost}}
= \frac{1 + K}{1 + \gamma\,\rho}
$$

Now the punchline. Both the numerator and the denominator start at 1, so

$$
\text{speedup} > 1 \iff K > \gamma\,\rho \iff \frac{K}{\gamma} > \rho
$$

$K/\gamma$ is the **keep rate**: the fraction of drafted tokens that end up in the output. Each drafted token costs $\rho$ of a target step; each kept one saves a whole target step. So the rule reads exactly like the trade it describes:

$$
\boxed{\ \text{speculation pays} \iff \underbrace{\frac{a + a^2 + \dots + a^{\gamma}}{\gamma}}_{\text{keep rate}} > \rho\ }
$$

With one draft per block the keep rate is just $a$, and the rule is $a > \rho$. With longer blocks the later drafts are kept less often, so the keep rate is **below** $a$ and the break-even acceptance **rises with $\gamma$**. For $\rho = 0.125$:

| $\gamma$ | 1 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| break-even acceptance $a^\star$ | 12.5% | 20.7% | 33.6% | 43.0% | 50.1% |

<Callout type="math" title="The single most useful sentence in this module">
  You win exactly when **the share of drafted tokens you keep** beats **what a drafted token costs**: keep rate $> \rho$.

  $a > \rho$ is the special case of one draft per block. It is necessary at any block size (if even the first draft does not pay, nothing after it will), but for longer blocks it is not enough. The block size $\gamma$ therefore matters twice: it decides how many tokens you can win, and how much drafting you pay for whether you win them or not.
</Callout>

## Worked example: 8B on an H100

Take Llama-3.1-8B targeted by Llama-3.2-1B, $\gamma = 4$, on an H100.

**Acceptance.** On typical chat text, a 1B drafting an 8B agrees about **$a = 0.72$** of the time. (This is an illustration; measure your own.)

**Cost ratio.** Llama-3.2-1B has 1.24B parameters, about 2.5 GB in BF16, against the 8B's 16.06 GB: a bytes ratio of about 0.15. Kernel launches, scheduling and sampling (Module 15) cost the small model as much as the large one, so a measured ratio is higher still, often 0.2 or more. For round numbers this lesson uses **$\rho = 0.125$**, the ratio for a drafter with one eighth of the target's bytes; put your own measured $\rho$ into the same formulas (at $\rho = 0.2$ the block below gives $2.88 / 1.8 = 1.60\times$).

**Kept drafts.**
$$
K = 0.72 + 0.72^2 + 0.72^3 + 0.72^4 = 0.720 + 0.518 + 0.373 + 0.269 = 1.880
$$

**Tokens per block.**
$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens}] = 1 + 1.880 = 2.88 \qquad \left(= \frac{1 - 0.72^{5}}{0.28} = \frac{0.8065}{0.28}\right)
$$

**Cost.**
$$
\text{cost} = 1 + 4 \times 0.125 = 1.5 \text{ decode steps}
$$

**Speedup.**
$$
\frac{2.88}{1.5} = 1.92\times
$$

So roughly **1.9× faster per user**. On an H100 at batch 1 that turns about 132 tokens/s into about 254. The keep rate is ${}1.880 / 4 = 47\%$, far above $\rho = 12.5\%$ — which is why this configuration ships.

### How sensitive is that to $\gamma$?

With $a = 0.72$ and $\rho = 0.125$ fixed:

| $\gamma$ | kept drafts $K$ | tokens/block | cost (steps) | keep rate | speedup |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.72 | 1.72 | 1.125 | 72% | 1.53× |
| 2 | 1.24 | 2.24 | 1.250 | 62% | 1.79× |
| 3 | 1.61 | 2.61 | 1.375 | 54% | 1.90× |
| **4** | **1.88** | **2.88** | **1.500** | **47%** | **1.92×** |
| 6 | 2.21 | 3.21 | 1.750 | 37% | 1.84× |
| 8 | 2.39 | 3.39 | 2.000 | 30% | 1.69× |
| 16 | 2.56 | 3.56 | 3.000 | 16% | 1.19× |

Two things to read off. First, **there is a peak**: $\gamma = 4$ gives 1.92×, and $\gamma = 16$ falls to 1.19× because the token count is already near its ceiling of $\frac{1}{1-a} = 3.57$ while the drafting bill has doubled. Second, the curve is **flat near the top**: anything from 3 to 6 is within 4% of the best. That is why engines pick $\gamma$ between 3 and 6 and rarely tune further.

### And how sensitive is it to acceptance?

At $\gamma = 4$, so the cost is 1.5 steps whatever the text:

| $a$ | tokens | keep rate | speedup | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 1.43 | 11% | 0.95× | a loss: keep rate below $\rho$ |
| 0.50 | 1.94 | 23% | 1.29× | real |
| 0.60 | 2.31 | 33% | 1.54× | solid |
| **0.72** | **2.88** | **47%** | **1.92×** | **the worked example** |
| 0.85 | 3.71 | 68% | 2.47× | strong |
| 0.95 | 4.52 | 88% | 3.02× | near the ceiling |

Acceptance is the strong lever: moving $a$ from 0.5 to 0.8 multiplies the speedup by about 1.7 (1.29× to 2.24×), while no choice of $\gamma$ beats the peak by anything. **When you have one knob to turn, turn the one that controls $a$** — which means choosing a method or choosing your traffic.

The first row hides a second lesson. At $a = 0.30$ a block of 4 loses, but a block of 1 wins: tokens ${}1.30$, cost ${}1.125$, **1.16×**. When acceptance is low, draft less.

## Exactness, again — but now as arithmetic

The previous lesson promised the output is unchanged. Here is the argument in full, because "it is exact" is a claim you should be able to defend.

Let $p$ be the target's distribution and $q$ the draft's at some position. We want the emitted token to be distributed exactly as $p$. Two branches:

**Accepted** (probability $q(x)\cdot\min\!\left(1,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)$, emitting $x$): this simplifies to $\min\!\left(q(x),\, p(x)\right)$.

**Rejected** (probability $\text{reject} = 1 - \sum_y \min(q(y),\, p(y))$, emitting from the corrected distribution): the corrected distribution is
$$
p'(x) = \frac{\max(0,\ p(x) - q(x))}{\sum_y \max(0,\ p(y) - q(y))}
$$

Now note the identity that makes it all work:

$$
\sum_y \max(0,\ p(y) - q(y)) = \sum_y \big(p(y) - \min(p(y), q(y))\big) = 1 - \sum_y \min(p(y), q(y))
$$

so the rejection branch's contribution to emitting $x$ is exactly $\max(0, p(x) - q(x))$. Add to the acceptance branch:

$$
\min(q(x), p(x)) + \max(0, p(x) - q(x)) = \min(q(x),p(x)) + p(x) - \min(p(x),q(x)) = p(x)
$$

**Exactly $p(x)$, term by term.** No approximation anywhere. This is a real proof, not an empirical claim, and it is why the technique is safe to enable unconditionally.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Greedy decoding breaks this — use care with it">
  The rule above assumes you sample from $p$ and $q$ as probability distributions. If you run the target in **greedy** mode (always take the argmax), the accept test $p(x)/q(x) > u$ with a random $u$ is no longer the right comparison, and implementations that reuse the stochastic rule with $\arg\max$ silently change behaviour. Real engines handle greedy separately: accept a draft if it equals the target's argmax, and on rejection emit the target's argmax. The exactness argument still holds, but you must use the matching rule, not the sampled one.
</Callout>

## Reading $\rho$ off the hardware

$\rho$ is not a free parameter you choose — it falls out of two things you can measure separately.

**The memory term.** A decode step reads the weights. A draft model with $P_d$ parameters against a target with $P_t$ reads proportionally fewer bytes, so its memory-bound share scales as $\frac{P_d}{P_t}$. For a 1B drafter on an 8B target that ratio is one eighth.

**The overhead term.** Both models pay the *same* fixed cost per step: kernel launches, the scheduler, sampling, Python. Call it $t_{\text{ovh}}$. Module 15 measured this. So

$$
t_{\text{draft}} = \underbrace{\frac{P_d \cdot b}{\text{BW} \cdot \text{MBU}}}_{\text{weights}} + t_{\text{ovh}},
\qquad
t_{\text{target}} = \underbrace{\frac{P_t \cdot b}{\text{BW} \cdot \text{MBU}}}_{\text{weights}} + t_{\text{ovh}}
$$

The parameter ratio is only the answer if $t_{\text{ovh}} \approx 0$. It never is, and the smaller the models the more it matters — which is why on a slow card a 1B drafter can cost **half** a target step rather than an eighth of it. This is the single most commonly mis-estimated number in the whole technique, and the lab measures it.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Two quantities, one Greek letter">
  Practitioners use "$\alpha$" for the acceptance rate *and* for the parameter or cost ratio, often in the same sentence. Before you reason about anyone's $\alpha$, find out which one it is.

  A quick test: if the number is described as being **about 0.6–0.8**, it is almost certainly an acceptance rate. If it is described as **0.1–0.2**, or as "roughly the size ratio", it is a cost ratio. The two live in different decades, which is a useful sanity check.
</Callout>

## Why $\gamma$ has an optimum

Look at what one more draft buys. Going from $\gamma$ to $\gamma + 1$ adds $a^{\gamma+1}$ expected tokens (the chance the new draft survives) and $\rho$ of cost (it is drafted either way). Adding it raises the speedup exactly when

$$
\frac{T + a^{\gamma+1}}{C + \rho} > \frac{T}{C} \iff a^{\gamma+1} > \rho \cdot \frac{T}{C} = \rho \cdot \text{speedup}(\gamma)
$$

where $T$ and $C$ are the current tokens and cost. **Keep adding drafts while the next one's chance of surviving beats $\rho$ times your current speedup.** For $a = 0.72$, $\rho = 0.125$:

- $\gamma = 3 \to 4$: the fourth draft survives with ${}0.72^4 = 0.269$, against ${}0.125 \times 1.90 = 0.237$. Add it.
- $\gamma = 4 \to 5$: the fifth survives with ${}0.72^5 = 0.194$, against ${}0.125 \times 1.92 = 0.240$. Stop.

So $\gamma^\star = 4$, matching the table. A higher acceptance or a cheaper draft pushes the optimum out; a free proposer like prompt lookup ($\rho \approx 0$) can afford long blocks.

There is a second, smaller effect on the verification side. The "verification is free" approximation degrades as the block grows:

$$
\frac{t_{\text{verify}}(\gamma)}{t_{\text{decode}}(1)} \approx 1 \quad \text{while memory-bound}, \qquad \text{but } \sim \gamma \text{ once compute-bound}
$$

The transition happens where $\frac{2P\gamma}{F \cdot \text{MFU}}$ overtakes $\frac{P\cdot b + \text{KV}}{\mathrm{BW} \cdot \text{MBU}}$. For Llama-3.1-8B at batch 1 on an H100 that is a very large $\gamma$, so the drafting cost sets the optimum long before verification does. At large batch, or on a compute-poor card, verification starts to cost real time too, and the optimum moves in. The lesson after next is about when the whole trade fails.

<Callout type="math" title="The metric papers actually report">
  Papers rarely report your speedup. They report the **average acceptance length**, often written $\tau$: tokens emitted per target forward pass, which is exactly the tokens per block from Step one. It is independent of your hardware, which is why papers like it:

  $$
  \tau = \mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per block}] = \frac{1 - a^{\gamma+1}}{1 - a} \quad \text{(for a chain of } \gamma \text{ drafts)}
  $$

  A headline claim like "3.5 tokens per forward pass" *is* $\tau$. It is an **upper bound** on the speedup: the real speedup divides it by the cost of a block, ${}1 + \gamma\rho$ for a chain of drafts, plus any extra verification cost. That is why a method with an excellent $\tau$ can still lose on a slow card.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mentor" title="What to actually measure">
  Do not measure $\gamma^\star$ first. Measure the two primitives:

  1. **$a$** — how often a drafted token is accepted when the target checks it, over a few hundred real requests from your own traffic. Not synthetic prompts. Careful: engines usually log the fraction of *all drafted tokens* that were accepted, which is the **keep rate** at your $\gamma$, not $a$. Both are useful; do not mix them up.
  2. **$\rho$** — time a decode step of the target and of the draft at the same batch size and context.

  Then read the speedup off the formula. It is far more robust than sweeping $\gamma$ and picking the best-looking run, because the sweep confounds two effects and these do not.

  Note also that **$a$ is not constant across a workload.** Summarisation or code have high acceptance; open-ended creative writing has low acceptance. Measuring on the wrong traffic is the single most common way people conclude "speculation does not work".
</Callout>
