The last lesson gave you a mechanism. This one gives you a decision procedure, because a mechanism you cannot evaluate is a mechanism you cannot deploy.

Two numbers do all the work, and it pays to keep them strictly separate — the literature is sloppy about this and confuses them constantly.

$$
\underbrace{a}_{\text{acceptance rate}} = \Pr[\text{draft agrees with target}]
\qquad\qquad
\underbrace{\rho}_{\text{draft cost ratio}} = \frac{\text{time to draft one token}}{\text{time to decode one token}}
$$

**$a$ is about text.** It is the <Term t="acceptance-rate">acceptance rate</Term>: how often the draft agrees with the target. A property of your model pair and your content.

**$\rho$ is about hardware.** It is the <Term t="draft-cost-ratio">draft cost ratio</Term>: what one drafted token costs relative to one target decode step. A property of your model sizes and your GPU.

Both appear in the literature under the name "alpha". Never let a paper's alpha slide between the two meanings without checking which one it means.

## Step one: how many tokens leave a block?

In speculative decoding, the target checks all of the drafted tokens in one parallel pass and keeps them only up to the first rejection. In our foundational model, we evaluate the expected accepted drafts $\min(k+1, \gamma)$ where $k$ is the accepted run length — geometric with mean

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{drafts}] = \sum_{i=1}^{\gamma} \Pr[\text{at least } i \text{ drafts}] = \sum_{i=1}^{\gamma} a^{i-1} = \frac{1 - a^{\gamma}}{1 - a}
$$

Note the truncation. If $\gamma$ is large and $a$ is high, this tends to $\frac{1}{1-a}$ — the infinite-series sum. If $a$ is low, it is dominated by the first term. (In standard batch drafting, all $\gamma$ candidate tokens are generated before verification runs, costing $\gamma \rho$; in the foundational model where draft cost is budgeted against accepted yield, it scales with $d$).

Each accepted draft yields one token, and the final position yields the bonus token. So the block's <Term t="tokens-per-block">tokens per block</Term> is

$$
\boxed{\ \mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per block}] = 1 + a\cdot\frac{1 - a^{\gamma}}{1 - a}\ }
$$

The "1" is the bonus token, and it is why a block never returns nothing.

## Step two: what does the block cost?

Two parts. **Verification** is one forward pass over $\gamma+1$ positions, which we established costs about one decode step:

$$
t_{\text{verify}}(\gamma) \approx t_{\text{decode}} \cdot \left(1 + O\!\left(\frac{\gamma\, P}{F/\text{BW}}\right)\right)
$$

where the $O(\cdot)$ is the FLOPs term finally becoming visible — the $P$ is parameters, $F$ the FLOP/s, and $\mathrm{BW}$ the bandwidth. **Drafting** is $\rho$ of a decode step per token actually drafted:

$$
t_{\text{draft}} = \rho \cdot t_{\text{decode}} \cdot \mathbb{E}[\text{drafts}]
$$

Put in decode-step units, the cost of one block is

$$
\boxed{\ \text{cost} = 1 + \rho\cdot\frac{1 - a^{\gamma}}{1 - a}\ }
$$

## Step three: the speedup, and the punchline

The honest baseline is one plain decode step per token. So

$$
\text{speedup} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per block}]}{\text{cost}}
= \frac{1 + a\,d}{1 + \rho\,d},
\qquad d = \frac{1-a^{\gamma}}{1-a}
$$

Now the punchline. Both the numerator's extra term and the denominator's extra term are the *same* quantity $d$, multiplied by $a$ and by $\rho$. So

$$
\text{speedup} > 1 \iff 1 + a\,d > 1 + \rho\,d \iff a > \rho
$$

**$\gamma$ cancels entirely in this foundational model.** The break-even condition simplifies to whether your acceptance rate exceeds your draft cost ratio: $a > \rho$. (In practice with fixed $\gamma$ drafting, break-even is $\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^\gamma a^i > \rho$, which approaches $a > \rho$ for small $\gamma$).

<Callout type="math" title="The single most useful sentence in this module">
  You win exactly when **how often the draft is right** beats **how cheap the draft was**. In other words, when your <Term t="acceptance-rate">acceptance rate</Term> exceeds your <Term t="draft-cost-ratio">draft cost ratio</Term> — the <Term t="break-even-acceptance">break-even acceptance rate</Term> is simply $\rho$.

  Nothing else. Not the block size, not the GPU, not the context length — none of it changes that inequality. Everything else only determines how far above the line you land, and therefore how big the speedup is once you are above it.
</Callout>

## Worked example: 8B on an H100

Take Llama-3.1-8B targeted by Llama-3.2-1B, $\gamma = 4$, on an H100.

**Acceptance.** On typical chat text, a 1B drafting an 8B agrees about **$a = 0.72$** of the time. (This is an illustration; measure your own.)

**Cost ratio.** A 1B model reads about \$1.2 GB of weights where the 8B reads \$16.06 GB, so $\rho \approx 0.075$ — but a small model still pays the same per-step *overhead* (Module 15: launches, scheduling, Python), so the realistic ratio is nearer **$\rho = 0.125$**.

**Tokens per block.**
$$
d = \frac{1 - 0.72^{4}}{1 - 0.72} = \frac{1 - 0.2687}{0.28} = \frac{0.7313}{0.28} = 2.612
$$
$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens}] = 1 + 0.72 \times 2.612 = 1 + 1.880 = 2.88
$$

**Cost.**
$$
\text{cost} = 1 + 0.125 \times 2.612 = 1 + 0.327 = 1.327 \text{ decode steps}
$$

**Speedup.**
$$
\frac{2.88}{1.327} = 2.17\times
$$

So roughly **2.2× faster per user**. On an H100 at batch 1 that turns about 132 tokens/s into about 285. And because $a = 0.72$ is far above $\rho = 0.125$, you are comfortably over the line — which is why this configuration ships.

### How sensitive is that to $\gamma$?

With $a = 0.72$ and $\rho = 0.125$ fixed:

| $\gamma$ | $d$ | tokens/block | cost (steps) | speedup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.00 | 1.72 | 1.125 | 1.53× |
| 2 | 1.72 | 2.24 | 1.215 | 1.84× |
| **4** | **2.61** | **2.88** | **1.326** | **2.17×** |
| 8 | 3.31 | 3.39 | 1.414 | 2.39× |
| 16 | 3.55 | 3.56 | 1.444 | 2.46× |
| $\infty$ | 3.57 | 3.57 | 1.446 | 2.47× |

Two things to read off. First, **the curve flattens hard**: going from $\gamma = 4$ to $\gamma = 16$ quadruples the block size and buys only about 1.13× more, because the token count has essentially reached its ceiling of $\frac{1}{1-a} = 3.57$. Second, the cost is almost flat across that whole range — 1.125 to 1.444 decode steps — which is exactly the "verification is nearly free" property doing its job.

So $\gamma$ has a sharply diminishing return after about 4. In practice engines pick $\gamma$ between 3 and 6 and rarely tune further, because past that the gains are inside the noise of everything else.

### And how sensitive is it to acceptance?

| $a$ | $d$ ($\gamma$=4) | tokens | cost | speedup | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 1.42 | 1.43 | 1.18 | 1.21× | thin |
| 0.50 | 1.88 | 1.94 | 1.23 | 1.57× | real |
| 0.60 | 2.18 | 2.31 | 1.27 | 1.81× | solid |
| **0.72** | **2.61** | **2.88** | **1.33** | **2.17×** | **the worked example** |
| 0.85 | 3.19 | 3.71 | 1.40 | 2.65× | strong |
| 0.95 | 3.71 | 4.52 | 1.46 | 3.09× | near the ceiling |

Acceptance is the strong lever: moving $a$ from 0.5 to 0.8 multiplies the speedup by 1.6, while moving $\gamma$ from 4 to 16 bought only 1.13. **When you have one knob to turn, turn the one that controls $a$** — which means choosing a method or choosing your traffic, not tuning $\gamma$.

## Exactness, again — but now as arithmetic

The previous lesson promised the output is unchanged. Here is the argument in full, because "it is exact" is a claim you should be able to defend.

Let $p$ be the target's distribution and $q$ the draft's at some position. We want the emitted token to be distributed exactly as $p$. Two branches:

**Accepted** (probability $q(x)\cdot\min\!\left(1,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)$, emitting $x$): this simplifies to $\min\!\left(q(x),\, p(x)\right)$.

**Rejected** (probability $\text{reject} = 1 - \sum_y \min(q(y),\, p(y))$, emitting from the corrected distribution): the corrected distribution is
$$
p'(x) = \frac{\max(0,\ p(x) - q(x))}{\sum_y \max(0,\ p(y) - q(y))}
$$

Now note the identity that makes it all work:

$$
\sum_y \max(0,\ p(y) - q(y)) = \sum_y \big(p(y) - \min(p(y), q(y))\big) = 1 - \sum_y \min(p(y), q(y))
$$

so the rejection branch's contribution to emitting $x$ is exactly $\max(0, p(x) - q(x))$. Add to the acceptance branch:

$$
\min(q(x), p(x)) + \max(0, p(x) - q(x)) = \min(q(x),p(x)) + p(x) - \min(p(x),q(x)) = p(x)
$$

**Exactly $p(x)$, term by term.** No approximation anywhere. This is a real proof, not an empirical claim, and it is why the technique is safe to enable unconditionally.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Greedy decoding breaks this — use care with it">
  The rule above assumes you sample from $p$ and $q$ as probability distributions. If you run the target in **greedy** mode (always take the argmax), the accept test $p(x)/q(x) > u$ with a random $u$ is no longer the right comparison, and implementations that reuse the stochastic rule with $\arg\max$ silently change behaviour. Real engines handle greedy separately: accept a draft if it equals the target's argmax, and on rejection emit the target's argmax. The exactness argument still holds, but you must use the matching rule, not the sampled one.
</Callout>

## Reading $\rho$ off the hardware

$\rho$ is not a free parameter you choose — it falls out of two things you can measure separately.

**The memory term.** A decode step reads the weights. A draft model with $P_d$ parameters against a target with $P_t$ reads proportionally fewer bytes, so its memory-bound share scales as $\frac{P_d}{P_t}$. For a 1B drafter on an 8B target that ratio is one eighth.

**The overhead term.** Both models pay the *same* fixed cost per step: kernel launches, the scheduler, sampling, Python. Call it $t_{\text{ovh}}$. Module 15 measured this. So

$$
t_{\text{draft}} = \underbrace{\frac{P_d \cdot b}{\text{BW} \cdot \text{MBU}}}_{\text{weights}} + t_{\text{ovh}},
\qquad
t_{\text{target}} = \underbrace{\frac{P_t \cdot b}{\text{BW} \cdot \text{MBU}}}_{\text{weights}} + t_{\text{ovh}}
$$

The parameter ratio is only the answer if $t_{\text{ovh}} \approx 0$. It never is, and the smaller the models the more it matters — which is why on a slow card a 1B drafter can cost **half** a target step rather than an eighth of it. This is the single most commonly mis-estimated number in the whole technique, and the lab measures it.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Two quantities, one Greek letter">
  Practitioners use "$\alpha$" for the acceptance rate *and* for the parameter or cost ratio, often in the same sentence. Before you reason about anyone's $\alpha$, find out which one it is.

  A quick test: if the number is described as being **about 0.6–0.8**, it is almost certainly an acceptance rate. If it is described as **0.1–0.2**, or as "roughly the size ratio", it is a cost ratio. The two live in different decades, which is a useful sanity check.
</Callout>

## Why $\gamma$ has an optimum

We showed tokens-per-block rises with $\gamma$ and saturates. But cost-per-block also rises, since $\mathbb{E}[\text{drafts}]$ grows toward $\frac{1}{1-a}$. And separately, the "verification is free" approximation degrades:

$$
\frac{t_{\text{verify}}(\gamma)}{t_{\text{decode}}(1)} \approx 1 \quad \text{while memory-bound}, \qquad \text{but } \sim \gamma \text{ once compute-bound}
$$

The transition happens where $\frac{2P\gamma}{F \cdot \text{MFU}}$ overtakes $\frac{P\cdot b + \text{KV}}{\mathrm{BW} \cdot \text{MBU}}$. For Llama-3.1-8B on an H100 that is a very large $\gamma$, which is why the "verification is free" approximation is so good in practice — and why on a memory-poor card like the T4, or with a much smaller model, the optimum $\gamma$ is smaller.

$$
\gamma^\star \approx \min\!\left(\ \frac{a}{1-a},\ \text{where compute catches memory}\ \right)
$$

There is always a sweet spot, and it is hardware-dependent. The next lesson is about who plays the roles of $a$ and $\rho$; the lesson after that is about when the whole trade fails.

<Callout type="math" title="The metric engines actually report">
  Papers do not usually report speedup. They report **forward passes per delivered token**, because it is sampling- and hardware-independent:

  $$
  \text{rounds} = \frac{\gamma + 1}{\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per block}]} = \frac{\gamma+1}{1 + a\,d}
  $$

  A headline claim like "2.9 tokens per forward pass" *is* this quantity. Convert it with the same formula you already have. And remember it is an upper bound on the speedup: the real speedup also divides by the draft model's cost, which is why a method with excellent rounds can still lose on a slow card.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mentor" title="What to actually measure">
  Do not measure $\gamma^\star$ first. Measure the two primitives:

  1. **$a$** — the fraction of drafted tokens the target accepts, over a few hundred real requests from your own traffic. Not synthetic prompts.
  2. **$\rho$** — time a decode step of the target and of the draft at the same batch size and context.

  Then read the speedup off the formula. It is far more robust than sweeping $\gamma$ and picking the best-looking run, because the sweep confounds two effects and these do not.

  Note also that **$a$ is not constant across a workload.** Summarisation or code have high acceptance; open-ended creative writing has low acceptance. Measuring on the wrong traffic is the single most common way people conclude "speculation does not work".
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. Two numbers decide everything: $a$, the acceptance rate (a property of your text and model pair) and $\rho$, the draft cost ratio (a property of your hardware and model sizes). Both are called "alpha" in the literature; keep them distinct.
  2. Drafting stops at the first rejection, so the expected number of drafted tokens is $d = \frac{1-a^{\gamma}}{1-a}$, and tokens per block is $a\,d + 1$.
  3. Cost per block, in decode-step units, is $\rho\,d + 1$ — one verify pass plus the sequential draft cost.
  4. Speedup is $(1 + a\,d)/(1 + \rho\,d)$, which exceeds 1 **exactly when $a > \rho$**. The block size $\gamma$ cancels from the break-even condition.
  5. For Llama-3.1-8B with a 1B draft at $a = 0.72$, $\rho = 0.125$, $\gamma = 4$: 2.88 tokens per block for 1.327 steps of cost, about **2.17×**.
  6. Exactness is provable, not empirical — the accepted branch contributes $\min(p,q)$ and the rejection branch $\max(0, p-q)$, which sum to $p$ term by term.
  7. Under greedy decoding the stochastic accept rule must be replaced with an argmax comparison, or the output changes.
  8. $\gamma$ has an optimum because the verify pass does $\gamma$ times the FLOPs; past the knee it stops being free, and the knee is further away on a bandwidth-rich GPU.
</KeyNotes>
