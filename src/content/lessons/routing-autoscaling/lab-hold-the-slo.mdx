## Part 1 · On the real GPU: measure the constants

Everything above is arithmetic on a model. This part confirms the constants with your own measurements, which is what makes the rest of the lab real.

Use **Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct** (0.49B parameters) so it fits a free T4 with room to spare. The absolute times are far smaller than an 8B model on an H100, but the ratios and the shape of the curve are the same — both scale linearly in parameter count.

The notebook measures, in order:

1. **Prefill slope** at 256–2,048 tokens. Prefill time is linear in prompt length; the slope `t_prefill_per_token` is what you need.
2. **Decode step** for batches of 1–128. The step is nearly flat in batch size while total throughput climbs — memory-bound behaviour, straight from Module 05.
3. **The capacity formula** from Lesson 23.1: users per replica = KV pool ÷ context, and requests per second = users ÷ time in system. This is the capacity per replica — check it against what your GPU can actually do.
4. **The cliff**, by modelling the queue that forms when the offered rate exceeds capacity. The dashed sustainable rates here are 6 req/s for one replica, 22 for four, and 30 for eight.
5. **Routing policies**, comparing KV spread across a mixed short/long workload.
6. **What a prefix hit rate is worth**, in both prefill milliseconds and resident memory.
7. **A capacity table** for real GPUs (T4, A100, H100) and real models (0.5B, 8B, 70B).

Record `t_prefill_per_token`, `t_decode_step` and `capacity_per_replica` in your notes.

## Part 2 · Compute the cliff by hand

Before you run the simulator, predict it. For a fleet of $N$ replicas at mean context $c$:

```
users_per_replica   = KV_pool_tokens / c
capacity_per_replica = users_per_replica / (out_tokens × TPOT + TTFT_budget)
capacity_fleet      = N × capacity_per_replica
queue_wait          = max(0, offered_rate − capacity_fleet) × time_per_request
TTFT                = queue_wait + one decode step
```

Compute this for four replicas at 20, 26 and 34 req/s, and write the three TTFT values down. **Predicting the shape and then explaining the gap between your prediction and the simulator is the skill.** They will differ, and the difference is the interesting part.

  2. You add one replica, raising capacity to 125 req/s. Does that fix it?

## Part 4 · The deliverable

Write a one-page **capacity plan** for the service described above. It must contain:

- the **routing policy** you would deploy, and one sentence on why for this workload;
- the **replica count** you would run at peak, with one spare, and the arithmetic;
- the **maximum sustainable arrival rate** at that replica count, from the simulator;
- what changes if the long-prompt share rises to 60%;
- the **metric you alert on first**, and the threshold;
- your estimate of **cost per million output tokens**, from the notebook's capacity table.

<Callout type="mentor" title="What a strong plan concludes">
  The interesting sentence is not the replica count — anyone can divide. It is the *rate* and the *alert*. The engineer's job is to say: at this replica count we serve N requests per second sustainably, we find out first from [metric], and we trip at [threshold]. That is the sentence that gets paged at 3am — or, more often, does not.
</Callout>

## Part 5 · Take it to a real engine

If you have a GPU with more than 16 GB, run two vLLM engines locally and put a real router in front:

```bash
# Two engines, one per port, sharing a host
vllm serve Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct --port 8001 --gpu-memory-utilization 0.45 &
vllm serve Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct --port 8002 --gpu-memory-utilization 0.45 &
```

Then drive both with a small load generator at a controlled rate and watch `vllm:num_requests_waiting` and `vllm:gpu_cache_usage_perc` per engine — those are the signals your router would score on. Compare the spread you observe against the simulator's. Expect the same shape, different absolute numbers, because a real engine adds scheduling fairness and launch overhead the simulator omits. **Predicting the shape and then explaining the gap is the skill.**

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Watch for the hot replica, not the average">
  A fleet with one saturated engine and three idle ones looks fine on the average. That is the affinity failure from Lesson 23.2, and it will not show up in a fleet-wide mean. Watch per-replica `num_requests_waiting` and `gpu_cache_usage_perc`, and alert on the worst replica, not the average — Module 06's percentile lesson, applied one level up.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. Capacity per replica = (KV pool ÷ context) ÷ time in system. Both terms are measurable before you deploy.
  2. The goodput cliff is sharp: past saturation extra arrivals do not add throughput, they only add queue wait.
  3. Deploy below the sustainable rate, not at it. A fleet sized exactly to its capacity sits on the cliff.
  4. Context length, not request rate, sets capacity — so re-measure whenever the prompt mix changes.
  5. Least-KV equalises the resource that actually runs out; round robin deals in counts and cannot see a 10× size difference.
  6. Affinity buys a hit rate and pays in balance; cache-aware routing buys most of the reuse with less of the imbalance.
  7. A burst drains if it is short relative to the drain time; sustained overload is the case your plan must survive.
  8. Alert on the worst replica, never the fleet average — a single hot replica is invisible in a mean.
</KeyNotes>
