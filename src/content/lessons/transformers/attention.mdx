Here is the equation that made transformers work, and the equation every serving engine spends most of its attention modules executing:

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}} + M\right) V
$$

That is the entire mechanism. It has no learned parameters of its own beyond the three projections that produce $Q$, $K$ and $V$, and it can be understood completely in about ten minutes of arithmetic. This lesson does that arithmetic, token by token.

## The idea in one sentence

**Each token asks a question, every token answers with a description of itself, and every token offers a contribution.** The attention step works out how well each answer matches the question, turns those matches into shares that sum to 1, and returns the share-weighted average of the contributions.

That is a **soft lookup**. A hard lookup would be: find the one entry whose key matches and return its value. Attention does the same thing but returns a blend of every entry, weighted by similarity — and crucially, the query, key and value for one token all live in the *same* place, so a token's contribution to every later token is available without any index.

## Step 1: three projections

Take a token's hidden vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{4096}$ and multiply it by three learned matrices:

$$
\mathbf{q} = W_Q \mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{k} = W_K \mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = W_V \mathbf{x}
$$

| | Llama-3.1-8B shape | Parameters | Bytes in BF16 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $W_Q$ | 4096 × 4096 | 16.78 M | 33.6 MB |
| $W_K$ | **4096 × 1024** | 4.19 M | 8.4 MB |
| $W_V$ | **4096 × 1024** | 4.19 M | 8.4 MB |
| $W_O$ (output) | 4096 × 4096 | 16.78 M | 33.6 MB |

$K$ and $V$ are narrower because Llama-3.1-8B has 8 key/value heads against 32 query heads — the subject of the next lesson. For this lesson, treat each head as a vector of $d_{\text{head}} = 128$ numbers, and use 4 numbers so you can see everything.

## Step 2: score, by dot product

For the query token, dot its query with **every** key, including its own:

$$
\text{score}_j = \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k}_j
$$

Using the visual's numbers, with query $\mathbf{q} = [0.9,\ 0.2,\ -0.2,\ 1.3]$:

$$
[2.14,\ 0.40,\ -1.12,\ 2.59]
$$

One dot product per earlier token. All of them at once is a single matrix multiply, $QK^\top$, and that is why attention is fast on a GPU.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why a dot product is the right question">
  You used this in Module 01: $q \cdot k = |q|\,|k|\cos\theta$. If the query and a key point the same way the score is large; if they are unrelated it is near zero. Learned keys are free to arrange themselves so that "points the same way" means "is relevant to". Nobody hand-designed this; it is what a dot product already measures.
</Callout>

## Step 3: divide by √d, or the softmax breaks

Here is a real failure, not a scare story. A dot product over $d$ numbers grows roughly like $\sqrt{d}$ in typical magnitude. At $d_{\text{head}} = 128$, scores routinely land in the range ±20. A softmax over scores of ±20 is arithmetically fine and *practically* dead:

$$
e^{20} \approx 4.9 \times 10^8, \qquad e^{-20} \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-9}
$$

so one key takes 100.000% of the attention and every other key gets a share so small that its gradient underflows to zero in BF16. The mechanism cannot learn — it can only pick the current best match.

Dividing by $\sqrt{d}$ fixes it. It is a **fixed temperature**:

$$
\sqrt{4} = 2, \qquad \sqrt{128} = 11.31
$$

**Measure it yourself in the visual.** Drag the divisor and watch the entropy readout. At ÷1 the shares are 35.9%, 6.3%, 1.4%, 56.4% — nearly one-hot, entropy 1.33 bits. At ÷2 they are 34.9%, 14.6%, 6.8%, 43.7%, entropy 1.72 bits. At ÷11.3, the real 128-wide value, the distribution flattens toward uniform: entropy 1.98 of a possible 2.00. Sharp enough to be selective, soft enough to learn from.

## Step 4: the causal mask

An <Term t="autoregressive">autoregressive</Term> model generates left to right. Token 5 cannot look at token 6, because when token 6 was generated nobody knew what it would be. So before the softmax you add a mask:

$$
M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & j \le i \\ -\infty & j > i \end{cases}
$$

Minus infinity, not zero. If you added zero the masked positions would get score 0, become $e^0 = 1$, and steal attention. With $-\infty$, $e^{-\infty} = 0$ exactly, and after normalising the remaining shares are correct for the shortened prefix.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Half the matrix is thrown away">
  Under a causal mask only the lower triangle of $QK^\top$ is ever computed. A full $T \times T$ score matrix for 8,192 tokens is 67 million numbers; a causal kernel computes about half. Every efficient attention kernel in production exploits this, and it is why attention FLOPs are usually quoted with that factor of two already applied.
</Callout>

## Step 5: softmax, and Step 6: mix

Softmax turns the scaled, masked scores into shares that sum to 1. Then take the weighted average of the value vectors:

$$
\mathrm{output} = \sum_j p_j \mathbf{v}_j
$$

With shares $[0.349,\ 0.146,\ 0.068,\ 0.437]$ and values

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1.0 & -1.0 & 0.5 & 0.0 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 & 1.0 & 0.5 \\ -1.0 & 0.0 & 0.5 & 1.0 \\ 0.0 & 0.5 & -0.5 & 1.0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

you get

$$
[0.354,\ -0.204,\ 0.136,\ 0.578]
$$

which is the head's output: four numbers that get concatenated with the other 31 heads and written back into the residual stream.

## What attention costs

This is where it becomes an inference problem. Count the work per layer:

- **$QK^\top$**: $F_{QK} = 2\,T^2 d_{\text{model}}$ FLOPs for a full $T \times T$ matrix (about half that under the causal mask)
- **$PV$**: the same again
- **Weights**: $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O$ = 41.94 M parameters = **83.9 MB in BF16**, read once per layer per decode step

So per layer, attention is $F_{\text{decode}} = 4\,T\,d_{\text{model}}$ FLOPs for a single query token against a context of $T$, and $F_{\text{prefill}} = 2\,T^2 d_{\text{model}}$ FLOPs to process a whole prompt of $T$ tokens at once. For Llama-3.1-8B ($d_{\text{model}} = 4096$, $L = 32$):

| Phase | Tokens | Attention FLOPs | Weight bytes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decode, 1 token | $T = 1{,}000$ | 5.2 G | 83.9 MB/layer |
| Decode, 1 token | $T = 8{,}192$ | **43.0 G** | 83.9 MB/layer |
| Prefill, $T = 8{,}192$ | | **5.5 T** | 2.68 GB |
| Prefill, $T = 32{,}768$ | | **88.0 T** | 2.68 GB |

Two things fall out of that table.

**The weight reads are constant.** 83.9 MB per layer, whether you are processing one token or eight thousand. Attention is a <Term t="memory-bound">memory-bound</Term> operation during decode for exactly the reason a GEMM is.

**The compute grows quadratically in context.** At 8,192 tokens a prefill spends 5.5 TFLOP inside attention; at 32,768 it spends 88 TFLOP. Set it against the linear layers' $F_{\text{lin}} = 2\,T\,P$ and you get the crossover:

$$
2 T^2 d L = 2 T P_{\text{layer}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \frac{P_{\text{layer}}}{d \cdot L} = \frac{6.979\times 10^9}{4096 \times 32} \approx 53{,}000 \text{ tokens}
$$

**Below about 53,000 tokens of context, attention costs less FLOPs than the weights do. Above it, attention dominates.** Llama-3.1-8B's advertised context is 128,000 — comfortably past the point where attention is the expensive part. This single derived number explains why long context is hard, and it is the hinge of Module 20.

<Callout type="math" title="The attention KV cache, previewed">
  For every earlier token you must keep its key and its value. For Llama-3.1-8B that is, per token:

  $$
  2 \times 32 \text{ layers} \times 8 \text{ KV heads} \times 128 \times 2 \text{ bytes} = 131{,}072 \text{ bytes}
  $$

  **128 KiB per token, per sequence.** Attention is the only reason you have to remember anything at all, and that memory is the <Term t="kv-cache">KV cache</Term>. Module 05 exists to make it cheap.
</Callout>

## In code

The whole thing, twenty lines, no libraries:

```python
import torch, math

def attention(q, k, v, causal=True):
    """q: [T, d]  k, v: [C, d]  ->  [T, d]"""
    d = q.shape[-1]
    scores = (q @ k.T) / math.sqrt(d)                 # 1. score, scaled
    if causal:
        T, C = scores.shape
        scores = scores.masked_fill(torch.triu(torch.ones(T, C, bool), 1), float("-inf"))   # 2. mask
    p = scores.softmax(dim=-1)                        # 3. shares summing to 1
    return p @ v                                      # 4. weighted average
```

PyTorch's fused `scaled_dot_product_attention` does the same thing, and at the time of writing also picks a memory-efficient backend for long sequences — Module 14 is entirely about what those backends do and when you should force one.

Getting all three true on a real model is the moment attention stops being a diagram.
</Callout>

<Callout type="real" title="Where inference engineers actually spend their time on attention">
  Not on the equation. The equation is fixed by the model file. They spend it on three things: **which kernel** runs it (FlashAttention avoids materialising the $T \times T$ matrix at all — Module 14), **where the KV cache lives** (paged, so long sequences do not fragment — Module 08), and **how the mask is applied** (by subtracting a large number, or with $-\infty$, and whether the masked half is skipped). All three are memory problems. The maths is never the problem.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. Attention is $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d} + M)V$: **project, score, scale, mask, softmax, mix**. Six steps, no mystery.
  2. $Q$, $K$, $V$ are three learned projections of the same hidden vector. RoPE rotates $Q$ and $K$ but never $V$.
  3. The score is a dot product, so it is large when a query and a key point the same way. In Llama-3.1-8B, $W_Q$ and $W_O$ are 4096 × 4096 but $W_K$ and $W_V$ are 4096 × **1024** — 8 KV heads, not 32.
  4. **Divide scores by $\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}$**: 11.31 at d = 128. Without it the softmax goes one-hot, other keys' gradients underflow in BF16, and the mechanism cannot learn.
  5. The **causal mask adds $-\infty$**, not 0, before the softmax, so future keys get exactly zero share. Zero would give them $e^0 = 1$ and steal attention.
  6. Attention costs $F = 4\,T\,d_{\text{model}}$ FLOPs per layer per decode step and $F_{\text{pre}} = 2\,T^2 d_{\text{model}}$ per layer for a whole prompt. Its weight reads are constant: 83.9 MB per layer.
  7. Attention overtakes the weights at $T = P_{\text{layer}}/(d \cdot L) \approx 53{,}000$ tokens for Llama-3.1-8B. Below that, long context is a memory problem; above it, a compute problem.
  8. Keeping K and V for every earlier token costs $B_{KV} = 2\,L\,n_{kv}\,d_h \times 2$ bytes per token — **128 KiB/token** for Llama-3.1-8B. That is the KV cache.
</KeyNotes>
