The forward pass is finished. What comes out of the output head is **128,256 numbers** — one score for every token in the vocabulary — and the job of this lesson is to turn them into exactly one token. It is the smallest step in the whole pipeline and the only one where a user has a dial.

It is also the step everyone hand-waves. "Set temperature to 0.7" is not an explanation. In twenty minutes you will be able to explain it, compute it, and predict what a change to any of the five knobs does to the output.

## Stage 1: scores, not probabilities

A **logit** is a raw score. It can be 7.2 or −3.6 and neither is a probability. Two things are true about them:

- **Only differences matter.** Softmax is shift-invariant: adding 5 to every logit leaves the output identical. There is no absolute meaning to a logit, only a ranking and a spacing.
- **They are unbounded.** Nothing stops a logit being 40. (And if one is, $e^{40}$ overflows BF16 — which is why you subtract the max before exponentiating, exactly as in Module 01.)

For the visual's example — the model has just seen *"The capital of France is"* — here are the top of the distribution, plus eight more so the filtering has something to remove:

| Token | Logit |

| ` the` | 3.1 |
| ` a` | 2.4 |
| ` known` | 1.9 |
| ` also` | 1.1 |

## Stage 2: softmax

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}
$$

$$
e^{7.2} = 1339.4,\ e^{3.1} = 22.2,\ e^{2.4} = 11.0,\ e^{1.9} = 6.7,\ e^{1.1} = 3.0, \ldots \quad \sum = 1385.3
$$

| Token | Logit | $e^{z}$ | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Paris` | 7.2 | 1,339.4 | **96.67%** |
| ` the` | 3.1 | 22.2 | 1.60% |
| ` a` | 2.4 | 11.0 | 0.80% |
| ` known` | 1.9 | 6.7 | 0.48% |
| ` also` | 1.1 | 3.0 | 0.22% |
| the other 128,251 tokens | ≤ 0.2 | ≤ 1.22 | **0.23% total** |

The model is not *unsure* here. It is 96.7% confident, and that is what a well-trained model looks like on a factual question.

<Callout type="math" title="What that costs, and why it does not matter">
  Softmax over 128,256 logits is about $F = 2 \times 128{,}256 = 0.26$ MFLOP, plus reading the 256 KB the output head just wrote. On an H100 that is a couple of microseconds — **0.03% of a 5.8 ms decode step**. Sampling is free.

  It matters for correctness, not for speed. Getting it wrong produces bad text, not slow text.
</Callout>

## Stage 3: temperature

Divide the logits by $T$ before the softmax:

$$
p_i(T) = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}}
$$

This is the *same operation* as the $d^{-1/2}$ divisor in attention (03.5) and for the same reason: it controls how peaked the distribution is. Compute it for the four temperatures that matter:

| $T$ | `Paris` | ` the` | Entropy (bits) | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | **99.96%** | 0.03% | 0.005 | effectively greedy |
| 1.0 | 96.67% | 1.60% | 0.29 | what the model actually believes |
| 1.5 | 84.5% | 5.49% | 1.03 | noticeably more exploratory |
| 2.0 | 68.6% | 8.83% | 1.85 | a different token is now plausible |

<Callout type="intuition" title="Read the entropy column, not the temperature">
  Temperature itself is meaningless out of context — 0.7 is not "70% creative". The number that means something is the **entropy of the resulting distribution**, in bits. 0 bits means the model has no choice left; $\log_2 V$ bits means it has no preference at all.

  Llama-3.1's default of 0.6 on this prompt gives 0.29 bits. That is not "60% deterministic" — it is a specific, computable statement about how much room the sampler has.
</Callout>

## Stage 4: filtering

Even at $T = 1$, 0.23% of the mass sits on 128,251 tokens. Over a 500-token answer that tail gets sampled ~1.2 times, and the tail is where garbage lives. So engines filter before they sample.

### Top-k

Keep the $k$ most probable tokens, zero the rest. $k = 1$ is greedy decoding; $k = V$ is no filtering at all. Qwen2.5 ships `top_k = 20`.

At $T = 1$ on our distribution, top-3 keeps 99.07% of the mass and top-5 keeps 99.77%.

### Top-p (nucleus sampling)

Sort descending, walk until the cumulative probability reaches $p$, keep everything up to that point. The set size **adapts to the distribution**:

| | $T = 1$ (peaked) | $T = 2$ (flat) |
|---|---|---|
| top-k 3 keeps | 3 tokens, 99.1% | 3 tokens, 81.5% |
| top-p 0.90 keeps | **1 token**, 96.7% | **5 tokens**, 91.7% |
| top-p 0.95 keeps | 2 tokens, 98.3% | **7 tokens**, 95.3% |

Read that table twice. On the confident question top-p keeps *fewer* tokens than top-k — the model is sure, so let it be sure. On the flat question it keeps *more* — the model is unsure, so let it explore. One parameter, both behaviours.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Do not set both aggressively and then wonder why it is repetitive">
  Llama-3.1 recommends **either** top_p **or** top_k, not both. Setting `top_p = 0.9` and `top_k = 5` together leaves you with the intersection of the two rules, which is usually just top-k — and you have thrown away the adaptivity that made top-p worth having. Pick one and tune it.
</Callout>

### Min-p

A newer, cheaper alternative: keep every token whose probability is at least $\varepsilon \times p_{\max}$. At $\varepsilon = 0.05$:

| | $T = 1$ | $T = 2$ |
|---|---|---|
| tokens kept | **1** | **4** |

It needs one maximum and one threshold — **no sort at all**. Top-p needs a full descending sort of 128,256 values on every generated token. Over a 500-token answer that is 500 sorts where none were necessary, which is why min-p is gaining ground in serving engines.

  <Step title="1. 128,256 raw scores">
    The output head gives one score per vocabulary token. Any real number, negatives included. Only differences matter: adding 5 to every logit changes nothing, because softmax is shift-invariant.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. Softmax: scores become probabilities">
    Exponentiate, subtract the max for safety, divide by the total. **`Paris` takes 96.67%** and the other 128,251 tokens share 0.23% between them. Subtract the max first or $e^{40}$ will overflow FP16 — the same trick as Module 01.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Temperature: a knob on the softmax">
    **T < 1 sharpens, T > 1 flattens** — the identical operation as attention's $d^{-1/2}$ divisor. At $T = 0.5$ the top token holds 99.96% and the entropy is 0.005 bits: no choice left at all.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Greedy is a special case">
    Greedy takes the argmax every step. Deterministic and reproducible, but it loops and writes bland text. A seed makes sampling reproducible too — that is the entire purpose of a seed parameter.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Top-k: only the k best">
    Zero everything below rank $k$. A fixed $k$ is a poor fit: too loose on an easy question, too tight on a hard one. Cost is a partial selection over 128,256 values.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Top-p: only the confident mass">
    **Nucleus sampling.** Sort descending, walk until the cumulative probability reaches $p$. Raise the temperature and watch it keep *more* tokens than top-k did. Llama-3.1 ships 0.9.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. Renormalise and draw">
    Zero the filtered logits, softmax again so the survivors sum to 1, then walk the cumulative sum with a uniform random $u$ in $[0, 1)$. One pass, no sort, no extra kernel. Press **Draw again**.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. What an engine actually receives">
    `temperature`, `top_p`, `top_k`, `min_p`, `repetition_penalty`, `seed` — request parameters, not model constants. At the time of writing Llama-3.1 defaults to **0.6 / 0.9** and Qwen2.5 to **0.7 / 0.8 / 20**.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Stage 5: the draw

The actual algorithm is one line of pseudocode, and it is worth knowing because it explains why sampling is so cheap:

```python
# inverse-transform sampling: one pass, no sort
p = softmax(logits / temperature)
p[p < top_k_threshold] = 0            # top-k and min-p: threshold
p = top_p_filter(p, p_threshold)      # top-p: needs a descending sort
p /= p.sum()                          # renormalise to 1
u = torch.rand(())                    # one uniform number
token = 0
acc = 0.0
while acc + p[token] <= u:            # walk the cumulative sum
    acc += p[token]
    token += 1
```

There is an elegant alternative worth knowing because **speculative decoding** (Module 16) depends on it. The **Gumbel-max trick** says:

$$
\arg\max_i \left( z_i + g_i \right) \;\sim\; \text{sample from } \mathrm{softmax}(z)
$$

where $g_i$ are independent Gumbel samples, $g_i = -\log(-\log u_i)$. Add Gumbel noise to the logits, take the argmax, and you have sampled exactly from the distribution — no softmax, no cumulative sum, one argmax. It is what makes it possible to verify a draft model's token with a single comparison.

**And one more knob you will see in every API: `repetition_penalty`.** It divides the logits of tokens already present in the context by a factor (typically 1.05–1.15) before the softmax. It is a blunt instrument — it treats a repeated number and a repeated name identically — and it distorts the distribution into something that is no longer any model's actual beliefs. Use it only if you must, and prefer temperature and top-p first.

## Choosing, in order

The practical advice, in the order you should actually try things:

1. **Greedy (T = 0)** for extraction, classification, code completion and anything parsed. Deterministic beats creative.
2. **Temperature alone**, around 0.6, if you want the model's own distribution and nothing else.
3. **Add top-p 0.9** if you see the tail leaking in — usually on factual or code tasks where a weird token is catastrophic.
4. **Add a repetition penalty** last, and as small as you can get away with.
5. **Use min-p instead of top-p** if you are running your own engine and care about the sort cost.

| Task | Temperature | top_p | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Code / JSON / SQL | 0 or 0.2 | 0.9–1.0 | one wrong token ruins the answer |
| Factual QA, tool calls | 0.3–0.6 | 0.9 | the model is usually confident; let it be |
| Chat, assistants | 0.6–0.8 | 0.9 | the shipped defaults |
| Creative writing | 0.9–1.2 | 0.95 | genuinely want to explore |
| Batch generation for training data | 1.0 | 1.0 | you want the model, unmodified |

<Callout type="real" title="Where this bites an inference engineer">
  - **Batching changes results.** With batched sampling on a GPU, a row that finishes early keeps generating unless you freeze it. Different batch compositions therefore produce different text for the same seed. If you need bit-identical output you need greedy decoding, a fixed batch composition, or per-request RNG — a real operational constraint, not a detail.
  - **`seed` is not free.** Per-request RNG states cost memory and a little time, which is why engines have historically used one shared generator per step.
  - **Structured output** replaces sampling with a mask and an argmax over the legal tokens (Module 26). That is both faster and exactly correct, and it is the right answer for JSON schemas and grammar-constrained generation.
  - **The logit filter is a common fast path.** If `top_k = 1`, you can skip the softmax entirely and just argmax the output head — one fewer kernel on a 128,256-wide tensor.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. The output head produces **128,256 logits** — unbounded scores, meaningful only in their differences, since softmax is shift-invariant.
  2. At softmax, a well-trained model on a factual prompt is usually **~97% confident** on one token. That is not overconfidence; it is what the distribution is.
  3. **Temperature** divides logits before softmax. $T = 0.5$ → 99.96% top token, 0.005 bits of entropy. $T = 2$ → 68.6%, 1.85 bits. It is the same knob as attention's $d^{-1/2}$ divisor.
  4. **Entropy in bits** is the number that means something, not the temperature. 0 = no choice; $\log_2 V$ = no preference.
  5. **Top-k** keeps a fixed count; **top-p** keeps the smallest set reaching a probability mass. On a peaked distribution top-p keeps fewer than top-k; on a flat one it keeps more. That adaptivity is the point.
  6. Use **top_p or top_k, not both aggressively** — the intersection is usually just top-k, throwing away top-p's adaptivity.
  7. **Min-p** keeps tokens with $p \ge \varepsilon p_{\max}$: one threshold, **no sort**. Over a 500-token answer that is 500 sorts avoided.
  8. Sampling costs **0.26 MFLOP and 256 KB** — about 0.03% of a decode step. Shipped defaults at the time of writing: Llama-3.1 `0.6 / 0.9`; Qwen2.5 `0.7 / 0.8 / 20`.
</KeyNotes>
