You have three lessons of arithmetic. This project turns them into a decision.

The deliverable is a document that answers one question — **what is the cheapest system that meets our SLO?** — with a number attached to every claim, and enough provenance that somebody else could check the arithmetic and disagree with you.

That last part is the actual skill. Anyone can produce a cost per token. The job is producing one that survives review.

<Callout type="mentor" title="This is the shape of a real technical proposal">
  In practice this document appears in three places: a design doc before you build, a cost review when finance asks why the bill moved, and a post-mortem after something broke. Write it so that all three are served — which means every number carries its workload, and every conclusion names what would overturn it.
</Callout>

## What you are building

A **decision document** plus the **harness that produced it**. Four parts:

| Part | What it is | Built from |
|---|---|---|
| **Workload & SLO** | The traffic shape you are sizing for, and the promise you must keep | Lesson 06, this lesson |
| **Candidate matrix** | Every configuration you considered, with derived and measured columns kept apart | Lessons 25.1, 25.2 |
| **Operating point & fleet** | The chosen configuration, its concurrency, and how many of them | This lesson |

The optional notebook below lets you verify the arithmetic against a real GPU. You do not need a T4 to complete the project — but running it will separate the configurations you *derived* from the ones you *measured*, which is the distinction the whole document rests on.

## Step 1 · Write the workload down

Everything downstream inherits this section, so make it precise. Two engineers reading it should compute the same numbers.

**What to state:**

- **Model** — name and parameter count, and whether you control the choice.
- **Prompt and output length distributions** — not averages. The median and the p90, because the tail is where the money is (Module 06).
- **Arrival rate** — requests per second at peak sustained, not instantaneous spike.
- **Concurrency you intend** — the batch size you will actually run.
- **Traffic shape** — interactive chat, overnight batch, or a mixture, and the proportion of each.

<Callout type="math" title="Why distributions and not averages">
  Machine time per request is $t_{pre} + n_{out}\,t_{step}/B$, and it is close to **linear in output length**. So the *mean* machine time is driven by the *tail* of the output distribution, and a p90 of 800 tokens with a p50 of 120 means your real cost per request is closer to the tail than to the median.

  A product with a median of 50 output tokens and a p95 of 2,000 is not a cheap product. This is the same lesson as "mean latency describes no actual request", applied to money instead of milliseconds.
</Callout>

**A worked example you can copy** — a customer-support assistant:

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Model | Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (chosen in Module 04) |
| Prompt length | median 600, p90 2,400 (long conversation history) |
| Output length | median 180, p90 600 (a paragraph to a few paragraphs) |
| Arrival rate | 8 req/s at peak sustained, ~0.3 req/s overnight |
| Intended concurrency | 64 streams |
| Prefix reuse | 400-token shared system prompt, high reuse |

## Step 2 · Write the SLO down

From Module 06, an SLO has four parts. Write all four, and write them **before** you look at any cost number — otherwise you will unconsciously pick a config that meets the SLO you can afford rather than the one you should.

> **p95 TTFT under 600 ms and p95 TPOT under 40 ms, over any rolling 5-minute window, at 8 req/s.**

That is the promise. Everything below is judged against it.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The SLO is a constraint, not a target">
  A candidate that misses the SLO is **eliminated**, not scored badly. This is the hard-constraint discipline from Module 17, and it runs *before* any cost comparison — a configuration that cannot meet the promise can never be the cheapest acceptable system, however cheap it looks.

  The most common failure here is quietly relaxing the SLO to fit a cheaper configuration. Write the SLO first, in writing, and treat it as fixed.
</Callout>

## Step 3 · Build the candidate matrix

Enumerate configurations across the four axes that actually move cost. For the worked example:

| Axis | Values to try |
|---|---|
| **Model size** | 8B, 32B (and 70B if quality demands it) |
| **Precision** | BF16, FP8, INT4 (Module 11) |
| **GPU** | A100 80GB, H100, H200 (Module 25.2) |
| **Concurrency** | 32, 64, 128 streams |

That is 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81 configurations. You do not score 81 — you **eliminate** most of them and score the survivors.

### Keep the column kinds apart

From Module 17, three kinds of column, never mixed:

| Kind | Example | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Derived** | weights 16.1 GB, KV pool, max streams, machine s/request | Exact, from your arithmetic |
| **Measured** | tok/s at the operating point, p95 TTFT, SLO pass rate | High, if your harness contract held |
| **Structural** | does this engine have an INT4 path for this model, is a build required | Certain, from documentation |

<Callout type="math" title="Derived column: free, exact, and it eliminates first">
  For each candidate you can compute, with no GPU:

  $$
  \text{weights} = P \times \text{bytes per weight}, \quad
  \text{KV bytes/token} = 2 \times L \times H_{kv} \times d_h \times \text{bytes}
  $$

  $$
  \text{max streams} = \frac{\text{mem} - \text{weights} - \text{reserve}}{\text{KV bytes/token} \times \text{context}}
  $$

  Here is that column for the support-assistant workload, at 64 streams and 2,048 tokens of context on an 80 GB card. Every number is arithmetic:

  | Model | Precision | Weights | Max streams on H100 | Fits 64 streams? | \$/M output |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Llama-3.1-8B | BF16 | 16.1 GB | 226 | yes | 0.227 |
  | **Llama-3.1-8B** | **FP8** | **8.0 GB** | **256** | **yes** | **0.201** |
  | Llama-3.1-8B | INT4 | 4.0 GB | 271 | yes | 0.198 |
  | Qwen2.5-32B | BF16 | 65.6 GB | 20 | **no — needs 64** | 0.708 |
  | Qwen2.5-32B | FP8 | 32.8 GB | 81 | yes | 0.593 |
  | Qwen2.5-32B | INT4 | 16.4 GB | 112 | yes | 0.556 |

  *(Derived from weight and KV arithmetic at MBU 0.8, MFU 0.55, with the MBU discount for lower precision from lesson 25.1. Approximate — measure before you commit.)*

  Three things are already decided by that table, with no benchmark run at all:

  - **The 32B model costs about 3× the price per token** of the 8B. That is the argument you will have with whoever wants the bigger model, and it is an arithmetic argument.
  - **BF16 32B is eliminated** — 65.6 GB of weights leaves only 20 streams' worth of KV on an 80 GB card, so 64 concurrent streams is impossible. It is not "slow"; it does not run.
  - **FP8 is the sweet spot**: it lands far closer to INT4 (0.198) than to BF16 (0.227), so you are buying almost all of the saving at a quality bar far easier to defend (Module 11).

  The derived column eliminated one candidate outright and ordered the rest, for free, before a single GPU was switched on.
</Callout>

## Step 4 · Eliminate, then score

Apply in this order. **The order matters more than the arithmetic.**

**Stage 1 — hard constraints.** Each of these is a yes/no, and a candidate that fails one is out:

- Does the model fit, with room for KV and a reserve?
- Does it meet the SLO at the intended concurrency? (measured)
- Is there an engine path for this precision on this GPU?

**Stage 2 — score the survivors** on value per dollar, with weights you write down *before* you compute the scores.

**Stage 3 — sensitivity.** If your top two are within about 10%, say so and name the tie-breaker explicitly.

<Callout type="intuition" title="How to weight the criteria">
  For most interactive products the weighting is close to:

  - **Goodput per dollar** — 3.0 (it is the deliverable)
  - **SLO headroom at peak** — 2.5 (how much room before you break)
  - **Quality vs the best available** — 3.0 (a cheaper model that fails the eval is not cheaper)
  - **Operational simplicity** — 1.0 (fewer moving parts)
  - **Team familiarity** — 0.5 (tie-breaker only)

  The weight *is* the argument. If your product is overnight batch with no latency SLO, the weights are completely different — and that is a legitimate choice, provided you say so.
</Callout>

## Step 5 · Choose the operating point and the fleet

From lesson 25.3, for the surviving configuration:

1. **Operating point** — the concurrency maximizing goodput per dollar, *subject to the SLO*. Not peak throughput.
2. **Per-replica capacity** — derived max streams, minus a margin.
3. **Fleet** — arrival rate × machine seconds per request ÷ target utilization, rounded up, plus committed headroom.

<Callout type="math" title="The fleet arithmetic, in one block">
  At the chosen operating point (Llama-3.1-8B, FP8 weights, H100, 64 streams, 600-in/180-out):

  $$
  t_{pre} \approx 19.7\ \text{ms}, \quad t_{step} \approx 7.0\ \text{ms}
  $$

  $$
  m = 0.0197 + \frac{180 \times 0.0070}{64} = 0.0197 + 0.0197 = 0.039\ \text{GPU-seconds per request}
  $$

  At 8 req/s, busy GPUs $= 8 \times 0.039 = 0.31$. Dividing by a 60% target gives 0.52 cards, so **one card is the floor**.

  Note how FP8 earns its place: at 8.0 GB of weights instead of 16.1, the step drops from 9.7 ms to 7.0 ms and the cost per million output tokens from \$0.18 to \$0.15 — a **16% saving**, from halving the bytes each step must read. That is the memory-bound argument from lesson 25.2 doing exactly what it should.

  One card is not a deployment. Committed floor **2** (\$3,650/month), autoscale to 4 on queue depth, because a single-card service has no spare capacity for a deploy, a node drain, or a spike — and because at 8 req/s the margin is thin.
</Callout>

## Step 6 · Sensitivity: what would change your mind

This is the section that separates a decision from a preference, and it is the one most often omitted.

**State, explicitly:**

- **The cheapest upgrade path.** If output length grows 2×, does the configuration still win?
- **The utilization threshold.** At what average utilization does the bill double?
- **The hardware price break.** At what H100 price would an A100 become the better choice? (Use the break-even test from lesson 25.2.)
- **The quality cliff.** At what quality regression does the cheaper model stop being acceptable? (Module 11's eval harness.)
- **The SLO you would relax.** If you had to trade latency for cost, how far would you go — and who signs off?

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The question that catches teams out">
  "What happens when a new model releases?" is not a sensitivity, it is a hope.

  The real version: at what point does a model upgrade become worth more than the engineering cost of validating it? For most teams the answer is "when the incumbent fails the eval on traffic we care about" — and that eval has to already exist, or the question is unanswerable.
</Callout>

## Extend it (pick at least two)

- **A/B two precisions on real traffic.** FP8 versus BF16 on the same harness, interleaved, with a quality check. Report whether the difference exceeds your run-to-run spread (Module 17).
- **A traffic-mix sweep.** Compute the bill across chat / RAG / batch profiles and show where the crossover between two configurations lies.
- **A fleet simulator.** Build the in-browser simulator your own team would use: arrival rate, machine time per request, target utilization → fleet size and monthly bill, with a cost-per-request readout.
- **A break-even table across five GPU prices.** Show the price at which each candidate becomes the winner, so the decision survives a pricing change.
- **A cost regression test.** Save the derived cost per token in version control and fail CI when it moves more than 10% without a reviewed change.

## Check yourself

Answer these before you call it done. If any answer is "no", the document is not finished.

1. Could a colleague who has never seen your work recompute your machine-seconds figure from what you wrote? (Name the model, GPU, precision, concurrency and both length distributions.)
2. Is your SLO written down **before** any cost number, and does every candidate explicitly pass or fail it?
3. Have you separated derived, measured and structural columns, and labelled every cell?
4. Did you eliminate on hard constraints *before* scoring on value?
5. Does your document state the operating point, the fleet, and the monthly bill — each with a number?
6. Have you named what would change your mind, with thresholds?
7. Is there a cheaper configuration you rejected for a *stated* reason rather than a vague one?

## Acceptance criteria

**Rigor**
- The workload section states distributions (median and p90), not averages, for both prompt and output.
- The SLO has all four parts and was written before any cost was computed.
- Every configuration in the matrix is labelled derived, measured or structural.
- Candidates are eliminated on hard constraints before any scoring.

**Decision quality**
- The chosen configuration is justified by goodput per dollar, not by peak throughput.
- The operating point is justified against the SLO, with a measured or explicitly-labelled-derived basis.
- The fleet has a floor, a committed number, a headroom argument and a scaling trigger.

**Communication**
- The document states the decision in one sentence, with a number attached.
- Every claim has a sensitivity attached, with thresholds.
- A reader can disagree with a specific claim and say which number is wrong.

## Rubric

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Distinguished** | Eliminates on constraints first, scores survivors by goodput per dollar, and ships a sensitivity section with real thresholds. The fleet has a committed floor and a named scaling trigger. Someone else could recompute the machine-seconds figure from the document alone. The decision is stated in one sentence with a number attached. |
| **Proficient** | Meets all acceptance criteria. Matrix is built and labelled, SLO is written, decision is defended by a goodput-per-dollar number. Sensitivity is present but qualitative. |
| **Developing** | Reaches a defensible configuration but the matrix mixes measured and derived numbers in a column, or the SLO is relaxed to fit the cheaper option, or the fleet is sized at peak with no headroom argument. |
| **Beginning** | Quotes a cost per token with no workload, no GPU, no precision, or compares candidates that do not fit in memory. |

<Callout type="mentor" title="The habit that separates the top band">
  Everyone in the top band **runs the arithmetic twice, from scratch, in a different order.**

  Once from the identity outward — machine seconds, then price. Once from the bill inward — "we spend \$3,650 a month, so we can afford N GPU-seconds per hour, so we can afford M seconds per request" — and checks the two agree.

  A document where those two agree is one you can defend in a review. A document where only one was computed is one you are hoping about.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. A decision document answers **one question with a number**: what is the cheapest system that meets the SLO? Everything else in it is provenance.
  2. **Write the workload and the SLO before any cost arithmetic.** Otherwise you will unconsciously pick the configuration that fits the budget rather than the one that fits the promise.
  3. Use **distributions, not averages**, for prompt and output length. Machine time is near-linear in output length, so the p90 drives your bill.
  4. The **derived column eliminates half your matrix for free**: weights, KV pool and max streams are arithmetic, and a candidate that does not fit is not a candidate.
  5. **Hard constraints run before scoring.** Missing the SLO eliminates a configuration; it does not score it badly. Relaxing the SLO to fit a cheaper option is the most common failure in this project.
  6. Keep **derived, measured and structural** columns apart and label every cell — the discipline from Module 17, now applied to money.
  7. Report the fleet as **floor + committed headroom + a named scaling trigger**. Sized at peak sustained utilization, with no spare, it will fail the first deploy.
  8. Ship a **sensitivity section with thresholds**: what utilization doubles the bill, what output length flips the answer, what price makes the cheaper card win. A decision without one is a preference.
</KeyNotes>
