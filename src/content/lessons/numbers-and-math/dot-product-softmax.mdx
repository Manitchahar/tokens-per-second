Attention, the mechanism that lets each token look back at earlier tokens, is built from two small operations. A **dot product** measures how relevant one token is to another. **Softmax** turns those relevance scores into weights that add up to 1. Sampling the next word uses softmax too. Learn these two and the scariest-looking equation in the field, $\text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})V$, becomes plain.

  <Step title="1. A vector is an arrow">
    A vector with 2 numbers, like q = (3, 1), is an arrow: 3 steps right, 1 step up. Real attention heads use 128 numbers per vector. You cannot draw 128 dimensions, but the geometry works exactly the same.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. The dot product">
    The **dot product** of two vectors multiplies matching coordinates and adds them up:

    $q \cdot k = q_1 k_1 + q_2 k_2 + \dots + q_d k_d$

    For q = (3, 1) and k = (2, 2): 3 × 2 + 1 × 2 = **8**. You already used this: every cell of a matrix multiply is one dot product.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Alignment = similarity">
    Geometrically, $q \cdot k = |q|\,|k| \cos\theta$, where θ is the angle between the arrows. So the dot product is **large when they point the same way**, zero when perpendicular, and negative when opposite.

    Drag the query around. Whichever key it points toward scores highest. In attention, the query asks *"what am I looking for?"* and each key says *"this is what I contain"*. The dot product measures the match.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Exponentiate">
    We want weights that are all **positive** and sum to 1. Step one: raise *e* ≈ 2.718 to the power of each score. Negative scores become small positive numbers; big scores become *huge* numbers.

    Exponentials exaggerate differences: a score 2 higher becomes e² ≈ 7.4× bigger.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Normalize: softmax">
    Divide each exponential by their sum. Now they are positive and add to exactly 1 (100%). That's **softmax**:

    $\text{softmax}(x)_i = \dfrac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}}$

    It is a "soft" version of picking the maximum: the winner gets most of the weight, but the others keep a share.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Temperature">
    Divide the scores by a **temperature** T before the softmax. **T < 1** stretches differences, so the winner takes nearly everything (sharper). **T > 1** shrinks differences, so the probabilities flatten toward equal.

    Try the temperature slider. This is the exact same knob as the *temperature* setting in a chatbot API. You will meet it again in sampling (Module 03).
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. Staying numerically safe">
    e^100 is about 2.7 × 10⁴³, far too big for FP16 (which tops out at 65,504, about e^11). Real code subtracts the largest score first:

    $\text{softmax}(x)_i = \dfrac{e^{x_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{x_j - m}}, \quad m = \max_j x_j$

    The e^(−m) factors cancel top and bottom, so the answer is identical, but now every exponent is ≤ 0 and every e^x is between 0 and 1. No overflow. FlashAttention (Module 14) is built on a clever extension of this exact trick.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Work it by hand once

Scores $x = [2, 1, 0]$:

| | token A | token B | token C | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| score $x_i$ | 2 | 1 | 0 | |
| $e^{x_i}$ | 7.39 | 2.72 | 1.00 | 11.11 |
| softmax | **0.665** | 0.245 | 0.090 | 1.000 |

With temperature T = 0.5 the scores become [4, 2, 0] → softmax ≈ [0.867, 0.117, 0.016]: sharper. With T = 2 they become [1, 0.5, 0] → ≈ [0.506, 0.307, 0.186]: flatter.

## Why the scaling √d shows up in attention

With 128-number vectors, dot products add up 128 terms, so they get large, and a softmax over large scores becomes almost one-hot (all weight on one key). Attention divides scores by $\sqrt{d}$ (for d = 128, about 11.3) to keep them in a sensible range. That is literally a fixed temperature of √d. You will see it in the attention formula next module.

## Numerical stability: why subtracting the max prevents FP16 overflow

In production inference, models run in 16-bit floating point formats (FP16 and BF16) to conserve GPU memory and utilize tensor cores. In IEEE 754 half precision (FP16), numbers use 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, and 10 mantissa bits.

The maximum finite number representable in FP16 is exactly:

$$
(2 - 2^{-10}) \times 2^{15} = 65,504
$$

Any intermediate calculation that exceeds 65,504 immediately overflows to positive infinity (`+inf`).

Now consider what happens in naive softmax when logits grow even slightly large. To find the exponent value that causes an overflow in FP16, we take the natural logarithm of 65,504:

$$
\ln(65504) \approx 11.08986
$$

If any unnormalized logit or attention score $x_i$ exceeds roughly 11.09, $e^{x_i}$ evaluates to `+inf`. For example, suppose a model produces raw scores $x = [12.0, 8.0, 4.0]$. If you compute exponentials directly:

- $e^{12.0} \approx 162,754.79 \to \text{overflows to } +\infty$
- $e^{8.0} \approx 2,980.96$
- $e^{4.0} \approx 54.60$

When computing the denominator, the sum includes infinity. When normalizing the first score, the division produces:

$$
\frac{+\infty}{+\infty + 2980.96 + 54.60} = \frac{+\infty}{+\infty} = \text{NaN}
$$

The result is Not-a-Number (NaN). Once a single NaN appears inside a layer, every subsequent matrix multiply and residual add propagates NaNs across all tokens. In production serving, this manifests as generation crashing or the server outputting empty strings.

### The mathematical fix: subtract the max

To prevent overflow, every production inference engine and GPU kernel implements **safe softmax**. We find the maximum score in the vector:

$$
m = \max_j x_j
$$

Then we subtract $m$ from each score before exponentiating:

$$
\text{softmax}(x)_i = \dfrac{e^{x_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{x_j - m}}
$$

Why is this mathematically identical to naive softmax? We factor out $e^{-m}$ from the numerator and denominator:

$$
\dfrac{e^{x_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{x_j - m}} = \dfrac{e^{x_i} \cdot e^{-m}}{\sum_j (e^{x_j} \cdot e^{-m})} = \dfrac{e^{x_i} \cdot e^{-m}}{e^{-m} \sum_j e^{x_j}} = \dfrac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}}
$$

The scalar $e^{-m}$ cancels out completely.

Notice the numerical guarantee: because $m$ is the maximum value in the set, $x_i - m \le 0$ for every element. Exponentiating any non-positive number yields a result strictly between 0 and 1:

$$
e^{x_i - m} \in (0, 1]
$$

The largest possible exponential is $e^{m - m} = e^0 = 1.0$. Overflow is impossible.

Let us trace our problematic vector $x = [12.0, 8.0, 4.0]$ with safe softmax:

1. Maximum value: $m = 12.0$.
2. Shifted scores: $[12.0 - 12.0, 8.0 - 12.0, 4.0 - 12.0] = [0.0, -4.0, -8.0]$.
3. Exponentials:
   - $e^0 = 1.0$
   - $e^{-4} \approx 0.018316$
   - $e^{-8} \approx 0.000335$
4. Sum of exponentials: 1.0 + 0.018316 + 0.000335 = 1.018651.
5. Probabilities:
   - $p_0 = 1.0 \div 1.018651 \approx \mathbf{0.9817}$
   - $p_1 = 0.018316 \div 1.018651 \approx \mathbf{0.0180}$
   - $p_2 = 0.000335 \div 1.018651 \approx \mathbf{0.0003}$

The probabilities sum to exactly 1.0000, with no numerical instability or overflow.

## Temperature: steering probability distributions

Temperature $T$ is the primary knob used to control token diversity during generation. When you call an LLM API and set `temperature=0.7`, you are dividing the output logits by $T$ prior to softmax:

$$
\text{softmax}(x / T)_i = \dfrac{e^{(x_i / T) - m'}}{\sum_j e^{(x_j / T) - m'}}, \quad m' = \max_j (x_j / T)
$$

Let us work through a 3-number example by hand to see how temperature reshapes probabilities. Consider raw logits $z = [6.0, 4.0, 2.0]$.

### Case 1: Baseline temperature ($T = 1.0$)

When $T = 1.0$, the scores remain $[6.0, 4.0, 2.0]$. The maximum is $m = 6.0$.
- Shifted scores: $[0.0, -2.0, -4.0]$.
- Exponentials: $e^0 = 1.0$, $e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$, $e^{-4} \approx 0.0183$.
- Sum: 1.0 + 0.1353 + 0.0183 = 1.1536.
- Probabilities:
  - $p_0 = 1.0 \div 1.1536 \approx \mathbf{0.8668}$
  - $p_1 = 0.1353 \div 1.1536 \approx \mathbf{0.1173}$
  - $p_2 = 0.0183 \div 1.1536 \approx \mathbf{0.0159}$

### Case 2: Low temperature ($T = 0.5$)

Dividing by 0.5 doubles all scores: $z / 0.5 = [12.0, 8.0, 4.0]$. The maximum is $m = 12.0$.
- Shifted scores: $[0.0, -4.0, -8.0]$.
- Exponentials: $e^0 = 1.0$, $e^{-4} \approx 0.0183$, $e^{-8} \approx 0.0003$.
- Sum: 1.0 + 0.0183 + 0.0003 = 1.0186.
- Probabilities:
  - $p_0 = 1.0 \div 1.0186 \approx \mathbf{0.9817}$
  - $p_1 = 0.0183 \div 1.0186 \approx \mathbf{0.0180}$
  - $p_2 = 0.0003 \div 1.0186 \approx \mathbf{0.0003}$

The gap between scores expanded. The top choice went from 86.7% to 98.2%. At very low temperatures ($T \to 0$), the highest score takes 100% of the probability mass, reducing softmax to an **argmax** operation (greedy sampling).

### Case 3: High temperature ($T = 2.0$)

Dividing by 2.0 halves all scores: $z / 2.0 = [3.0, 2.0, 1.0]$. The maximum is $m = 3.0$.
- Shifted scores: $[0.0, -1.0, -2.0]$.
- Exponentials: $e^0 = 1.0$, $e^{-1} \approx 0.3679$, $e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$.
- Sum: 1.0 + 0.3679 + 0.1353 = 1.5032.
- Probabilities:
  - $p_0 = 1.0 \div 1.5032 \approx \mathbf{0.6652}$
  - $p_1 = 0.3679 \div 1.5032 \approx \mathbf{0.2447}$
  - $p_2 = 0.1353 \div 1.5032 \approx \mathbf{0.0900}$

The differences are compressed. The top choice drops to 66.5%, while the trailing options gain significant probability mass. As $T \to \infty$, all probabilities approach equal values: $\frac{1}{N} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.3333$ (a uniform distribution).

## Cosine similarity vs dot product: why magnitude matters

Learners often ask: *“Why does attention use dot products instead of cosine similarity?”*

Recall the mathematical definitions for two vectors $q$ and $k$:

$$
\text{dot product}: \quad q \cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^d q_i k_i = \|q\| \|k\| \cos\theta
$$

$$
\text{cosine similarity}: \quad \frac{q \cdot k}{\|q\| \|k\|} = \cos\theta
$$

Cosine similarity normalizes both vectors to unit length ($\|q\| = 1$, $\|k\| = 1$), isolating only the directional angle $\theta$. Its output is strictly bounded between −1 and +1.

In contrast, the dot product scales with both angle AND vector lengths. Why do transformer models rely on dot products?

1. **Expressive confidence**: By adjusting vector length during training, a model can express confidence. If an attention head determines that a specific key is critical, it can scale the magnitude of the query or key vector. Because softmax uses exponentials, a larger dot product creates a wider score gap, allowing the attention head to place 99% of its weight on that key. If attention were restricted to cosine similarity bounded between −1 and +1, the maximum score difference would be 2.0, severely limiting how sharply attention could focus.
2. **Computational efficiency**: Normalizing vectors to unit length requires calculating square roots ($\sqrt{\sum x_i^2}$) and reciprocal divisions for every query and key vector. In contrast, unnormalized dot products between thousands of vectors are computed as a single matrix multiplication ($Q K^\top$), running at peak speed on GPU tensor cores. Instead of per-vector normalization, transformers apply RMSNorm once at the block input, stabilizing overall activations without slowing down attention.

## Why standard softmax requires two passes over memory

To compute safe softmax over an array of $N$ numbers on a GPU, standard implementations require **two sequential passes** over memory:

1. **Pass 1 (Find maximum)**: The GPU reads all $N$ numbers from High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) into fast on-chip registers, performs a parallel tree reduction across threads, and computes $m = \max_j x_j$.
2. **Pass 2 (Exponentials and sum)**: The GPU reads all $N$ numbers from HBM a second time, computes $e^{x_j - m}$, accumulates their sum $S = \sum_j e^{x_j - m}$, and writes the exponentials back to HBM.
3. **Normalization**: The GPU reads the exponentials and divides each by $S$, writing the final probabilities back to HBM.

### The quadratic memory problem

For single-token decoding, $N$ is the context length, which fits easily in on-chip SRAM. But during prefill, attention computes an $N \times N$ matrix representing the interaction between every query token and every key token.

If context length is $N = 32,768$ (32K tokens):

$$
N \times N = 32,768 \times 32,768 = 1,073,741,824 \text{ scores}
$$

In 16-bit precision (2 bytes per score), this single attention matrix occupies:

$$
1,073,741,824 \times 2 \approx 2.15 \text{ GB}
$$

That is 2.15 GB of memory for a single attention head in a single layer! For a model with 32 layers and 32 heads, materializing full attention matrices in HBM would require over 2,000 GB (2 TB) of memory, causing instant out-of-memory crashes.

Furthermore, reading and writing gigabytes of intermediate scores across HBM multiple times saturates the memory bus, dragging GPU utilization down.

### Foreshadowing FlashAttention: online softmax

How do modern inference engines run 32K, 64K, and 128K contexts without running out of memory?

In Module 14 (Attention Optimization), you will study **online softmax** and FlashAttention. By maintaining a running maximum and running denominator, the attention kernel computes softmax incrementally across small tiles of tokens that reside entirely in on-chip SRAM (~100 KB per Streaming Multiprocessor). The intermediate $N \times N$ attention matrix is never written to slow HBM, eliminating the two-pass memory traffic and reducing memory consumption from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N)$.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Common mistake: computing softmax along the wrong dimension">
  In PyTorch, `torch.softmax(tensor, dim)` requires an explicit `dim` argument. For attention scores of shape `[batch, heads, queries, keys]`, the reduction must happen across keys: `dim=-1`.
  
  If you omit `dim` or mistakenly specify `dim=0`, PyTorch normalizes probabilities across batches or queries instead of keys. The resulting numbers still sum to 1 and look like valid probabilities, but the model produces complete nonsense. Always verify that `dim=-1` is explicitly specified when normalizing attention logits.
</Callout>

## In code: stable softmax and temperature

Here is a runnable Python snippet verifying naive softmax overflow, numerically safe softmax, temperature scaling, and cosine similarity:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

# 1. Overflow in FP16 naive softmax vs safe softmax
scores = torch.tensor([12.0, 8.0, 4.0], dtype=torch.float16)

# Naive softmax: e^12 in FP16 exceeds 65504 -> inf
naive_exp = torch.exp(scores)
naive_softmax = naive_exp / naive_exp.sum()
print("Naive FP16 softmax:", naive_softmax)

# Safe softmax: subtract max first
m = scores.max()
safe_exp = torch.exp(scores - m)
safe_softmax = safe_exp / safe_exp.sum()
print("Safe FP16 softmax: ", safe_softmax)

# 2. Temperature scaling (T = 1.0, 0.5, 2.0)
logits = torch.tensor([6.0, 4.0, 2.0])
print("\nT = 1.0 (base):  ", torch.softmax(logits / 1.0, dim=-1))
print("T = 0.5 (sharp): ", torch.softmax(logits / 0.5, dim=-1))
print("T = 2.0 (flat):  ", torch.softmax(logits / 2.0, dim=-1))

# 3. Dot product vs Cosine similarity
q = torch.tensor([3.0, 1.0])
k = torch.tensor([2.0, 2.0])
dot_prod = torch.dot(q, k)
cos_sim = F.cosine_similarity(q.unsqueeze(0), k.unsqueeze(0))
print(f"\nDot product: {dot_prod.item():.1f}, Cosine similarity: {cos_sim.item():.4f}")
```

Printed output:

```text
Naive FP16 softmax: tensor([nan, 0., 0.], dtype=torch.float16)
Safe FP16 softmax:  tensor([0.9814, 0.0180, 0.0003], dtype=torch.float16)

T = 1.0 (base):   tensor([0.8668, 0.1173, 0.0159])
T = 0.5 (sharp):  tensor([0.9817, 0.0180, 0.0003])
T = 2.0 (flat):   tensor([0.6652, 0.2447, 0.0900])

Dot product: 8.0, Cosine similarity: 0.8944
```

Notice how naive softmax produced `nan` in FP16, while safe softmax computed the correct distribution effortlessly.

<Callout type="real">
  In a decode step, attention runs a softmax over every previous token for every head in every layer. For a 32-layer model with 32 heads and 8,000 tokens of context, that is 32 × 32 × 8,000 ≈ 8 million exponentials per generated token. Tiny operations add up. Where softmax runs, and whether its inputs sit in fast on-chip memory, decides much of attention's speed.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. **Dot product** = sum of pairwise products = |q||k|cos θ. It measures alignment while scaling with vector magnitude.
  2. Each cell of a matrix multiply is a dot product; attention scores are dot products of queries with keys.
  3. **Softmax** exponentiates scores and divides by their sum, yielding positive probabilities that sum to 1.
  4. In FP16, exponentials overflow above 65,504 (scores &gt; 11.09). **Safe softmax** subtracts the maximum score ($m = \max x_j$), keeping all exponents $\le 0$.
  5. **Temperature** divides scores before softmax: $T &lt; 1$ sharpens differences toward argmax; $T &gt; 1$ flattens probabilities toward uniform.
  6. Cosine similarity discards vector length; attention uses dot products so models can express attention confidence through vector norms.
  7. Standard softmax requires two memory passes over HBM (one to find max, one to sum); FlashAttention uses online softmax to compute attention in on-chip SRAM.
  8. Attention scales scores by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ to prevent large dot products from saturating softmax into one-hot distributions.
</KeyNotes>
