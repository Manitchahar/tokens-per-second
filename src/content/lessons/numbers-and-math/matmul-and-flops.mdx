If you profiled an LLM and sorted every GPU operation by time, one would sit at the top, far ahead of the rest: **matrix multiplication**, called a **GEMM** (GEneral Matrix Multiply) in GPU-speak. Over 90% of an LLM's arithmetic is GEMMs. Understand this one operation deeply and you understand where the time goes.

## What a matrix multiply computes

<Scrolly>
  <MatmulViz client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. The setup">
    Take **A**, 3 rows × 4 columns. Think of it as 3 tokens, each a vector of 4 numbers (tiny, so we can watch). Take **B**, 4 × 3: a weight matrix. Their product **C = A × B** has shape 3 × 3.

    The rule for shapes: `[m × k] × [k × n] → [m × n]`. The inner sizes (**k**) must match, and they disappear.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. One output cell">
    Each cell of C is a **dot product**. To get C[0, 0], walk along **row 0 of A** and down **column 0 of B**, multiply the pairs, and add them up: 1×2 + 2×1 + 0×0 + 1×1 = **5**.

    That is all a matrix multiply is: lots of dot products, one per output cell.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Count the work">
    That cell needed **k = 4 multiplications** and 4 additions (counting the first add into zero). Each is one **FLOP**, a floating-point operation. So one output cell costs **2k FLOPs**.

    GPUs actually do a multiply and an add as one "fused multiply-add" (FMA), but everyone still counts it as 2 FLOPs, and so will we.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Every cell">
    Repeat for every cell: every row of A meets every column of B. There are m × n = 9 cells, each costing 2k = 8 FLOPs.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. The formula">
    Total work:

    $$\text{FLOPs} = 2 \cdot m \cdot k \cdot n$$

    For a real layer, 1,000 tokens through a 4,096 × 4,096 weight, that is 2 × 1000 × 4096 × 4096 ≈ **33.6 billion FLOPs**. For one layer. Of one kind. Now you see why we need GPUs.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Decode: matrix × vector">
    During decode we feed **one token** at a time, so m = 1. The GPU must load all 33.5 MB of this weight from memory, then does just 2 × 4096 × 4096 = 33.5 M FLOPs with it. That is about **1 FLOP per byte** loaded. The weight is used once and thrown away.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. Prefill: matrix × matrix">
    During prefill we feed the **whole prompt**, say 512 tokens (m = 512). The weight is loaded once and reused for all 512 rows: **512× more math for the same bytes**.

    This ratio, FLOPs per byte, is called **arithmetic intensity**. It is the single most important number in GPU performance, and Module 02 builds everything on it.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## From one GEMM to a whole model: 2 FLOPs per parameter

Here is a beautiful shortcut you will use for the rest of your career.

In a matrix × vector product, each weight $W_{ij}$ is used in **exactly one** multiply-add: it multiplies one input number and adds into one output. That is 2 FLOPs per weight per token.

Almost all of an LLM's parameters live in such weight matrices, and every token passes through all of them once. So:

$$
\text{FLOPs per token} \approx 2 \times \text{number of parameters}
$$

<Callout type="math" title="Worked example">
  Llama-3.1-8B has about 8 × 10⁹ parameters. Generating one token costs ≈ 2 × 8 × 10⁹ = **16 GFLOP** (16 billion FLOPs).

  A 1,000-token prompt (prefill) costs ≈ 1,000 × 16 GFLOP = **16 TFLOP** (16 trillion).
</Callout>

This ignores the attention computation between tokens (the part that grows with context length). For short and medium contexts it is small. We add it back in Module 05.

## Deriving the 2 × params rule for an 8B model

To understand why this rule of thumb is remarkably accurate, let us dissect the exact matrix projections of Llama-3.1-8B.

Llama-3.1-8B has 32 transformer layers, a hidden dimension $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$, and uses SwiGLU feed-forward networks with intermediate dimension $d_{\text{ff}} = 14336$. Its multi-head attention uses Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) with 32 query heads ($d_q = 4096$) and 8 key/value heads ($d_{kv} = 8 \times 128 = 1024$).

Let us tally every linear projection weight matrix in each of the 32 layers:

1. **Query projection ($W_q$)**: shape `[4096, 4096]`: 4,096 × 4,096 = 16,777,216 parameters.
2. **Key projection ($W_k$)**: shape `[4096, 1024]`: 4,096 × 1,024 = 4,194,304 parameters.
3. **Value projection ($W_v$)**: shape `[4096, 1024]`: 4,096 × 1,024 = 4,194,304 parameters.
4. **Output projection ($W_o$)**: shape `[4096, 4096]`: 4,096 × 4,096 = 16,777,216 parameters.
   - Total attention projection weights per layer: 16,777,216 + 4,194,304 + 4,194,304 + 16,777,216 = **41,943,040 parameters**.
5. **MLP Gate projection ($W_{\text{gate}}$)**: shape `[4096, 14336]`: 4,096 × 14,336 = 58,720,256 parameters.
6. **MLP Up projection ($W_{\text{up}}$)**: shape `[4096, 14336]`: 4,096 × 14,336 = 58,720,256 parameters.
7. **MLP Down projection ($W_{\text{down}}$)**: shape `[14336, 4096]`: 14,336 × 4,096 = 58,720,256 parameters.
   - Total MLP projection weights per layer: 3 × 58,720,256 = **176,160,768 parameters**.

Summing attention and MLP projections gives 41,943,040 + 176,160,768 = **218,103,808 parameters per layer**. Across all 32 layers, the linear weights total:

$$
32 \times 218,103,808 = 6,979,321,856 \text{ parameters} \approx 6.98\text{B}
$$

When you add the embedding table and the final language model head (128,256 × 4,096 ≈ 525,336,576 parameters each, or shared), plus normalization parameters, the total parameter count comes to approximately **8.03 billion parameters**.

Now consider the compute required to pass a single token ($m = 1$) through any weight matrix of shape `[k, n]`:

$$
\text{FLOPs} = 2 \cdot m \cdot k \cdot n = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (k \cdot n) = 2 \times (\text{parameters in that matrix})
$$

Because every token vector must be multiplied by every weight matrix in every layer, summing the compute across the entire model gives:

$$
\text{Total FLOPs per token} = 2 \times \sum \text{parameters} \approx 2 \times 8.03 \times 10^9 \approx \mathbf{16.06 \text{ GFLOPs}}
$$

Every parameter performs exactly 2 FLOPs per token during generation. The quadratic attention score calculations ($Q K^\top$ and softmax weights times $V$) add extra operations, but for prompts up to 4,096 tokens, attention represents less than 2% of the overall compute budget.

## FLOPs vs bytes moved: counting work and memory traffic

Every GPU workload is governed by two fundamental constraints:

1. **Compute work**: the number of floating-point arithmetic operations performed (FLOPs).
2. **Memory traffic**: the number of bytes moved across the memory bus between GPU High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) and on-chip SRAM/registers.

For a matrix multiplication $C = A \cdot B$ with shapes $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}$, and $C \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ in 16-bit precision (FP16 or BF16, 2 bytes per number):

- **Compute work**: Each of the $m \cdot n$ output elements requires $k$ multiply-accumulate operations (2 FLOPs each):
  $$\text{FLOPs} = 2 \cdot m \cdot k \cdot n$$
- **Memory traffic**:
  - Reading matrix $A$: $m \cdot k \times 2$ bytes
  - Reading matrix $B$: $k \cdot n \times 2$ bytes
  - Writing matrix $C$: $m \cdot n \times 2$ bytes
  - Total bytes moved:
    $$\text{Bytes} = 2 \cdot (m \cdot k + k \cdot n + m \cdot n)$$

The ratio of compute work to memory traffic is called **arithmetic intensity** ($I$):

$$
I = \dfrac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{Bytes moved}} = \dfrac{2 \cdot m \cdot k \cdot n}{2 \cdot (m \cdot k + k \cdot n + m \cdot n)} = \dfrac{m \cdot k \cdot n}{m \cdot k + k \cdot n + m \cdot n} \quad [\text{FLOPs/byte}]
$$

Arithmetic intensity measures how much math the GPU extracts from every byte transferred across the memory bus.

## Decode vs prefill arithmetic intensity: a worked derivation

Let us contrast the arithmetic intensity of a decode step against a prefill step using a standard layer projection with $k = n = 4096$ in FP16 (2 bytes per number).

### Case 1: Single-token decode (`[1, 4096] · [4096, 4096]`)

During single-user decode, $m = 1$. The model evaluates one newly generated token against the weight matrix:

- **Compute work**:
  $$\text{FLOPs} = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 4096 \cdot 4096 = 33,554,432 \text{ FLOPs} \approx 33.55 \text{ MFLOPs}$$
- **Memory traffic**:
  - Input $A$ `[1, 4096]`: 1 × 4,096 × 2 = 8,192 bytes
  - Weight $B$ `[4096, 4096]`: 4,096 × 4,096 × 2 = 33,554,432 bytes ≈ 33.55 MB
  - Output $C$ `[1, 4096]`: 1 × 4,096 × 2 = 8,192 bytes
  - Total bytes moved: 8,192 + 33,554,432 + 8,192 = 33,570,816 bytes ≈ **33.57 MB**
- **Arithmetic intensity**:
  $$I_{\text{decode}} = \dfrac{33,554,432 \text{ FLOPs}}{33,570,816 \text{ bytes}} \approx \mathbf{0.9995 \text{ FLOPs/byte}} \approx \mathbf{1.0 \text{ FLOP/byte}}$$

Notice what just happened: to perform 33.55 million math operations, the GPU had to move 33.57 megabytes across the memory bus. That is roughly **1 FLOP per byte loaded**.

Now compare this with hardware specs. An NVIDIA H100 SXM GPU provides **989 TFLOP/s** of dense 16-bit tensor core compute and **3.35 TB/s** of HBM3 memory bandwidth. Its hardware balance ratio (the roofline knee) is:

$$
\dfrac{989 \times 10^{12} \text{ FLOP/s}}{3.35 \times 10^{12} \text{ bytes/s}} \approx \mathbf{295 \text{ FLOPs/byte}}
$$

To keep the tensor cores fully saturated, a kernel must perform at least 295 FLOPs for every byte loaded from memory. Because decode achieves only 1.0 FLOP/byte, it falls 295× short of the compute balance point. Single-token decode is deeply **memory-bound**. The tensor cores sit idle 99.7% of the time, waiting for weights to stream in from memory.

### Case 2: 512-token prompt prefill (`[512, 4096] · [4096, 4096]`)

During prompt prefill, all prompt tokens are processed together in parallel: $m = 512$.

- **Compute work**:
  $$\text{FLOPs} = 2 \cdot 512 \cdot 4096 \cdot 4096 = 17,179,869,184 \text{ FLOPs} \approx 17.18 \text{ GFLOPs}$$
  (The GPU performs 512× more arithmetic than during decode!)
- **Memory traffic**:
  - Input $A$ `[512, 4096]`: 512 × 4,096 × 2 = 4,194,304 bytes ≈ 4.19 MB
  - Weight $B$ `[4096, 4096]`: 4,096 × 4,096 × 2 = 33,554,432 bytes ≈ 33.55 MB
  - Output $C$ `[512, 4096]`: 512 × 4,096 × 2 = 4,194,304 bytes ≈ 4.19 MB
  - Total bytes moved: 4,194,304 + 33,554,432 + 4,194,304 = 41,943,040 bytes ≈ **41.94 MB**
- **Arithmetic intensity**:
  $$I_{\text{prefill}} = \dfrac{17,179,869,184 \text{ FLOPs}}{41,943,040 \text{ bytes}} \approx \mathbf{409.6 \text{ FLOPs/byte}}$$

The arithmetic intensity jumped from 1.0 to **409.6 FLOPs/byte**!

Because 409.6 FLOPs/byte exceeds the H100 balance threshold of 295 FLOPs/byte, prompt prefill is **compute-bound**. The 33.55 MB weight matrix is loaded once into fast on-chip SRAM/registers and reused across all 512 token rows. The memory bus easily keeps up, and tensor cores run near peak capability.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Common mistake: confusing FLOPs with FLOP/s and ignoring memory bandwidth">
  A common beginner mistake is calculating single-user decode speed by dividing peak GPU FLOP/s by model FLOPs per token:
  
  *“My GPU delivers 100 TFLOP/s, and an 8B model needs 16 GFLOPs per token, so I should get 100,000 ÷ 16 = 6,250 tokens per second!”*
  
  In reality, a single user gets around 30 to 60 tokens per second. Why? Because single-user decode does not hit the compute ceiling; it hits the **memory bandwidth ceiling**. To generate one token, the GPU must read all 16 GB of weights from memory.
  
  On an H100 with 3.35 TB/s bandwidth: 16 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 4.8 ms per token (≈ 208 tokens/s maximum). On a T4 with 320 GB/s bandwidth: 16 GB ÷ 320 GB/s ≈ 50 ms per token (≈ 20 tokens/s). Peak FLOP/s only becomes relevant when you batch many requests together to raise arithmetic intensity.
</Callout>

## From FLOPs to seconds

A GPU's compute speed is quoted in **FLOP/s** (FLOPs per second). An H100 does about **989 TFLOP/s** (989 × 10¹² per second) in dense BF16 on its tensor cores. The free T4 does about **65 TFLOP/s** in FP16.

$$
\text{time} \ge \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{FLOP/s}}
$$

The 1,000-token prefill on an H100: 16 × 10¹² ÷ 989 × 10¹² ≈ **16 ms** at perfect efficiency. Real kernels reach 50–70% of peak, so expect around 25–30 ms.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Peak FLOP/s is a ceiling, not a promise">
  Spec sheets list the fastest possible rate, often *with sparsity* (which inference rarely uses). That doubles the number. Always use the **dense** figure, and expect to reach only part of it. The fraction you reach is called **MFU**, model FLOPs utilization.
</Callout>

## The question that drives everything

The same formula says one decode token of an 8B model is 16 GFLOP, which takes an H100 just **0.016 ms** of math. Yet in Module 00 a decode step took about 7 ms. Where did the other 99.8% of the time go?

Into **loading 16 GB of weights from memory** to do that little bit of math. For decode, FLOPs are not the limit; bytes are. Keeping both numbers in view, FLOPs and bytes, is what the next module is about.

## In code: calculating FLOPs and arithmetic intensity

Here is a runnable Python script demonstrating the exact FLOP count, memory traffic, and arithmetic intensity for decode ($m = 1$) versus prefill ($m = 512$):

```python
def matmul_metrics(m: int, k: int, n: int, bytes_per_elem: int = 2):
    flops = 2 * m * k * n
    bytes_a = m * k * bytes_per_elem
    bytes_b = k * n * bytes_per_elem
    bytes_c = m * n * bytes_per_elem
    total_bytes = bytes_a + bytes_b + bytes_c
    intensity = flops / total_bytes
    return flops, total_bytes, intensity

# Compare decode (m = 1) vs prefill (m = 512) for a 4096 x 4096 projection in FP16
f_dec, b_dec, i_dec = matmul_metrics(m=1, k=4096, n=4096, bytes_per_elem=2)
f_pre, b_pre, i_pre = matmul_metrics(m=512, k=4096, n=4096, bytes_per_elem=2)

print(f"Decode (m=1):   {f_dec/1e6:6.2f} MFLOPs, {b_dec/1e6:5.2f} MB moved -> Intensity: {i_dec:5.2f} FLOPs/byte")
print(f"Prefill (m=512): {f_pre/1e9:5.2f} GFLOPs, {b_pre/1e6:5.2f} MB moved -> Intensity: {i_pre:5.2f} FLOPs/byte")
```

Printed output:

```text
Decode (m=1):    33.55 MFLOPs, 33.57 MB moved -> Intensity:  1.00 FLOPs/byte
Prefill (m=512): 17.18 GFLOPs, 41.94 MB moved -> Intensity: 409.60 FLOPs/byte
```

The numbers match our derivations exactly: prefill achieves over 400× higher arithmetic intensity than decode for the same weight matrix.

## Why inference engineers care: bridges to later modules

Understanding GEMM arithmetic intensity is the foundation of inference optimization:

- **Module 02 (The GPU from First Principles)**: You will construct roofline models plotting arithmetic intensity on the horizontal axis to pinpoint whether an operation is memory-bound or compute-bound.
- **Module 05 (Prefill, Decode & the KV Cache)**: You will analyze the fundamental dichotomy between high-intensity prefill and bandwidth-starved decode loops.
- **Module 07 (Batching & Scheduling)**: You will use continuous batching to group decode requests from 64 concurrent users together ($m = 64$), raising arithmetic intensity from 1.0 to ~62 FLOPs/byte and saturating tensor cores.
- **Module 11 (Quantization)**: You will quantize weights from 16-bit to 4-bit (INT4), halving the bytes moved across the bus and directly doubling decode speed on memory-bound workloads.
- **Module 12 (Profiling & GPU Performance)**: You will measure Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) using hardware profilers to verify whether GEMM kernels reach peak hardware efficiency.

<KeyNotes>
  1. Shapes: `[m × k] × [k × n] → [m × n]`. Each output cell is a dot product of a row and a column.
  2. A matrix multiply (GEMM) costs **2·m·k·n FLOPs**: k multiplications and k additions per output cell.
  3. Total memory moved for a GEMM is 2·(mk + kn + mn) bytes in 16-bit precision.
  4. **Arithmetic intensity** ($\text{FLOPs} \div \text{bytes}$) dictates whether an operation is memory-bound or compute-bound.
  5. **Decode** ($m = 1$) has an arithmetic intensity of ~1.0 FLOP/byte: tensor cores sit idle waiting for weights.
  6. **Prefill** ($m = 512$) reuses weights across all tokens, achieving ~410 FLOPs/byte and running compute-bound.
  7. Each weight is used once per token, so a forward pass costs about **2 × parameters FLOPs per token** (~16 GFLOPs for Llama-3.1-8B).
  8. Batching multiple users raises $m$, converting low-intensity decode operations into high-throughput tensor core workloads.
</KeyNotes>
