Five lessons of argument, and so far you have had to take every claim on trust: that BERT uses both sides, that 15% of tokens are graded, that a fresh model starts at 10.82 nats, that a decoder cannot see the right-hand side, that an encoder cannot write. This lab checks all of them, on the real `bert-base-uncased` and the real 124M `gpt2`, in about ten minutes on the free Colab T4.

Both models are small: together about a gigabyte to download, and they run fine in full 32-bit precision. That matters here, because it means every loss you print is exact, and the notebook can *assert* the identities the lessons derived instead of eyeballing them.

## The map

Every experiment in the notebook is about one question: **what is each model allowed to look at, and what does that cost?** Step through the map before you run anything, so you know what each printout is testing. The notebook's section numbers are in each step.

<Scrolly>
  <TwoJobsLab client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. BERT fills a blank from both sides">
    Section 1 of the notebook. BERT reads every token around the `[MASK]`, left and right, and ranks its whole vocabulary in **one pass**. Drag the blank along the sentence: the evidence always arrives from both directions.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. GPT-2 predicts from the left only">
    Section 3. GPT-2 predicts each token from the tokens before it, and the table of per-token losses you will print is exactly these predictions, **all made in one forward pass** thanks to the causal mask.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. The blank near the start">
    Section 5a. For "The ___ was steep and muddy", GPT-2 predicting the token after "The" sees **only "The"**. Every word that tells you it is a river bank is hidden from it.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Scoring whole sentences instead">
    GPT-2's workaround: write each candidate sentence out in full and compare their total surprise. The right-hand side now counts, but at **one full pass per candidate**. Drag the slider to 1,000 candidates and read the bill.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Asking BERT to write">
    Section 5b. BERT fills six masks at the end of a prompt, one per pass, re-reading the **whole sequence every time** because every token sees every other. Nothing from one pass can be reused in the next.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Graded positions per pass">
    Section 6a. On the same text, GPT-2 is graded at every position and BERT at about 15% of them. The notebook counts both on the same batch.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Task 1 · BERT fills the blank

Run section 1. The fill-mask pipeline prints BERT's top five guesses, with probabilities, for four sentences.

**Expected:** "paris" at the top for "The capital of France is `[MASK]`", with most of the probability. The two "bank" sentences are identical up to the blank, and differ only in what comes *after* it ("steep and muddy … water" versus "closed on Sunday … money"). Their top guesses should differ.

**How to read it:** that difference is the whole of lesson 3 in one printout. Anything that changed between those two lists came from the right-hand side of the gap, which a left-to-right model could never have used.

Then the parameter check: the cell rebuilds BERT-Base's size from its config and asserts it equals **109,482,240**, the number derived in lesson 3. It also prints the share taken by the embeddings (about 22%). If the assertion fails, a matrix is missing from the formula; find it.

## Task 2 · Measure the masking recipe

Run section 2. It cuts a passage into 64 sequences of 128 tokens, runs Hugging Face's `DataCollatorForLanguageModeling` with `mlm_probability=0.15`, and **counts** what it actually did.

**Expected:** about 15% of real tokens chosen, and of those roughly 80% turned into `[MASK]`, 10% into a random token and 10% left alone. Around 19 graded positions per sequence (0.15 × 126 real tokens = 18.9). The numbers will not be exact, because each choice is a random draw.

Then it computes the masked-LM loss on the chosen positions only and prints it next to the uniform-guess baseline, $\ln 30{,}522 = 10.33$ nats. The printed example sequence marks each graded position: `[MASK](word)` for a masked one, `{x→word}` for a random replacement, `<word>` for one left unchanged. Find the unchanged ones; the model is graded on them even though nothing about them looks special.

## Task 3 · GPT-2, token by token

Run section 3. It confirms GPT-2's **124,439,808** parameters from its config (the output head is tied to the embedding matrix, so it adds nothing), generates a short continuation greedily and by sampling, then prints a table: for every token of "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired.", the probability GPT-2 gave it, and its surprise in nats and bits.

**Expected:** small words that grammar nearly forces ("on", "the" after "sat") cost well under a nat; content words cost several. The mean for an ordinary sentence typically lands somewhere around 3 to 5 nats per token, a perplexity in the tens. The cell asserts that your hand-computed mean equals Hugging Face's own `labels=` loss.

**How to read it:** the bits-per-byte figure printed below the table is the same total surprise divided by the sentence's 44 UTF-8 bytes, so it is directly comparable with any other model on the same text, whatever its tokenizer (lesson 2).

Then the four-sentence comparison. **Rank them before you run.** Expect the shuffled sentence to be far worse than the natural one: same words, same tokens, but no grammar. The "false fact" sentence should be worse than the "true fact" one, but only a little, because only the last word is surprising and the average is over every token. That is the pitfall from lesson 2 in miniature: perplexity averages away the one token you might care about.

## Task 4 · An untrained model starts at ln V

Run section 4. It builds a GPT-2 with **random weights** (same shape, nothing learned) and scores the natural sentence.

**Expected:** a loss close to $\ln 50{,}257 = 10.82$ nats, a perplexity in the tens of thousands. The assertion allows a margin of 1 nat, because random weights do not give a perfectly flat distribution; in practice it lands within a few tenths.

**How to read it:** put this next to the trained model's loss on the same sentence from Task 3. The gap between them, around 6 to 8 nats, is everything pretraining on WebText bought, measured on one sentence.

## Task 5 · Swap the jobs

**5a.** The cell gives GPT-2 just "The" and prints its top eight next tokens. **Expected:** generic sentence-openers, nothing about rivers or money. GPT-2 is not being stupid; it simply cannot see the evidence. Then it scores six complete candidate sentences ("The bank was…", "The road was…", and so on) by total surprise. With the right-hand side included, plausible candidates should rank above implausible ones. Note the bill printed at the end: one forward pass per candidate.

**5b.** BERT is asked to write six tokens after a prompt, filling one `[MASK]` per pass with its top guess, and GPT-2 continues the same prompt greedily. **Expected:** BERT's text is short, repetitive or ungrammatical, often padded with punctuation or very common words, because each guess was made with empty masks to its right and no training at writing. GPT-2's continuation is fluent, if not necessarily sensible. The cell also prints how many token-forwards BERT needed: the whole sequence, six times.

## Task 6 · Supervision and the cache

**6a.** The cell counts graded positions on the 64 sequences from Task 2 under both objectives. **Expected:** a ratio close to ${}1/0.15 = 6.67$ — lesson 5's first reason, counted on real data.

**6b.** GPT-2 generates 256 tokens twice: with its KV cache, and with `use_cache=False`, which forces it to re-read the whole sequence for every new token, as an encoder would have to. **Expected:** the uncached run is clearly slower, several times on a T4. The cell also prints the token-forward counts, which differ by far more than the measured times.

**How to read it:** the measured gap is much smaller than the arithmetic gap because a 124M model on a T4 spends most of each step on fixed overheads (launching hundreds of small kernels), not on arithmetic. Module 05 measures exactly this, and Module 15 removes much of the overhead. Keep the two numbers; they are a preview.

## Check yourself

Answer these from your own printouts, then compare with the reasoning below each.

1. **Which words changed BERT's guess for the "bank" blank, and could GPT-2 have used them?**
   Only the words after the blank differed between the two sentences, so they caused the change. GPT-2 predicting that position sees only "The", so no.

2. **Why is your measured masking split not exactly 15% and 80/10/10?**
   Every choice is an independent random draw; with a few thousand tokens the counts wander by a percent or so. The *expected* fractions are exact.

3. **Why is the "false fact" sentence only slightly worse than the "true fact" one overall?**
   The two differ in one token. That token's surprise is much higher in the false sentence, but it is averaged with every other token's, which are identical. A mean over tokens hides single-token failures.

4. **How many forward passes would it take to rank 1,000 candidate words for a blank with GPT-2, and with BERT?**
   1,000 with GPT-2 (one complete sentence each). One with BERT, which outputs a distribution over its entire vocabulary at the masked position.

5. **Why does the uncached GPT-2 run lose by less than the token-forward ratio predicts?**
   At this size each step's time is dominated by fixed per-step costs, not by the number of tokens processed, so doing more tokens per step is nearly free until the GPU's arithmetic is actually busy.

<KeyNotes>
  1. BERT ranks its whole 30,522-token vocabulary for a `[MASK]` in one pass, using both sides; changing only the words after a blank changes its answer.
  2. Hugging Face's MLM collator chooses about 15% of real tokens and splits them about 80/10/10; the loss is computed only on the chosen positions, including the unchanged ones.
  3. The parameter counts are identities you can assert: 109,482,240 for BERT-Base and 124,439,808 for GPT-2 124M, both rebuilt from their configs.
  4. GPT-2's per-token losses come from one forward pass; their mean is the library's `labels=` loss, its exponential is perplexity, and dividing total bits by UTF-8 bytes gives bits per byte.
  5. A randomly initialised GPT-2 scores close to ln 50,257 = 10.82 nats; the gap to the trained model is what pretraining bought.
  6. GPT-2 cannot use right-hand context for a single prediction; scoring complete candidate sentences works but costs one pass per candidate.
  7. BERT can be forced to write, one mask per pass, but the text is poor and every pass re-reads everything; a decoder with a KV cache does one new token per step.
</KeyNotes>
