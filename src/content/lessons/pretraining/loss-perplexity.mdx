The previous lesson turned raw text into an almost endless supply of exam questions: here is the context, what comes next? The text itself holds the answer key. But an answer key is only half of an exam. You also need a **marking scheme**: one number that says how wrong each answer was.

That number matters more than it sounds. In *How a model learns: loss & gradient descent* (Module 03) you saw that training is nothing but "make the loss smaller, one small step at a time". So the loss *is* the definition of what the model is trying to do. Pick a bad one and billions of dollars of compute go into optimising the wrong thing.

There is a complication. A language model never actually gives one answer. As you saw in *Logits to tokens: sampling*, its output is a probability for **every** token in the vocabulary, all 50,257 of them for GPT-2. So the marking scheme has to grade a *bet spread across the whole vocabulary*, not a single guess.

## The wall: accuracy cannot see confidence

The obvious marking scheme is the one from school: take the model's top token and mark it right or wrong. That is **accuracy**, and it fails in two ways.

**It ignores how close the model came.** Take the context "The cat sat on the ___", where the text says "mat". Model A puts 45% on "floor" and 40% on "mat". Model B puts 80% on "floor" and 1% on "mat". Both have "floor" on top, so both score zero. Yet model A was nearly right and model B was confidently wrong, and A is clearly the better model.

**It gives gradient descent nothing to push on.** Gradient descent needs a slope: if I nudge this weight, does the score get a little better or a little worse? Nudge any weight by a tiny amount and the top token almost never changes, so accuracy does not change either. It is flat almost everywhere and jumps occasionally. A flat surface has slope zero, and slope zero means no update.

There is a third, quieter problem. Language is genuinely uncertain. After "I ate a ___" there are many correct words. A model that honestly spreads its bet across "sandwich", "banana" and "pizza" is behaving *correctly*, but accuracy punishes it every time the text picked a different word from the one on top.

What we need instead is a score that looks only at **how much probability the model put on the true token**, and moves smoothly as that probability moves.

## Surprise: score the bet by −log p

Here is the score every language model is trained on. If the model gave the true token probability $p$, its loss on that token is

$$
\ell = -\ln p
$$

Read it as **surprise**: how surprised the model was to see what actually came next. It has exactly the properties we wanted:

| $p$ (true token) | $-\ln p$ (nats) | $-\log_2 p$ (bits) | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0 | 0 | certain and right: no surprise |
| 0.5 | 0.693 | 1 | a coin flip |
| 0.25 | 1.386 | 2 | one of four |
| 0.1 | 2.303 | 3.32 | one of ten |
| 0.01 | 4.605 | 6.64 | very surprised |
| → 0 | → ∞ | → ∞ | confident and wrong: unbounded |

It only depends on the probability of the true token, so model A above ($p = 0.40$, loss 0.916) scores much better than model B ($p = 0.01$, loss 4.605). It is smooth, so every small change in a weight that raises $p$ a little lowers the loss a little: there is always a slope. And it punishes confident mistakes hardest of all.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: p = 0.25">
  $$
  -\ln 0.25 = 1.386\ \text{nats}, \qquad -\log_2 0.25 = 2\ \text{bits}, \qquad \frac{1}{0.25} = 4
  $$

  Two bits is the information needed to pick one of four equally likely options. So a model that gives the right token 25% is exactly as surprised as someone guessing among four doors. The last number, 4, is the perplexity, which you will meet in a moment.
</Callout>

**Why a logarithm, and not, say, ${}1 - p$?** Because of how the probability of a whole text works. The model predicts tokens one at a time, so the probability it gives a whole sequence is the product of its per-token probabilities (the chain rule of probability):

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_T) = p(x_2 \mid x_1)\, p(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \cdots p(x_T \mid x_{<T})
$$

Products of hundreds of small numbers are awkward: they underflow, and they are not "per token". A logarithm turns the product into a sum, so the total surprise of a text is just the sum of the surprises of its tokens. Every halving of $p$ adds the same fixed amount, $\ln 2 = 0.693$ nats, which is exactly one bit.

**Units.** With the natural log the unit is the **nat**; with $\log_2$ it is the **bit**. They differ by a constant: one nat is ${}1/\ln 2 = 1.443$ bits. PyTorch and every training framework use natural logs, so a loss printed during training is in nats.

## Then derive it properly

### Cross-entropy: the average surprise

Over a corpus of $N$ target tokens, the training loss is the mean surprise:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \ln p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})
$$

where $p_\theta$ is the model with weights $\theta$. This is called the <Term t="cross-entropy">cross-entropy loss</Term>, and also the *negative log-likelihood*. The two names describe the same number.

Where does the name come from? In information theory, the cross-entropy between a true distribution $P$ and a model's distribution $Q$ is

$$
H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x} P(x) \ln Q(x)
$$

For one training example, the "true distribution" is the text: probability 1 on the token that actually came next and 0 on everything else (a *one-hot* vector). Every term of the sum is multiplied by zero except the true token's, so the whole formula collapses to $-\ln Q(\text{true token})$: the surprise. Averaged over the corpus, it is the loss above.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: one sentence">
  The model reads "The cat" and must predict "sat", "on", "the", "mat", giving them probabilities 0.10, 0.50, 0.80 and 0.25.

  $$
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{2.303 + 0.693 + 0.223 + 1.386}{4} = \frac{4.605}{4} = 1.151\ \text{nats per token}
  $$

  In bits: ${}1.151 / 0.693 = 1.661$ bits per token.
</Callout>

### The gradient is "predicted minus true"

One reason cross-entropy is so universal is how simple its slope is. If the model's logits are $z$, the probabilities are $q = \mathrm{softmax}(z)$ and the true token is $y$, then the gradient of the loss with respect to each logit is

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_i} = q_i - [\,i = y\,]
$$

where $[\,i = y\,]$ is 1 for the true token and 0 otherwise. Suppose a three-token vocabulary gives $q = [0.25, 0.60, 0.15]$ and the true token is the first. The gradient is $[-0.75,\ 0.60,\ 0.15]$. Gradient descent moves *against* the gradient, so it pushes the true token's logit **up** by an amount proportional to 0.75 (how far it was from certain), and every other logit **down** in proportion to how much probability it wrongly took. No probability is ever ignored, and the push is gentle when the model was nearly right and strong when it was badly wrong.

### In code

```python
import math, torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

ids = tok(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids           # [1, T]
logits = model(ids).logits                               # [1, T, V]: one distribution per position
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits[0, :-1], ids[0, 1:])        # position t predicts token t+1
print(loss.item(), "nats/token")                          # the mean surprise
print(loss.exp().item(), "perplexity")
# Hugging Face does the same shift for you:  model(ids, labels=ids).loss
```

The `[:-1]` and `[1:]` are the shift-by-one from the previous lesson: the output at position $t$ is graded against the token at $t + 1$.

## Perplexity: the effective number of choices

Losses in nats are hard to feel. So people often report <Term t="perplexity">perplexity</Term>, the exponential of the mean loss:

$$
\mathrm{PPL} = e^{\mathcal{L}}
$$

For the sentence above, $e^{1.151} = 3.162$. Perplexity has a beautiful reading: it is the size of a fair die that would be exactly as surprising. A perplexity of 3.16 means that, on average, the model was as uncertain as someone choosing uniformly among about three tokens.

There is a second way to see the same number. Undo the logarithms and the mean loss becomes a geometric mean of the probabilities:

$$
\mathrm{PPL} = \left(\prod_t p_t\right)^{-1/N} = (0.10 \times 0.50 \times 0.80 \times 0.25)^{-1/4} = 0.01^{-1/4} = 3.162
$$

**Perplexity is one over the typical probability the model gave the truth.**

Two anchors make it easy to read:

- **A model that knows nothing** gives every token ${}1/V$. Its loss is $\ln V$ and its perplexity is exactly $V$. For GPT-2, $\ln 50{,}257 = 10.82$ nats and perplexity 50,257. That is where training starts, and the lab checks that a freshly initialised GPT-2 begins there.
- **A model that is always certain and right** has loss 0 and perplexity 1. That is the unreachable other end.

| Loss (nats/token) | Perplexity |
|---|---|
| 1 | 2.72 |
| 2 | 7.39 |
| 3 | 20.1 |
| 4 | 54.6 |
| 10.82 (uniform over GPT-2’s vocabulary) | 50,257 |

Because perplexity is an exponential, **small changes in loss are big changes in perplexity**. Lowering the loss by 0.1 nats multiplies perplexity by $e^{-0.1} = 0.905$: a 9.5% drop. For real numbers, lesson 4 will show the GPT-2 paper's results on the WikiText-103 benchmark: perplexity 37.50 for the smallest GPT-2 and 17.48 for the largest. In losses that is $\ln 37.50 = 3.624$ against $\ln 17.48 = 2.861$ nats per token.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Perplexities are only comparable on the same text and the same tokenizer">
  A perplexity is an average over *a specific test set* and is counted *per token of a specific tokenizer*. Change the test text and the number changes (news is easier to predict than poetry). Change the tokenizer and it changes again, because "one token" is a different amount of text. Comparing GPT-2's perplexity with Llama 3's directly is meaningless: they do not even agree on what a token is. Bits per byte, below, fixes the second problem.
</Callout>

## Why the loss can never reach zero

Look again at "I ate a ___". Suppose that across all the text ever written, the true next word there is "sandwich" 50% of the time, "banana" 30% and "pizza" 20% (made-up numbers). That uncertainty is a property of the *language*, not of any model. Its <Term t="entropy">entropy</Term> is

$$
H(P) = -(0.5 \ln 0.5 + 0.3 \ln 0.3 + 0.2 \ln 0.2) = 1.030\ \text{nats}
$$

A perfect model that learned exactly these probabilities would still average 1.030 nats of loss here, because the text keeps picking different words. A slightly wrong model, say $Q = (0.6, 0.2, 0.2)$, averages

$$
H(P, Q) = -(0.5 \ln 0.6 + 0.3 \ln 0.2 + 0.2 \ln 0.2) = 1.060\ \text{nats}
$$

The difference, 0.030 nats, is the model's real mistake. This is true in general:

$$
\underbrace{H(P, Q)}_{\text{the loss}} = \underbrace{H(P)}_{\text{language's own uncertainty}} + \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q)}_{\text{the model's error,}\ \ge 0}
$$

Training can only shrink the second term. **The first term is a floor that no model, of any size, can get under.** Nobody knows the floor for real text exactly. Claude Shannon estimated in 1951, from people guessing the next letter of English text, that it is somewhere around 0.6 to 1.3 bits per character. Module 3B's scaling laws model a training run's loss as exactly this: an irreducible floor plus terms that shrink as the model and data grow.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Two kinds of uncertainty">
  When a model is unsure, it is either because *it* does not know enough (that part shrinks with training) or because *the text itself* could go several ways (that part never shrinks). The loss measures the sum. A good model is not one with zero loss; it is one whose remaining loss is almost all the second kind.
</Callout>

### A suspiciously low perplexity is a warning sign

The floor gives you a useful alarm. Suppose a model scores a far lower perplexity on a famous benchmark's test set than on fresh text of the same style written after its training data was collected. The text did not suddenly become more predictable. The likeliest explanation is that **the test set leaked into the training data** and the model has partly memorised it. This is called <Term t="data-contamination">contamination</Term>, and an unexpectedly low perplexity is one of its fingerprints.

The GPT-2 authors worried about exactly this and measured how much of each benchmark's text also appeared in their training data (lesson 4). Module B2 covers contamination in detail; for now, remember that a loss *below* what is plausible is not good news. It usually means the exam answers were in the textbook.

## Bits per byte: comparing different tokenizers

Here is the tokenizer problem in numbers. Two models read the same 16 bytes of text, "the cat sat down":

- **Model A** uses a normal 50K-token vocabulary, so the text is 4 tokens of 4 bytes each. Its loss is 3.0 nats per token, a perplexity of $e^{3.0} = 20.1$.
- **Model B** uses one token per byte, so the text is 16 tokens. Its loss is 0.8 nats per token, a perplexity of $e^{0.8} = 2.23$.

B's perplexity is nine times lower. Is it the better model? Not necessarily: guessing the next *byte* is much easier than guessing the next *word-sized chunk*, so its per-token loss is lower for free. The fair comparison is the total information each model needed for the **same text**, divided by the size of that text in bytes. That is <Term t="bits-per-byte">bits per byte</Term> (BPB):

$$
\text{BPB} = \frac{\text{total loss in nats}}{\ln 2 \times \text{number of UTF-8 bytes}} = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{token}}}{\ln 2} \times \frac{\text{tokens}}{\text{bytes}}
$$

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: A against B">
  Model A: ${}3.0 / 0.6931 = 4.328$ bits per token, times 4 tokens = 17.31 bits for 16 bytes:

  $$
  \text{BPB}_A = 17.31 / 16 = 1.082
  $$

  Model B: ${}0.8 / 0.6931 = 1.154$ bits per token, times 16 tokens = 18.47 bits for 16 bytes:

  $$
  \text{BPB}_B = 18.47 / 16 = 1.154
  $$

  **A needs fewer bits to describe the same text, so A is the better model**, even though its per-token perplexity looked nine times worse.
</Callout>

Bytes are the one unit every model shares, because every tokenizer ultimately turns text into UTF-8 bytes. That is why papers that compare models with different tokenizers, or train byte-level models, report bits per byte (or bits per character on plain-English datasets). The GPT-2 paper reports some of its results this way, on datasets built for character-level models.

```python
n_bytes = len(text.encode("utf-8"))
n_targets = ids.shape[1] - 1                  # predictions made
total_nats = loss.item() * n_targets
bpb = total_nats / math.log(2) / n_bytes
```

<Callout type="real" title="Where you will use these numbers">
  **Perplexity is the cheapest quality check there is.** It needs no labels and runs in minutes, which is why Module 11 uses it as the first smoke test after quantizing a model: if perplexity on held-out text jumps, something broke, before any benchmark moves.

  **The training loss is the number on every pretraining dashboard.** When Module 3B plots loss against model size and data, the vertical axis is exactly this cross-entropy in nats per token.

  **Bits per byte is how to compare across tokenizers** — for example, when choosing between an open model with a 50K vocabulary and one with a 128K vocabulary for your own text.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. A language model outputs a probability for every token; it is graded only on the probability it gave the **true** token.
  2. Accuracy fails: it ignores how close the model came, it is flat almost everywhere so gradient descent gets no slope, and it punishes honest uncertainty.
  3. The loss of one prediction is its **surprise**, $-\ln p$: 0 when certain and right, unbounded when confident and wrong. $p = 0.25$ gives 1.386 nats = 2 bits.
  4. Averaged over tokens this is the **cross-entropy** loss. Its gradient with respect to the logits is simply predicted minus true, $q - \text{onehot}(y)$.
  5. **Perplexity** $= e^{\text{mean loss}}$: the effective number of equally likely choices, and one over the geometric-mean probability of the truth. A uniform guess over $V$ tokens has loss $\ln V$ and perplexity $V$: 10.82 nats and 50,257 for GPT-2.
  6. The loss cannot reach zero: cross-entropy = the text's own entropy + the model's error. Training shrinks only the error.
  7. An unusually low perplexity on a benchmark is a sign the benchmark leaked into the training data (**contamination**).
  8. Per-token numbers depend on the tokenizer. **Bits per byte** $= \mathcal{L}/\ln 2 \times$ tokens/bytes compares any two models on the same text.
</KeyNotes>
