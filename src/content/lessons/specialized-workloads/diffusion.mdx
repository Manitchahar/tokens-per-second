Every workload so far in this course has had the same skeleton: read the weights, produce one token, repeat. The KV cache made that efficient by never recomputing. Everything in Modules 05 through 20 is a refinement of that loop.

Diffusion throws the loop away.

There is no token. There is no KV cache, because there is nothing to cache — every one of the 20 to 50 steps re-reads every weight and recomputes everything. And instead of producing something small, each step processes the *entire image*.

The result is a workload that is **compute-bound where LLM decode is memory-bound.** That single fact inverts most of the instincts this course has built.

<Callout type="mentor" title="The one-sentence difference">
  LLM decode asks "how fast can I read the weights?" Diffusion asks "how fast can I multiply?" Same GPU, opposite constraint, opposite optimisation priorities, opposite buying criteria.
</Callout>

## The latent, and why 512×512 is really 64×64

A diffusion U-Net never sees pixels. It sees a **latent**: the image downsampled by 8 and given 4 channels.

$$
512 \div 8 = 64 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{latent shape} = 4 \times 64 \times 64 = \boxed{16{,}384 \text{ values}}
$$

Compare the image itself: 3 × 512 × 512 = 786,432 values. The latent is **48× smaller**. Every denoising step runs over those 16,384 values; the full-resolution pixels only exist at the very end, when a VAE decoder expands the final latent back into an image.

So diffusion *is* token-like in one respect: it has a natural unit of work. One latent position is roughly one token. At 16,384 units per image, a single image is a long sequence — but processed all at once, in parallel, every step.

## Why it is compute-bound

This is the claim that drives everything else, so let me show it rather than assert it.

Take Stable Diffusion 1.5 at 512×512 on an A100 (2.0 TB/s peak bandwidth). One denoising step touches roughly every weight plus the live activations:

$$
\text{bytes per step} \approx \underbrace{0.86\text{e}9 \times 2}_{\text{weights}} + \underbrace{320 \times 512 \times 512 \times 2 \times 6}_{\text{widest activations}} \approx \boxed{2.7\text{ GB}}
$$

Published A100 timings for SD 1.5 with classifier-free guidance put a 20-step 512×512 image at about **1.2 seconds**, so roughly 55 ms per step. How long would those 2.7 GB take to move at *full* bandwidth?

$$
\frac{2.7\text{ GB}}{2.0\text{ TB/s}} = \boxed{1.35\text{ ms}}
$$

Compare with the actual 55 ms:

$$
\text{bandwidth utilisation} = \frac{1.35}{55} = \boxed{2.5\%}
$$

The step spends 55 milliseconds moving bytes that would take 1.35 milliseconds at full speed. It is nowhere near memory-bound — it is deeply compute-bound.

| workload | bandwidth utilisation | bound by |
|---|---|---|
| LLM decode, batch 1 | ~20–35% | memory |
| LLM decode, batch 32+ | 60–80% | memory |
| **diffusion denoising step** | **~2%** | **compute** |

<Callout type="math" title="Why this inverts your buying criteria">
  A memory-bound workload rewards **memory bandwidth**. That is why HBM has become the scarce resource in LLM serving, why GQA and KV quantization (Modules 03, 11) matter so much, and why the decode step is a bandwidth problem above all else.

  A compute-bound workload rewards **FLOP/s and tensor-core throughput**, and is largely indifferent to memory bandwidth. So for a diffusion workload, a card with more compute and less bandwidth is the better card — the opposite of the LLM-serving conclusion.

  This is Module 12's roofline model doing exactly what it is for: telling you *which* of the two limits a workload is against, and therefore which knob to turn.
</Callout>

## The memory story: activations, not weights

Diffusion's memory is unusual, and it is the part that catches people out. At 512×512, the U-Net's highest-resolution blocks carry 320 channels, so a single feature-map tensor is:

$$
320 \times 512 \times 512 \times 2\text{ B} = \boxed{168\text{ MB}}
$$

And several of them are live at once — roughly six tensors across the widest stage of the forward pass:

$$
168 \text{ MB} \times 6 \approx \boxed{1.0\text{ GB per image}}
$$

Compare the weights: 0.86B U-Net parameters plus VAE and text encoder, about **2.1 GB total**, shared across every image in the batch.

$$
\underbrace{2.1\text{ GB}}_{\text{weights, once}} \quad\text{vs}\quad \underbrace{1.0\text{ GB} \times \text{batch}}_{\text{activations, per image}}
$$

**Activations overtake the weights at batch 2.** For an 8B LLM the weights dominate until batch 20 or so; for a diffusion model the crossover is immediate.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="This is why diffusion OOMs at batch 2">
  People arrive from LLM serving expecting a large batch to fit comfortably, and SD 1.5 OOMs on an A100 at batch 4 while a 7B LLM happily runs 128. Nothing is wrong — the weight-to-activation ratio is simply reversed.

  On an A100 (80 GB) with 2.1 GB of weights, the activations leave 78 GB, so about 77 images fit by this estimate. In practice you get far fewer, because real U-Nets hold more than six live tensors and attention at the top level needs a workspace. **Treat any activation estimate as a lower bound.**
</Callout>

Two mitigations, both standard practice:

- **Attention slicing / tiling.** Break the self-attention at high resolution into chunks so it never materialises the full 16,384 × 16,384 attention matrix. Costs some speed, saves a great deal of memory.
- **Lower resolution or fewer channels.** 512×512 → 256×256 quarters the activations and the FLOPs together.

## The latency levers

Four, and only one is free.

**Fewer steps.** Latency is *exactly linear* in step count. SD 1.5 at 20 steps takes 1.2 s; at 10 steps, 0.6 s. But the image quality curve flattens, and below roughly 15 steps the images visibly degrade.

**Distilled few-step models.** SDXL-Turbo is the same 2.6B U-Net trained to work in 4 steps instead of 30. That is a **5.6× latency win** and a genuinely different image — but it is a training-time decision, and it is the single largest lever available.

| model | steps | seconds/image on A100 | relative |
|---|---|---|---|
| SDXL base | 30 | 4.5 | 1.0× |
| SDXL-Turbo | 4 | 0.8 | **0.18×** |
| SD 1.5 | 20 | 1.2 | 0.27× |
| SD 1.5 at 10 steps | 10 | 0.6 | 0.13× |

**Classifier-free guidance.** This is not a lever for saving time — it is a cost. Guidance runs the U-Net twice per step, once on the prompt and once on an empty prompt, then combines them. Published timings above already include it. **It is a 2× tax on every step**, and it is what makes images follow the prompt.

**Feature caching.** There is no prefix cache, but there is an analogue: later denoising steps change the latent only slightly, so shallow U-Net features barely move and can be reused.

| cache mode | step compute retained | quality impact |
|---|---|---|
| none | 100% | reference |
| cumulative | ~35% | small |
| residual | ~15% | very small |

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why feature caching is not free even though it looks like prefix caching">
  In an LLM, the KV for a prefix is *identical* on the next request, so reusing it is exact. In diffusion, the features depend on the latent, and the latent changes every step. Caching them is an approximation that gets worse as the step index grows.

  So there is no correctness guarantee — unlike prefix caching, where a hit is bit-exact. Caching buys you speed by accepting a small quality cost, and the honest framing is that it is a lossy optimisation, not a free one.
</Callout>

## Batching, which behaves differently than you expect

Since diffusion is compute-bound, batching should behave the way prefill behaves: nearly free. And it is — up to the activation limit.

On an A100 with SD 1.5:

| batch | seconds per image | images/second |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.2 | 0.83 |
| 4 | 1.2 | 3.33 |
| 8 | 1.2 | 6.67 |

Per-image latency is unchanged, because the extra images ride along on spare compute. **This is the same free-lunch structure as prefill batching** — and it is why diffusion throughput scales so cleanly with batch, until activations stop fitting.

<Callout type="real" title="What differs from LLM serving, concretely">
  In LLM serving, batch size trades latency against throughput and you have to pick a point on the curve (Module 06). In diffusion, latency per image is flat in batch, so there is no such trade-off — **you should always run the largest batch that fits**, because there is no latency cost.

  The constraint flips sides entirely: in LLM serving, memory limits batch; in diffusion, activations limit batch far earlier than weights do. And in both cases there is no KV cache to manage, which also means none of Module 08's paging machinery applies.
</Callout>

## Sizing a diffusion service

| decision | guidance |
|---|---|
| model | smaller U-Net is a bigger win than more steps |
| steps | the largest count that meets your latency SLO; few-step models win outright |
| resolution | FLOPs and activations both scale with pixels — 1024² costs ~4× 512² |
| batch | as large as activations allow; per-image latency is flat |
| attention | slicing/tiling to fit; costs some speed |
| guidance | assume 2×; it is not optional in practice |
