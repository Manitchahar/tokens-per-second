You have finished. Twenty-eight modules, a mini engine, a benchmark harness, a capstone design. Now the last practical thing: **being able to talk about it**, convincingly, to someone deciding whether to hire you.

Inference interviews are not memory tests. Nobody asks you to recite a config flag. They ask you to **derive an answer and defend it** — how big is the KV cache, why is this workload memory-bound, what would you do about it. If you can do that for the systems you have built, you can do it for a system you have not seen yet.

<Callout type="mentor" title="The single thing interviewers are testing">
  Can you reason from first principles to a defensible answer about a system you have never seen? The specific repo, framework or GPU does not matter. The habit of deriving the number, stating the assumption, and saying what would change your mind is the whole skill.
</Callout>

## The map: six kinds of question

Inference interviews cluster into a small number of kinds. Each kind has an answer you already own, in an earlier module. Recognize the kind and you know what to reach for.

<Scrolly>
  <InterviewPrep client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. Know the map">
    Six kinds cover almost everything: **roofline and bottlenecks** (is this memory- or compute-bound?), **memory and KV math** (does it fit, and how many users), **batching and scheduling** (TTFT versus throughput), **parallelism and scaling** (what happens when one GPU is not enough), **quantization and kernels** (what do you trade for speed), and **cost and capacity** (what does this cost per million tokens).

    Each maps to a module you finished. When you hear a question, the first move is to place it: *this is a memory-budget question; the answer comes from the KV arithmetic in Module 5.*
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. The whiteboard questions">
    Most whiteboard questions are **arithmetic with a story attached**. "Is this workload memory-bound?" is a roofline calculation. "How many concurrent users fit on an A100?" is the KV memory math from Module 5. "What does this cost per million tokens?" is Module 25. "How many requests are in flight?" is Little's law from Module 6.

    The story matters as much as the arithmetic. Say what you assume (batch size, context length, precision), do the arithmetic, then state the answer *as a function of those assumptions*. An interviewer wants to see your reasoning, so narrate it.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Designing a system, live">
    The design question is where this whole course pays off, and where the capstone is your rehearsal. The structure that works: **start from the SLO**, derive the memory budget, then choose the engine, precision and topology to satisfy both — and say what you rejected and why.

    Worked skeleton for "design a system serving Llama-3.1-8B to 500 concurrent users under a 1-second TTFT SLO":
    1. Weights are 16 GB in BF16, so it fits one 80 GB A100 with room for KV (Module 5).
    2. At 2,048-token contexts and 128 KB per token, each stream costs about 0.26 GB, so one GPU holds roughly 220 streams (Module 5).
    3. 500 concurrent needs 3 GPUs with headroom, at 70% utilization (Module 23).
    4. Engine: vLLM with continuous batching (Module 7); enable prefix caching if traffic shares prefixes (Module 10).
    5. Cost at goodput, not raw throughput (Module 25), and here's the measurement that would change the plan.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. The debugging story">
    Almost everyone gets a debugging or "walk me through a bottleneck" question, and it is a **credibility** question: they want to see how you isolate a problem under time pressure, not whether you have ever hit this exact bug.

    Your answer should be the profiling workflow from Module 12: reproduce, measure the two speed limits, find where the time actually goes, form one hypothesis, test it, and report the number. Say what you would expect *before* you measure — that is what separates debugging from guessing. One real bottleneck you found and fixed, told cleanly, beats five you did not.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Numbers under pressure">
    Know a small set of numbers **cold**, because they come up in almost every interview and you should not need to look them up. Not to recite — to *derive on the spot* from the formulas you know:

    - **KV bytes per token** for Llama-3.1-8B: 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 128 KB.
    - **Decode step time**, 8B on an H100 at batch 1: 16 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 5 ms (Module 5).
    - **Cost per million tokens**: at \$2.50/hour and ~8,000 tokens/s, about \$0.09 (Module 25).
    - **Little's law**: 4,000 users each taking 8 seconds ⇒ about 500 requests in flight (Module 6).

    If you forget a number, **derive it** on the whiteboard. Watching you reconstruct it from the model shape is the point.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Where to go next">
    Four directions, and they suit different people. **Systems and serving**: SLOs, capacity, cost, reliability — you decide what runs and what it costs. **Kernels and runtime**: bandwidth-bound fusion, attention kernels, the compiler — you live in the GPU's inner loop. **Product and applied**: tuning for a workload, structured output, agents, RL rollouts. **Research**: reproduce, extend, publish.

    Pick the one whose *daily work* you enjoy, not the one with the best title. You are infinitely better at something you like doing, and this module — plus a capstone, a public write-up, or an open-source PR — is the strongest possible way into whichever it is.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## How to answer, whatever they ask

Under interview pressure, people make one of two mistakes: they guess a number and defend it, or they freeze and say "I don't know." There is a third path that is always available and always works: **state what you know, state what you'd need, and derive what you can.**

> *"I'm not certain of that constant off the top of my head. Let me derive the KV budget instead: for Llama-3.1-8B, 2 (K and V) × 32 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 dim × 2 bytes gives 128 KB per token, so at a 2,048-token context each concurrent stream costs about 0.26 GB."*

That answer is better than a correct number you pulled from nowhere, because it shows the interviewer exactly how you think — and thinking is what they are buying.

<Callout type="real" title="The two-sentence project story">
  Every candidate has projects. The ones who stand out can say, in two sentences: **what the system was, and what you changed that moved a number.** *"I built a serving stack for a chat product; we were memory-bound in decode, so I added prefix caching for our shared system prompt, which cut goodput-per-dollar by 30%."* That is a project. Be ready to say yours, with the number.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Three interview traps">
  1. **Reciting instead of deriving.** "Decode is memory-bound" earns nothing. "Decode reads 16 GB of weights to produce one token, which is 5 ms at 3.35 TB/s while the math takes under 1 ms, so it is memory-bound" earns the job.
  2. **Optimizing the wrong number.** Do not lead with tokens/s. Lead with goodput against the SLO; that is how real systems are judged (Module 6, Module 25).
  3. **Defending a decision you cannot explain.** If you cannot say why you chose vLLM over SGLang in one sentence, you did not choose it — you defaulted. Interviews probe that exact gap.
</Callout>

## The long game

This course gave you a foundation and a portfolio of projects. The rest is compounding: pick a direction, build one real thing in it, and write about what you learned. The engineers who get interesting work are rarely the ones with the most knowledge; they are the ones who can **derive, measure, and defend** — the exact skill this course taught.

Keep your harness, your mini-engine, your capstone design and your verdict sheets. They are not homework; they are the evidence you point at when someone asks what you can do.

<Callout type="recap" title="You are ready">
  Across 28 modules you learned: where tokens come from, how memory and compute trade off, how to serve many users from one GPU, how to scale out, how to run it in production, how to price it, and how to design and defend a whole system from first principles. That is not a course. That is a job.
</Callout>
