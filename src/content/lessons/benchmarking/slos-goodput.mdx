Everything so far has been about measuring. This lesson is about the moment you stop measuring and start **promising** — and about the one number that survives contact with that promise.

The trap is obvious once you see it: a team is judged on tokens per second, so they maximize tokens per second. The system gets faster and faster while getting worse and worse to use.

## An SLO is a contract, not a hope

A **service-level objective** is a measurable promise about your service. It has four parts, and all four are required:

1. **The metric** — TTFT, TPOT, end-to-end.
2. **The percentile** — p50, p95, p99.
3. **The threshold** — how many milliseconds.
4. **The window** — over what period it must hold.

> p95 TTFT under 600 ms and p95 TPOT under 40 ms, over any rolling 5-minute window.

Any missing part makes the objective unenforceable. Without a percentile it is ambiguous; without a window you can fail on one bad afternoon and be technically correct.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Where the number should come from">
  Not from the hardware. From **users**. What is the longest a person will wait for a first token before they conclude the product is broken?

  Find it by looking at where abandonment happens in your existing product, or by asking. Then pick a percentile that matches the population you are willing to disappoint — usually p95 or p99 for interactive work. Then choose a threshold slightly *inside* that, so you have room.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The two-sided mistake">
  Set the SLO too loose and you ship something nobody notices is bad. Set it too tight and your team spends every sprint chasing a number that users never asked for, and burns money on capacity to hit it.

  An SLO is a **product decision with an engineering consequence**. The person who sets it owns the bill that follows.
</Callout>

## Goodput: the only number that counts

**Goodput** is throughput restricted to the work that met its SLO:

$$
\text{goodput} = \text{throughput} \times \Pr(\text{request meets its SLO})
$$

That is the entire definition. Everything interesting is in the second factor.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked example">
  At 95% of capacity your system produces **5,089 tokens/s**. But only **17%** of requests finish inside the SLO, because the rest are queuing.

  $$
  \text{goodput} = 5{,}089 \times 0.17 \approx 840\ \text{tokens/s}
  $$

  **83% of the GPU's output is work that violates your own promise.** You are paying full price for it. And the dashboard showing "5,089 tokens/s" looks like your best day ever.
</Callout>

At 60% load the same system produces about 4,100 tokens/s and almost all requests meet the SLO, so goodput is nearly 4,100. **The busier system delivers roughly a fifth of the useful work** and costs more to run.

## Why the tail ruins goodput

Goodput inherits the tail problem from the last lesson, and it is worse than it looks.

Suppose your SLO is p95 TTFT. By definition **5% of requests are allowed to miss it**. That is a permanent 5% tax on goodput, before anything else goes wrong. If you want half your throughput to be good, you must have a pass rate around 50% — which in the visual means operating at roughly 80% load, with a much worse median to show for it.

<Callout type="math" title="The percentile tax">
  Let the SLO be at percentile $q$ (as a fraction, e.g. 0.95). At best, the pass rate is $q$, so:

  $$
  \text{goodput}_{\max} = q \times \text{throughput}_{\text{at that load}}
  $$

  A **stricter** SLO (higher $q$) allows a higher pass rate but forces a lower load. A **looser** SLO (lower $q$) allows a higher load but caps your pass rate. There is no percentile that is purely better — you are trading one against the other, and the optimum depends on your latency curve.

  This is why the p95-versus-p99 decision is an economic one, not a technical one.
</Callout>

## Choosing the operating point

The practical method, which *Break one GPU* turns into a full sweep:

1. **Define the SLO** (metric, percentile, threshold, window).
2. **Sweep offered load** from idle to overload, in steps.
3. **At each step, record throughput, the SLO pass rate, goodput, and the tail percentiles.**
4. **Find the peak of goodput.** That is your operating point.
5. **Verify it has headroom** — sit at perhaps 80% of the peak load, so bursts and deploys do not push you over.
6. **Re-run whenever anything changes**: a new model, a quantization, an engine upgrade, a traffic mix shift.

<Callout type="real" title="How this looks in production">
  In practice you enforce the operating point with **admission control and load shedding**, not by hoping:

  - A **maximum queue depth** or **maximum concurrency**, so you refuse work rather than let it wait. This is what keeps the tail flat when traffic spikes.
  - **Autoscaling on p95 latency or queue depth**, since (as the queueing math showed) those lead utilization.
  - **A load-shedding policy** at the edge: past a threshold, fail fast with a retryable error. A fast error beats a slow success — it protects goodput for everyone else.

  At the time of writing, the major serving engines expose concurrency and token-budget limits that, tuned this way, are how you hold an SLO. Module 23 covers the surrounding orchestration.
</Callout>

## Goodput per dollar

Once you have an operating point, the cost question follows immediately. Goodput per dollar is the metric that decides whether your service is viable:

$$
\text{goodput per dollar} = \frac{\text{goodput (tokens/s)} \times 3600}{\text{GPU dollars per hour}}
$$

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: two configurations, same GPU">
  One H100 rented at 2.50 dollars per hour, running Llama-3.1-8B.

  - **At 60% load:** ~4,100 tok/s goodput, so about 4,100 × 3,600 = 14.8M tokens per hour. Goodput per dollar = 14.8M ÷ 2.50 = **5.9M good tokens per dollar**.
  - **At 95% load:** 5,089 tok/s throughput, but only 840 tok/s goodput, so about 3.0M tokens per hour → **1.2M good tokens per dollar**.
</Callout>

## The three numbers to keep

<Callout type="recap" title="If you remember nothing else from this module">
  1. **Throughput** — what the GPU produced. Meaningless alone.
  2. **Goodput** — what arrived inside the promise. This is the deliverable.
  3. **Goodput per dollar** — whether the business works.

  All three are reported as distributions, over a stated workload, on stated hardware, at a stated time. A number without those four things is not a result.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. An **SLO** names a metric, a percentile, a threshold and a window. Missing any one makes it unenforceable.
  2. Choose the SLO from **user behaviour**, not from what the hardware can currently do, and from the users you are willing to disappoint.
  3. **Goodput = throughput × the fraction of requests that met their SLO.** The second factor is where all the difficulty lives.
  4. **Goodput is not monotonic in load.** It peaks below saturation — around 55–65% of capacity in typical configurations — and falls beyond it. At 95% load in the worked example, 83% of produced tokens violated the SLO: full price for unusable work.
  5. An SLO at percentile $q$ caps your pass rate at $q$, so a p95 objective taxes goodput by 5% before any real failure.
  6. Find the operating point by **sweeping load and measuring goodput**, then sit below the peak with headroom for bursts.
  7. Enforce it with admission control, a concurrency cap and autoscaling on latency or queue depth — never on utilization.
  8. Report **goodput per dollar**. Between two configurations it can differ by 5× while throughput differs by less than 20%.
</KeyNotes>
