You now know how work is placed on the GPU. The next question is the one that decides almost every kernel's runtime: **when a thread asks for a number, how many bytes actually cross the memory bus?**

The answer is not "the number of bytes you wanted". It is "every 32-byte sector that any lane in the warp touched". Get that wrong and a kernel can be thirty times slower than its mathematics deserves, with no bug anywhere.

## The ladder again, with the numbers that matter

Module 02 gave you the hierarchy. What it did not give you was the unit of transfer, and that unit is the whole lesson.

| Level | Size | Latency | Transfer unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Registers | 256 KB per SM | ~1 cycle | per thread, no transfer at all |
| Shared memory | 256 KB per SM (carved from L1) | ~30 cycles | 32 banks, 4 bytes each |
| L1 / L2 | 256 KB per SM / 50 MB shared | ~30 cycles / ~200 ns | 128-byte cache lines |
| HBM | 80 GB | ~600–700 ns | **32-byte sectors** |

HBM is the only level where the granularity really bites, and it is the level everything in an LLM lives on.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The one idea of this lesson">
  A load instruction does not fetch the bytes you asked for. It fetches every 32-byte <Term t="sector">sector</Term> that any of its 32 lanes touched, and it hands all of them to L2 whether you wanted them or not. **Achieved bandwidth counts the sectors, not your data.** A kernel's memory efficiency is the ratio between the two, and in inference the ratio is often 1.0 or 0.03.
</Callout>

## One warp, one load, 32 addresses

A warp's load instruction names 32 addresses, one per lane. The memory system collects them and gathers every 32-byte sector containing any of them. Thirty-two sectors is the worst case; the hardware will happily do it.

When the 32 lanes read consecutive addresses the access is <Term t="coalescing">coalesced</Term> — the term you will see in every profiler report for the rest of this module.

<Scrolly>
  <Coalesce client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. One instruction, 32 lanes">
    Lane *i* wants the element at index *i* of a tensor. That looks like 32 separate reads, but it is **one** instruction and the hardware fetches for all 32 at once.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. The unit is the 32-byte sector">
    HBM does not deliver bytes or cache lines at this level; it delivers **32-byte sectors**, the smallest unit it can transfer. A warp's load is answered by fetching every sector any lane touched.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Coalesced: consecutive lanes">
    If lane *i* reads element *i*, the 32 BF16 values occupy 64 contiguous bytes — **two sectors**. Sixty-four bytes moved, sixty-four bytes wanted. The label is *coalesced*, and it is what every well-written kernel looks like.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Strided: the same work, 16× the bytes">
    If lane *i* instead reads element 8*i*, the 32 addresses span 512 bytes and touch **16 sectors**. You move 512 bytes to use 64. A perfectly correct kernel, sixteen times slower on the memory system, with no error message anywhere.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. What that costs in a real model">
    One row of an Llama-3.1-8B weight matrix is 4,096 BF16 values, 8 KiB ideal. Read it with stride 8 and you fetch 128 KiB to use 8 — a **16× traffic amplification** that applies to every weight, every layer, every step.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. The fix is layout, not luck">
    Almost every coalescing problem is solved by making the innermost index consecutive: transpose the activation once, store a weight transposed, or tile through shared memory. It is a data-layout decision, made once, repaid every step.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. Bank conflicts are the small version">
    Shared memory has no sectors but it has **32 banks**. Two lanes reading the same bank in the same cycle serialize into two cycles. Padding a shared-memory row stride by one element removes the conflict for free.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. The rule">
    Achieved bandwidth counts bytes you wanted. Every extra sector is HBM you spent to throw away. **Your arithmetic intensity, from Module 02, is measured against the sectors — not the useful bytes.**
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Compute the waste, then the bandwidth

The formula is short enough to do in your head. For a warp-level access where each lane loads $v$ contiguous elements and the lanes are $s$ elements apart:

$$
\text{bytes per lane} = v \cdot b, \qquad
\text{ideal traffic} = 32 v b
$$

$$
\text{sectors touched} = 32 \cdot \left\lceil \frac{vb}{32} \right\rceil \cdot \min(s, 32),
\qquad
\text{actual traffic} = \text{sectors} \times 32
$$

$$
\text{waste factor} = \frac{\text{actual traffic}}{\text{ideal traffic}}
$$

<Callout type="math" title="Three patterns, three answers (BF16, one element per lane)">
  | Pattern | Addresses | Sectors | Ideal | Actual | Waste |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Contiguous | `x[i]` | 2 | 64 B | 64 B | **1×** |
  | Stride 2 | `x[2i]` | 4 | 64 B | 128 B | **2×** |
  | Stride 8 | `x[8i]` | 16 | 64 B | 512 B | **8×** |
  | Stride 16 | `x[16i]` | 16 | 64 B | 512 B | **8×** (sector-capped) |
  | Column of a row-major matrix | `x[i][j]` | 32 | 64 B | 1,024 B | **16×** |

  Note the cap at stride 16: once every lane is in its own sector, extra stride costs nothing more, because the sectors were already being fetched. **The damage is done by the first factor of 16, and it saturates.** This is why "make it contiguous" is the fix and "make the stride a power of two" is not.
</Callout>

Now connect that to speed. If your kernel's ideal traffic is $B_{\text{ideal}}$ and it moves $w \times B_{\text{ideal}}$ in $t$ seconds, then:

$$
\text{achieved bandwidth} = \frac{w \cdot B_{\text{ideal}}}{t}
$$

Compare it to the machine's peak. That ratio is your <Term t="achieved-bandwidth">memory bandwidth utilization</Term>, and Nsight Compute reports it directly. Anything under about 60% on a memory-bound kernel means you are leaving bandwidth on the floor.

<Callout type="math" title="A decode step, measured and explained">
  Llama-3.1-8B in BF16, batch 32, 8k context, on an H100 (3.35 TB/s):

  $$
  \text{weights} = 8.03\times 10^9 \times 2 = 16.1\ \text{GB}, \qquad
  \text{KV} = 32 \times 8192 \times 131{,}072 = 34.4\ \text{GB}
  $$

  So the step must read 50.5 GB. At full bandwidth that is 50.5 ÷ 3.35 = 15.1 ms. A real engine step takes about 20 ms, so it is running at roughly 15.1 ÷ 20 = 76% of peak — a well-tuned number.

  Now suppose one weight load is strided by 8 and amplifies the 16.1 GB of weights to 128 GB. The floor becomes $(128 + 34.4)/3.35 = 48$ ms: a 3.2× regression from a data layout, with the same FLOPs, the same model and the same GPU.
</Callout>

## Traffic you did not ask for: partial-line and write effects

Two more sources of wasted traffic, both common in inference:

**Partial cache lines.** A 128-byte L2 line is fetched whole. If your warp's 32 lanes together touch only 32 bytes of it, the other 96 are fetched anyway. This is why tensors whose leading dimension is not a multiple of 64 elements (128 bytes / 2 bytes per BF16) perform worse than round numbers.

**Read-for-ownership on writes.** Writing to a cache line the kernel does not fully own can cost a read of the line first. In practice this is why writing a contiguous output tensor is cheaper than writing one element at a time into a strided slice.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Two tensor facts that quietly cost you bandwidth">
  **Non-contiguous tensors.** `q.transpose(1, 2)` or `x[:, ::2]` gives you a view with a stride. Reading it directly is the stride-8 case above, in disguise. Call `.contiguous()` once, or make sure the kernel you reach for handles the stride.

  **KV cache reads are the biggest single stream.** From Module 05, decode reads the entire KV cache every step: 128 KiB per token for Llama-8B. At 8k context and batch 32 that is 34 GB per step, dwarfing the weights. This is why **PagedAttention's block table lookups** (Module 08) and **KV cache quantization** (Module 11) are bandwidth plays, not memory-capacity plays — they exist to reduce this one stream.
</Callout>

## Shared memory and bank conflicts, priced

Shared memory is 256 KB per SM, organized as **32 banks** of 4 bytes. A warp's shared-memory access is served one cycle per bank: if 32 lanes hit 32 distinct banks, one cycle. If two lanes hit the same bank, two cycles. This is a <Term t="bank-conflict">bank conflict</Term>.

The classic conflict: reading a `float tile[32][32]` column-wise, where column *j* is at offset `j*32`. Lane *i* reads `tile[i][j]`, so the addresses are 128 bytes apart — every lane in the same bank. 32-way conflict, 32 cycles instead of 1.

The fix is one character:

```python
tile = torch.empty(32, 33, device='cuda')   # stride 33, not 32
tile.copy_(from_hbm)                       # each row starts in a different bank
```

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why tiling is the whole game, restated in bytes">
  A naive matrix multiply reads each weight from HBM once per output row: a 4096×4096 matrix times 4096 rows moves 4096 × 33.5 MB — 137 GB for one matrix multiply. Tiling it so each block loads a $T \times T$ block of the weight into shared memory and reuses it $T$ times cuts that traffic by a factor of $T$.

  That is the same statement as "high arithmetic intensity" from Module 02, and the same statement as "move each byte from HBM once" from Module 13's Triton kernels. Coalescing gets the bytes down to the right place; **tiling keeps them there and reads them many times.**
</Callout>

## Reading achieved bandwidth out of a trace

The habit worth building: any time you see a kernel's duration in a profile, immediately compute what bandwidth it *must* have used and compare to peak.

```python
bytes_moved = (weights_bytes + kv_bytes + activations_bytes)
achieved_gbs = bytes_moved / duration_seconds / 1e9
utilization = achieved_gbs / (gpu_bw_bytes / 1e9)
```

For the decode step above: 50.5 GB in 20 ms is 2.5 TB/s, which is 76% of the H100's 3.35 TB/s. Good. If instead the same step took 60 ms, you would be at 25% — and the first thing to check is not "which kernel is slow" but "did a load pattern change", because a drop from 76% to 25% is almost never arithmetic.

<KeyNotes>
  1. The unit of transfer from HBM is the **32-byte sector**, not the byte and not your data. A load fetches every sector any of the 32 lanes touched.
  2. Consecutive lanes reading consecutive elements is **coalesced**: 32 BF16 = 64 B = 2 sectors, nothing wasted. This is the target for every load in every kernel.
  3. Stride 8 on BF16 costs 8× the traffic; a stride beyond 16 costs no more, because the sectors were already being fetched. The damage saturates at 16×.
  4. Waste factor = actual traffic ÷ ideal traffic. Multiply it by your byte count and you have the kernel's true floor; that is what the roofline must be drawn against.
  5. Achieved bandwidth = (traffic including waste) ÷ time. Compare it to peak: under ~60% on a memory-bound kernel means layout, not math.
  6. Non-contiguous tensors (`transpose`, `[::2]` slices) are the stride problem wearing a disguise. Call `.contiguous()` or handle the stride.
  7. KV cache reads dominate decode traffic — 34 GB per step versus 16 GB of weights for Llama-8B at batch 32 and 8k context. That is why PagedAttention and KV quantization are bandwidth wins.
  8. Shared memory has 32 banks; a column of a `[32][32]` tile is a 32-way conflict. Pad the row stride to 33 and it is free.
</KeyNotes>
