A profiler trace is a stack of colored bars, and each bar is one **kernel launch**: a rectangle that says "on this GPU, this function ran from here to here". Once you know that a kernel is a grid of blocks of warps of threads, the rectangle stops being a mystery and starts being a machine you can reason about.

This lesson is that vocabulary. Module 02 gave you the two speed limits and the die. Now we look at *how work is actually placed onto the arithmetic units*, because that is what decides whether your kernel reaches the bandwidth its hardware advertises.

## One launch, four layers

When you write `y = torch.relu(x)` in PyTorch, a CUDA kernel is launched with three numbers: how many blocks, how many threads per block, and how much memory each thread gets. The GPU then does the following, in order, and always in this order:

1. **Threads.** The finest unit. Each thread has its own program counter and its own registers. Your code's `for` loop index, your `if`, your local variable — all per-thread.
2. **Warps.** The hardware does not schedule threads, it schedules <Term t="warp">warps</Term>. On every current NVIDIA GPU a warp is exactly **32 threads** that issue the same instruction together. All 32 lanes are computing, or none are: there is no partial warp.
3. **Blocks.** Warps are bundled into <Term t="thread-block">thread blocks</Term> of 32, 64, 128, 256, 512 or 1024 threads. A block runs on **one** SM. It cannot straddle two, and that is what makes shared memory possible at all: every thread in a block can address the same on-chip scratchpad.
4. **Grid.** The whole launch. Potentially hundreds of thousands of blocks — a <Term t="grid">grid</Term>, all independent, with no ordering guarantee between them.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The one sentence that makes the rest of this lesson make sense">
  The GPU schedules **blocks**, and it can only run as many blocks at once as it has free slots on its SMs. Everything about occupancy and about the "tail" of a kernel follows from that one fact.
</Callout>

  <WarpGrid client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. You pick the number of threads">
    A typical elementwise kernel launches **one thread per element**. For a `[32, 512, 4096]` activation that is 67 million threads. You are not naming SMs; you are naming a *quantity of work*, and the hardware decides how to spread it.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. Threads are bundled into warps of 32">
    The scheduler hands out **warps**, not threads. A warp is 32 lanes of the arithmetic unit moving in lockstep on one instruction. This is why a branch that some threads take and others skip costs *both* branches — the warp cannot split.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Warps are bundled into blocks">
    A block of 256 threads is 8 warps. It is scheduled onto **one** SM and stays there until it finishes. Two blocks cannot share an SM's shared memory, which is exactly why shared-memory tiling works inside a block and nowhere else.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Blocks are the unit the grid scheduler sees">
    With 256 threads per block and 67 million elements, the grid is about 262,000 blocks. The GPU's grid scheduler drops blocks onto SMs as slots free. The blocks are independent — that is the entire parallelism model.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Only some blocks are resident at once">
    Each SM can host only a limited number of blocks, set by how many **registers** and how much **shared memory** each block wants. On an H100 with 256-thread blocks you can usually fit around 8 at once, so 132 SMs hold roughly 1,056 blocks concurrently and the rest queue.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Occupancy is how full the machine is">
    **Occupancy** is resident warps divided by the SM's maximum. It exists for one reason: an SM stays busy only when at least one of its warps is not stalled on memory. More resident warps means more chances to overlap a wait with arithmetic.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. The wave tail">
    One full round of blocks is a **wave**. If your grid needs 1.05 waves, the first wave runs on the whole GPU, then 5% of it runs for another full block-duration while 95% idles. That is **wave quantization** and it is a real, measurable loss.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. The practical rules">
    Prefer 128–512 threads per block. Keep registers under 64 per thread or occupancy collapses. And check the wave count: a grid just over a multiple of the resident capacity is the classic hidden 2× loss.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Compute the grid and the waves yourself

The arithmetic is not deep, but it is the arithmetic you will do while reading a trace. For a one-thread-per-element kernel over $n$ elements with $T$ threads per block:

$$
\text{blocks} = \left\lceil \frac{n}{T} \right\rceil, \qquad \text{waves} = \frac{\text{blocks}}{\text{blocks resident per SM} \times \text{number of SMs}}
$$

Take an H100 (132 SMs) with 256-thread blocks that fit 8 blocks per SM, so 1,056 blocks are resident. Launch over $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ elements:

$$
\text{blocks} = \left\lceil \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{256} \right\rceil = 3{,}907 \qquad \text{waves} = \frac{3{,}907}{1{,}056} = 3.70
$$

Since there is no partial wave below one full one, the loss from the final wave is <Term t="wave-quantization">wave quantization</Term>:

$$
\text{efficiency} = \frac{3.70}{\lceil 3.70\rceil} = \frac{3.70}{4} = 92.5\%
$$

Perfectly acceptable. Now launch over **1,090,000** elements:

$$
\text{blocks} = \left\lceil \frac{1{,}090{,}000}{256} \right\rceil = 4{,}258 \qquad \text{waves} = \frac{4{,}258}{1{,}056} = 4.03
$$

Efficiency $= 4.03 / 5 = 80.6\%$. Four full waves hold 4,224 blocks, so this grid has 34 blocks too many — and the fifth wave is only 3.2% full while the GPU waits for it. **The extra work is 0.8%; the wasted wall clock is 19%.** Nothing in the kernel body changed.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why tiny overruns are so expensive">
  Wave quantization has an ugly shape: the loss is zero when the grid divides evenly, and climbs steeply just past a boundary. Going from 4.00 waves to 4.01 waves costs you a 19% drop in efficiency — you pay for a whole fifth wave to run 34 blocks.

  This is why grid sizes are worth checking. The cure is a **grid-stride loop**: launch exactly the resident capacity's worth of blocks and have each one loop over several elements. The grid then always fills the machine exactly once, at any problem size.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Wave quantization loss, stated once">
  With $W$ waves of work and a machine that retires $R$ blocks per wave:

  $$
  \text{efficiency} = \frac{W}{\lceil W \rceil}
  $$

  The loss is $\lceil W\rceil - W$ whole waves' worth of idle time. **Fixes:** size the grid to a multiple of $R$ with a grid-stride loop so each block does several elements; or shrink the block size so more blocks fit per SM; or merge the launch with the neighbouring kernel. All three are free.
</Callout>

## Occupancy: what it is and what it is not

Derive the register limit first. Each SM on an H100 has a 64K-register file. A 256-thread block using 128 registers per thread needs 256 × 128 = 32,768 registers, so only **two** such blocks fit per SM — regardless of how many you would like. If a compiler spills registers to local memory (which is HBM), it destroys the kernel twice over: lower <Term t="occupancy">occupancy</Term> *and* extra bandwidth traffic. That double hit is what practitioners mean by <Term t="register-pressure">register pressure</Term>.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Occupancy is a means, never a goal">
  A register-heavy kernel can run beautifully at 25% occupancy: it has so little memory traffic per instruction that one warp already keeps the tensor cores fed. Chasing 100% occupancy there just burns registers.

  The number that actually matters is **achieved bandwidth or FLOP/s versus the speed of light** — which is exactly what Nsight Compute reports and what the next lesson, *The memory hierarchy*, and Module 12.3, *The roofline model, in depth*, are about.

  There is a middle ground where occupancy genuinely hurts: too *few* warps per SM and the latency of a single HBM access (~700 ns, Module 02) shows through as stalls. That is why ~50% is often the practical sweet spot rather than 100%.
</Callout>

## Warp divergence, priced

Inside a warp, all 32 lanes must take the same branch. This is <Term t="warp-divergence">warp divergence</Term>, and it is why a data-dependent `if` is the most expensive control flow you can write in a kernel. If your kernel does:

```python
if x[i] > 0:      # suppose 10 of 32 lanes are positive
    y = x * 2      # only 10 lanes want this
else:
    y = x * 3      # but 22 lanes want this
```

the hardware executes **both** bodies, masking off the inactive lanes. Two instructions where the source shows one `if`. If the branch is data-dependent and roughly random, you get close to the worst case: with 4 divergent paths you pay 4 instructions.

$$
\text{divergence cost} \approx \frac{\text{number of distinct paths taken}}{\text{paths in the ideal branchless version}}
$$

The fix is not clever branching; it is **branchless code**, which Module 13 will write in Triton. Replace the branch with arithmetic or a mask:

```python
mask = (x > 0)
y = torch.where(mask, x * 2, x * 3)   # every lane computes both, no divergence
```

`torch.where` evaluates both sides but the *warp* still issues both instructions — the difference is you have made the cost explicit and fixed, rather than paying it accidentally wherever data happens to disagree.

<Callout type="real" title="Where divergence actually bites in inference">
  The kernels that suffer are not the GEMMs (those are dense, no branches) but the **epilogue** code: activation functions with data-dependent clamping, top-k and top-p sampling from Module 03, sparsity checks, and MoE routing (Module 19). Sampling in particular is inherently divergent: each sequence picks a different token, so a naive `if sampled == id` loop costs every lane the maximum path.

  This is a real reason engines implement sampling as a **sort-then-scan** rather than as a loop with branches — every lane does the same work regardless of the data.
</Callout>

## What this buys you when you read a trace

A Nsight Systems trace shows one bar per kernel launch. To reason about a bar you now know to ask:

| Question | Answered by |
|---|---|
| Is the grid big enough to fill the GPU? | blocks × threads per block vs resident capacity |
| Is the kernel stalling on memory or math? | occupancy, achieved bytes/s vs speed of light |
| Is there a wave-quantization tail? | waves = blocks ÷ resident blocks; efficiency = W/⌈W⌉ |
| Is register pressure hurting me? | registers per thread and spills in Nsight Compute |
| Why is one lane in my warp idle? | divergence — see the branchless rule above |

Every one of those is a launch-configuration decision, not a mathematics decision, and it is a different lever from everything in Modules 10 and 11. There, you changed *what work is done* (chunk the prefill, quantize the weights). Here you change *how the same work is placed*, and a 2× gap between your kernel and the hardware's limit is very often pure placement.

<KeyNotes>
  1. The hierarchy is thread → warp (32 threads, lockstep) → block (one SM) → grid. The hardware schedules **blocks**; it never schedules threads.
  2. All 32 lanes of a warp are busy or all idle. A warp cannot split, so a data-dependent branch costs both sides — the basis of warp divergence.
  3. Block size should be a multiple of 32 and usually 128–512. Blocks never straddle SMs, which is why shared-memory tiling is scoped to exactly one block.
  4. Occupancy = resident warps ÷ SM max. It exists to hide ~700 ns HBM latency, and it is limited by **registers per thread**, not by anything you choose directly.
  5. Registers are the real occupancy ceiling: 64K registers per SM means a 128-register 256-thread block fits only twice, and any spill to local memory is doubly catastrophic.
  6. Wave efficiency = $W/\lceil W\rceil$. A grid at 1.05 waves throws away almost half the machine; size to a wave multiple or use a grid-stride loop.
  7. Occupancy is a means. The number that matters is achieved bandwidth or FLOP/s against the speed of light, which Nsight Compute reports directly.
  8. Fix divergence with `torch.where` or arithmetic, never with `if`. Sampling, MoE routing and activation epilogues are where this shows up in real inference.
</KeyNotes>
