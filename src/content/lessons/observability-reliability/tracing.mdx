Metrics tell you that the p95 got worse. They do not tell you **which part of the system** got worse, and in an inference service "which part" is not obvious — a request might have waited 400 ms in a queue, or spent 400 ms on the GPU, or spent 400 ms in your own retry loop, and those three have three completely different fixes.

This lesson is about the instrument that answers "which part": the **trace**.

## Metrics aggregate, traces do not

A metric is a number summed over everything that happened in a time window. It is deliberately lossy: it tells you the *shape* of the fleet and nothing about any individual event.

A trace is the opposite trade. It keeps one request's entire story — every operation, its start, its end, and which operation contained which — and throws away everything about the other requests. That is why you cannot compute a p99 from traces, and why you cannot debug from metrics. **You need both**: the metric to tell you *that* there is a problem and *how big* it is, the trace to tell you *where*.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The analogy that makes it stick">
  Metrics are the **odometer** on the dashboard: cheap, always on, tells you how far you went. Traces are the **dashcam**: expensive, only records when something interesting happens, but it tells you exactly which door you scraped.

  You would not debug a car with an odometer. You also cannot drive a fleet with a dashcam.
</Callout>

## Spans, and where the boundaries go

A **<Term t="span">span</Term>** is one timed operation. Four fields, and only four:

```json
{
  "name": "prefill: 512 tokens",
  "service": "prefill-pool-2",
  "start_ms": 108.2,
  "duration_ms": 44.0,
  "parent_span_id": "b71c9e04"
}
```

Spans **nest**. A span has at most one parent, and a parent can have many children. That structure is the whole trick: the timing picture is a tree, and flattening it onto a time axis is what a waterfall view does.

The arithmetic that makes the picture is simple and worth writing down, because "where did the time go" is a sum over the critical path:

$$
\text{E2E} = \text{edge} + \text{queue} + \text{prefill} + \text{decode} + \text{egress}
$$

Take the trace below: 50 + 58 + 44 + 174 + 12 = 338 ms, and every millisecond is attributable to exactly one span.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Three span boundaries people get wrong">
  1. **One giant span for the whole request.** Technically correct and completely useless. It is the uninstrumented case wearing a span as a disguise.
  2. **A span per kernel.** 300 spans per request is unqueryable, costs more to store than the request itself, and tells you nothing you did not already learn in Module 12 with a profiler. Trace what a *component* did, not what a kernel did.
  3. **No parent link.** If you cannot tell which edge request a KV transfer belonged to, you cannot debug a slow request — only a slow *system*.
</Callout>

Where the boundaries belong in an inference stack, from the outside in:

| Component | Span it should emit | Why it is a boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Edge / gateway | TLS + auth, rate limit check, tokenize, detokenize | The only place you can see client-side overhead. If this is 200 ms, no amount of GPU helps. |
| Router | prefix-cache lookup, queue wait | Queue wait is invisible everywhere else. It is the single most common cause of a bad p95 and it exists in exactly one place. |
| Prefill | prefill chunk (name it with the token count) | Chunked prefill means many prefills per request. Naming the size makes the cost legible. |
| Transfer | KV copy P→D, bytes transferred | Only exists in a disaggregated deployment, and only this span tells you whether the wire was the bottleneck. |
| Decode | one span per request covering the whole decode phase | Per-step spans are noise. What you want is "how long was this request decoding, and how many tokens did it produce". |
| Engine | KV allocation, preemption events | A preemption is a scheduling decision with a latency cost. It belongs in the trace as an event, not inferred from a latency bump. |

    You cannot store every trace at 10,000 req/s. **Head sampling** decides at the front door and keeps an unbiased sample — but slow requests are a vanishing fraction, so at a 1% rate you will have almost none of them. **<Term t="tail-sampling">Tail sampling</Term>** decides after you know the outcome: keep 100% of slow and failed traces, and a fraction of fast ones.   </Step>

## Carry the identifier end to end

A trace is only useful if you can **find it** after the fact. That means a <Term t="trace-id">correlation identifier</Term> that survives every hop:

1. The edge **mints** a `trace_id` and returns it in a response header, so a user can quote it in a bug report.
2. Every downstream call forwards it in a header (`traceparent` is the W3C standard; OpenTelemetry uses it natively).
3. The engine **echoes it back** with the request's internal id, so an engine-side span can be found from an edge-side span.
4. Your client logs it with the request, so support tickets have something to search.

<Callout type="real" title="What this looks like in practice">
  At the time of writing, the mainstream serving path supports this: OpenTelemetry is the vendor-neutral standard, and the engines expose hooks for it — vLLM has a request-level id and structured logging that can be correlated. What you will build is the *glue*: header propagation through your own gateway, sampling rules, and the query you run at 3 a.m.

  The part people skip is step 3 — correlating engine-internal events back to the edge request. Without it, your trace covers your gateway and stops at the door, which is exactly where the interesting part is not.
</Callout>

## Propagating context across a streaming boundary

Streaming makes this harder than it looks, and it is worth being precise about why.

The connection stays open for the whole response — tens of seconds. Several mechanisms that normally carry context assume a short request:

- **Connection-scoped state** (an HTTP/2 stream, a keep-alive socket) is shared across many requests, so anything you store "on the connection" will be clobbered.
- **Thread-locals** in the server process may not be the thread the stream callback runs on.
- **A shutdown** mid-stream loses the spans you had not yet flushed.

The pattern that works is: bind the trace context to the **request object**, not to the connection or the thread, and make the stream's callbacks close over it.

```python
# bind context to the request, never to the connection or the thread
class StreamRequest:
    def __init__(self, trace_id: str, span_stack: list[str]):
        self.trace_id = trace_id        # stable for the whole stream
        self.span_stack = span_stack    # the open parent chain

def stream(req: StreamRequest):
    with tracer.start_as_current_span("decode", context=req.context) as decode_span:
        # every token event attributes to decode_span, from any callback
        for tok in engine.stream(req.prompt, parent=decode_span.context):
            req.span_stack[-1] = decode_span.context
            yield tok
        # close the span in a finally, so a client disconnect still records it
```

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The two failures you will actually see">
  1. **Orphan spans.** The trace has a gap in the middle because the decode span was started on one thread and finished on another. Symptom: a trace that shows TTFT perfectly and then nothing for 30 seconds.
  2. **Context lost at the async boundary.** Your `await` crossed a task boundary and the context variable was not propagated. Symptom: everything after the await attributes to a new, unparented root.

  Both are cheap to fix once you know the shape, and nearly impossible to debug without traces.
</Callout>

## What to do when a span shows you something ugly

A trace does not stop at diagnosis; it hands you a decision. Here is the mapping from what you see to what you do, and it is the same mapping every time:

| What the span shows | What it means | The lever |
|---|---|---|
| Edge + auth > 20 ms | You are TLS-terminating and re-handshaking per request | Keep-alive, connection pooling, terminate TLS once |
| Tokenize spikes on long prompts | CPU tokenization is serial with the GPU work | Cap prompt length, or tokenize in a thread pool ahead of admission |
| Queue wait grows with load | Admission is unbounded | Concurrency cap, load shedding (next lesson) |
| Prefill span dominates TTFT | Long prompts are hogging the batch | Chunked prefill with a token budget (Module 10) |
| KV transfer > 10% of TTFT | The wire is in your critical path | Bigger transfer bandwidth, fewer bytes (FP8 KV, Module 11) |
| Decode span long, step count normal | Per-step time is too slow | Bigger batch, or fewer bytes (quantize — Module 11) |
| A span with no parent and no name | Something is uninstrumented | Add the span before you optimize anything |

<Callout type="recap" title="The tracing discipline">
  1. **Span the components**, not the kernels.
  2. **Nest everything.** Without a parent you have a log with timestamps.
  3. **Always span the queue.** It is where most of your unexplained latency lives.
  4. **Mint one id at the edge** and propagate it to the last hop, including through streams.
  5. **Tail-sample** if you sample at all, so your slowest requests survive.
  6. When you find a gap in the trace, the first move is to **add instrumentation**, not to guess.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. Metrics aggregate and traces do not. The metric says *that* and *how big*; only the trace says *where*.
  2. A span has exactly four interesting fields — name, service, start, duration — plus a parent link. Keep the parent, because the waterfall throws the tree away.
  3. Span components, not kernels. Around 300 spans per request is unqueryable and teaches you nothing you did not get from a profiler.
  4. **Always span the queue wait.** It is the most common cause of a bad p95 and it is invisible in every other instrument.
  5. The **critical path** — spans with no gaps — is the latency. Work off the path happened in parallel and cost nothing to fix first.
  6. Mint a `trace_id` at the edge and propagate it to the last hop, including through engine-internal events, or a user cannot give you anything actionable.
  7. Bind trace context to the **request**, never to the connection or the thread; streaming connections are long-lived and shared.
  8. Prefer **tail sampling** when you sample at all: head sampling keeps an unbiased sample of which, by construction, contains almost none of the slow requests you built traces to find.
</KeyNotes>
