At 3 a.m. your p95 time-to-first-token goes from 400 ms to nine seconds. Throughput does not move — it is still exactly as high, because the GPU is doing precisely the same work per second as it was at midnight. Nothing broke. Nothing scaled. The requests simply started **waiting**, and waiting is not a thing any of your dashboards measures directly.

This lesson is about the part of the system that decides what happens when more work arrives than can be handled. It is the difference between a service that degrades and a service that collapses.

## The arithmetic of capacity, from first principles

Everything in this lesson follows from one number. A replica serves requests **concurrently**: each in-flight request occupies one slot from prefill until its last token. So:

$$
\text{capacity} = \frac{\text{concurrency slots}}{\text{mean service time}}
$$

Take the pool of 24 slots and a workload mix taken from a real chat product:

| Request class | Share | Duration | Slot-seconds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short chat | 62% | 0.8 s | 0.50 |
| Tool call | 30% | 2.5 s | 0.75 |
| Long summary | 8% | 8.0 s | 0.64 |
| **Total** | 100% | **1.89 s mean** | **1.89** |

Capacity works out to $\dfrac{24}{1.89} \approx 12.7$ requests per second. Not 24. Not 30. **12.7.**

<Callout type="math" title="Why the mean is a lie you must still use">
  Mean service time is the right number for capacity, because capacity is an average. But it hides the thing that actually kills you: the **variance**.

  At 12.7 req/s the pool is 100% occupied on average — meaning *every slot is busy all the time*. There is no slack anywhere. The consequence is not that a late arrival waits a long time; it is that it waits at all, and in the tail it waits a lot. With arrivals that are spread randomly rather than evenly, some requests will always arrive to find no free slot. Variance, not utilization, is what turns a busy server into a slow one.
</Callout>

## The long prompt is a capacity attack

Here is the asymmetry that makes inference different from ordinary web serving, and it comes straight from Module 05.

A web request costs you one request's worth of CPU. An LLM request costs you its **whole lifetime** of a slot. A 2,000-token prompt and a 2-token prompt both occupy exactly one slot — but the first also does 2,000 tokens of prefill compute before generating a single token.

So a single user sending a 128k-token prompt can:

- hold a slot for minutes rather than seconds,
- block the entire batch with prefill compute, because prefill and decode are competing for the same tensor cores unless you chunk it,
- and allocate a KV cache far larger than anything else in the pool.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The attack that works by accident">
  A well-behaved client sending a batch of 50 long documents is indistinguishable, from the metrics, from an attack. That is why the defense is a **quota**, not a heuristic:

  - per-request `max_model_len`, enforced at admission, not at the scheduler;
  - per-tenant concurrency caps, so one tenant cannot occupy the whole pool;
  - a token budget on prefill (Module 10's chunked prefill), so one prompt cannot own a batch for long;
  - a per-tenant share of the KV pool, so a tenant with long contexts cannot evict everyone else's cache.
</Callout>

## Where the queue goes is a decision

This is the central design choice, and it has exactly three answers.

**1. No queue.** Reject anything that cannot start immediately. This is what a synchronous API does. Latency is bounded and predictable — you either get a slot or you get a fast, retryable error.

**2. A bounded waiting room.** Accept up to $B$ waiting requests; reject past that. This <Term t="waiting-room">waiting room</Term> absorbs a burst and flattens the p95, at the cost of some latency for the requests inside it.

**3. An unbounded queue.** Accept everything, always.

Only the third is indefensible, and the reason is the standard queueing result from Module 06:

$$
W_{\text{queue}} = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho} \times S
$$

where $\rho$ is utilisation and $S$ is service time. As $\rho \to 1$, the wait diverges. An unbounded queue does not protect your service; it **converts a rejection into an indefinite wait**, and the users in that wait consume their entire context window without receiving a token.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: the burst budget">
  You know your traffic. Normal peak is 20 req/s. During an incident — a product launch, a downstream outage — it goes to 60 req/s for about 30 seconds. Capacity is 12.7 req/s.

  The burst is $(60 - 12.7) \times 30 = 1{,}419$ requests of excess. If you want that burst to wait no longer than **2 seconds**, you need a waiting room of:

  $$
  B = 1{,}419 \times 2 = 2{,}838
  $$

  That is 2,838 requests of waiting capacity on a pool of 24 slots — 118 queued per slot. This is not a typo: **any room large enough to absorb that burst also allows a request to wait minutes**, because the room is shared and served first-in-first-out. This is why the real defense for a 4× burst is not a bigger queue, it is load shedding with a good error and a fast retry.
</Callout>

## Admission control: deciding at the door

**<Term t="admission-control">Admission control</Term>** is the check you run before a request is allowed to take a slot. Everything in it should be O(1) and free, because it runs on every request including the ones you are about to reject.

The checks, in the order you should run them:

1. **Identity and quota.** Is this key allowed, and is it under its per-key concurrency and token-rate limit?
2. **Shape.** Is the request's token count within `max_model_len`? Is `max_tokens` sane? Reject absurd values instantly — they are cheap to reject and ruinous to accept.
3. **Capacity.** Is there a free slot, and is the waiting room below $B$?
4. **Priority.** If it will not fit, who gets it? (Below.)

<Callout type="real" title="What the engines already give you">
  At the time of writing, the mainstream serving engines expose the levers this lesson describes as configuration: a maximum number of concurrent sequences, a maximum model length, a maximum number of batched tokens per step, and in some builds an explicit queue depth. Those four knobs are admission control. Module 23's autoscaling then scales the *number of replicas* against them.

  What they generally do not give you: per-tenant fairness, weighted priority, or a shed reason your clients can act on. That layer is yours to build, and it is where multi-tenant services earn their keep.
</Callout>

## Load shedding: refusing on purpose

**<Term t="load-shedding">Load shedding</Term>** is the deliberate decision to reject work that would otherwise degrade the service for everyone.

The decision rule is simple and worth stating out loud:

> **Shed a request when serving it would make more requests miss the SLO than it would make succeed.**

That is a calculation, not a feeling. If one request occupies a slot for 8 seconds and it pushes four other requests past their TTFT budget, shedding one and serving four is correct even though you served five fewer.

Three policies, and they are not equally good:

| Policy | Rule | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold | Shed when queue depth > $B$ | Simple, works, has a cliff. Add hysteresis or you oscillate. |
| Latency-aware | Shed when p95 wait > budget | Directly targets the SLO. Needs the queue wait measured (it is — the tracing lesson). |
| Cost-aware | Shed the cheapest requests to fit the worst ones | Fairer, better for batch workloads, genuinely more complex. |

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The oscillation trap">
  A threshold with no hysteresis will oscillate: you shed, the queue drains, you re-admit, the queue refills, you shed. Within thirty seconds you have produced a sawtooth that makes your p95 look worse than either state would on its own, and it burns GPU on requests you then throw away.

  The fix is a **two-threshold** gate: shed above $B_{\text{high}}$, resume below $B_{\text{low}}$, with $B_{\text{low}} < B_{\text{high}}$ — typically 80% of the way down. Or use a smoothed signal, like the EMA of queue depth, rather than the instantaneous value.
</Callout>

## Backpressure: pushing the problem upstream

**<Term t="backpressure">Backpressure</Term>** is making the overload visible to whoever can fix it, before you have to refuse work.

Two levels:

- **Within one replica:** when the waiting room fills, the admission check starts returning errors immediately. That is backpressure inside the process.
- **Across replicas (via the router):** when the router sees a replica whose queue is deep, it stops sending to it. This is the queue-aware routing of Module 23, and it is backpressure expressed as a routing decision.

The reason this matters is capacity planning. If you shed at 60 req/s and autoscale on request *rate*, you will scale down, the queue will refill, you will shed again, and you have built an oscillator at the fleet level. If the router sends fewer requests to a saturated replica and the autoscaler reads **queue depth**, the loop closes correctly.

## Fairness, and who gets served when you cannot serve everyone

Once you are shedding, you are making a policy decision. There are three reasonable ones:

- **First in, first out.** Simplest. Starves long requests, which is exactly the request class whose users are most patient — and it starves them *unpredictably*.
- **Shortest job first.** Maximizes completions per second, so it maximizes goodput. But it systematically punishes long-context users, and a service that never answers long requests eventually only serves short ones.
- **Weighted fair queuing.** Each tenant gets a share; within a tenant, FIFO. This is what you want the moment you have more than one paying customer, and it is the only policy where "we shed fairly" is a true sentence.

<Callout type="mentor" title="What to write down before you build any of this">
  Four numbers, in a runbook, before the incident:

  1. The concurrency cap, and the arithmetic that produced it (slots ÷ mean service time).
  2. The waiting-room size, and the burst assumption behind it.
  3. The shed threshold, and the shed error the client sees (status code, body, retry hint).
  4. What you scale on, and why it is not request rate.

  Everything else in this lesson can be discovered during an incident. Those four cannot, because discovering them requires knowing what your service was supposed to do.
</Callout>

<Callout type="recap" title="Overload, in one list">
  1. **Capacity = slots ÷ mean service time.** For a chat mix, that is about 1.9 s mean, so a 24-slot pool does 12.7 req/s — not 24.
  2. **A slot is held for the whole request.** One 128k prompt is a capacity attack wearing a normal request.
  3. **The waiting room is sized from a burst budget**, and any room big enough to absorb a large burst also permits multi-minute waits.
  4. **Unbounded queues are never right.** They convert rejections into indefinite waits at full cost.
  5. **Shed when serving would cost more requests than it would save.** That is a calculation, and it is worth doing out loud.
  6. **Always use hysteresis** on a shed threshold, or the system oscillates and the p95 gets worse than either state.
  7. **Scale on queue depth or latency, not on request rate** — otherwise autoscaling fights your shed policy and the fleet oscillates.
  8. With more than one tenant, **weighted fair queuing** is the only policy where "we shed fairly" is true.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. Capacity is `concurrency slots ÷ mean service time`. A realistic chat mix averages about 1.9 s, so 24 slots serve **12.7 req/s**, not 24 — and at 100% average occupancy there is no slack anywhere.
  2. A slot is held from prefill to the last token, so **one long prompt can starve the pool**. Bound it with `max_model_len`, per-tenant concurrency caps and a prefill token budget.
  3. Size the **waiting room from a burst budget** — excess requests × tolerable wait — and accept that a room large enough for a big burst necessarily permits long waits.
  4. Unbounded queues are indefensible: queueing math says the wait diverges as utilisation approaches 1, so they convert fast rejections into indefinite waits at full cost.
  5. **Shed when serving one request would cause more requests to miss the SLO than it would make succeed.** Latency-aware shedding beats a pure depth threshold because it targets the actual promise.
  6. Put **hysteresis** on the shed threshold (resume at ~80% of the shed level), or the system oscillates and the p95 is worse than either steady state.
  7. Push backpressure upstream through **queue-aware routing** and scale on queue depth, not request rate — otherwise autoscaling fights the shed policy and the fleet sawtooths.
  8. With more than one tenant, **weighted fair queuing** is the only admission policy in which "we shed fairly" is a true statement.
</KeyNotes>
