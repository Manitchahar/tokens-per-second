The last four lessons have been about the *math*: scales, granularity, schemes, the three clever methods. This one is about the part that actually determines whether your deployment is faster or slower, and it is a single question with a single correct answer: **when does the integer become a float?**

## The one decision that matters

You have a matrix of INT4 codes in memory. You need $Y = XW$. At some point each code has to be turned back into a BF16 value, because the tensor cores that do the multiply only speak BF16 (or FP8, or INT8 — but they do not speak "INT4 with a scale attached").

There are exactly two places that turn can happen:

### Unfused: dequantize to HBM, then GEMM

```python
W_bf16 = (codes.to(torch.float32) - zero_points) * scales   # writes P×2 bytes
Y = X @ W_bf16                                                # reads P×2 bytes, writes a BF16 temp
```

Count the bytes. You saved memory *on disk* — the packed model is 4.3 GB. But the moment you start serving, you read the 4.3 GB of codes and then **write and read back 16 GB of BF16**. The decode step, which from Lesson 11.1 is *entirely* determined by bytes moved, now moves **more** bytes than BF16 did.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="This is why people report that quantization made their model slower">
  Nothing is wrong with the quantization. The arithmetic is correct and the answer is right. You simply paid the bandwidth cost of materialising a BF16 copy on every single decode step, which is strictly worse than never quantizing at all.

  It shows up in three places: a slow first token after a cold load, a step time that does not improve with INT4, and a GPU memory report that says you are using 16 GB despite loading a 4-bit checkpoint.
</Callout>

### Fused: dequantize inside the GEMM

The fix is to never materialise the BF16 matrix. A fused kernel:

1. Loads a **packed tile** of codes from HBM — 4-bit codes are tiny, so a large tile arrives in one transaction.
2. Writes them to **shared memory**, still packed.
3. **Widens to BF16 in registers** as it streams into the tensor cores.
4. Runs the matmul, accumulating in FP32, and writes the BF16 output.

The widened values exist only in on-chip registers, in a tile a few kilobytes across, and are overwritten before the next tile arrives. **HBM only ever sees the packed codes.** The byte count drops by the full factor you bought.

    Three timelines, one scale: bytes moved per decode step divided by bandwidth. The top bar is BF16. The others are the same model at 4 bits. The whole question is which of the bottom two you actually get.

  <Step title="2. The unfused path, and why it is slower">
    Unfused, you read the small packed weights and then **write and re-read a full BF16 copy**. The red bar is longer than the BF16 bar. You saved disk and lost bandwidth, and bandwidth is what the decode step is made of.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. The fused path">
    Fused: load the packed tile into shared memory, widen it to BF16 in registers, and hand it straight to the tensor cores. The green bar is a quarter of the BF16 bar. Nothing writes a BF16 copy back to HBM — that is the entire trick.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. The KV cache is a separate pipeline">
    Now the other term. The KV cache is written once per token and read once per step, with a different access pattern and different error lifetime. It takes different bit widths and different grouping from the weights.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. How far can KV go">
    Halve the KV cache and the users who fit roughly double. But KV error accumulates over sequence length, so 4-bit KV costs real quality on long prompts — more than the same 4-bit cost on weights.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. What this buys a decode step">
    Weights and KV are independent levers on the same byte count. Pull both and you get a shorter step *and* more concurrency from the same card. This is where the memory saving finally becomes a throughput number.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## The arithmetic of a quantized decode step

You now have everything needed to size a configuration. Recall the byte count from Lesson 11.1:

$$
\text{bytes} = \underbrace{P \cdot \frac{b_w}{8}\cdot(1 + \tfrac{32}{g\,b_w})}_{\text{weights, incl.\ scales}} \;+\; \underbrace{B\,T \cdot 2LH_{kv}D \cdot \frac{b_{kv}}{8}}_{\text{KV}}
$$

For Llama-3.1-8B ($P = 8.03$e9, $L = 32$, $H_{kv} = 8$, $D = 128$) at $B = 32$, $T = 4096$, on an H100 (3.35 TB/s, 80% achieved = 2.68 TB/s):

| Config | Weights | KV | Total bytes | Step time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BF16 / BF16 | 16.06 GB | 17.18 GB | 33.24 GB | 12.4 ms |
| W4A16 / BF16 (g=128) | 4.27 GB | 17.18 GB | 21.45 GB | 8.0 ms |
| FP8 / FP8 | 8.03 GB | 8.59 GB | 16.62 GB | 6.2 ms |
| W4 (g=128) / KV8 | 4.27 GB | 8.59 GB | 12.86 GB | 4.8 ms |
| W4 (g=128) / KV4 | 4.27 GB | 4.29 GB | 8.56 GB | 3.2 ms |

Read that table as an engineer, because the last three rows are where people get into trouble:

- **W4 alone is only 1.55×.** Not 4×. The KV cache was already 52% of the bytes and you did not touch it.
- **Halving the KV cache is a bigger lever than the weights** at this batch and context, and it is the one people skip.
- **Doing both is where the 4× lives** — 3.9× on the step, and a large multiple on concurrency.

<Callout type="math" title="When does weight-only quantization become the bigger lever again?">
  Look at the ratio:

  $$
  \frac{\text{KV bytes}}{\text{weight bytes}} = \frac{B\,T \cdot 2LH_{kv}D \cdot (b_{kv}/8)}{P\,(b_w/8)}
  $$

  KV dominates at **small batch, long context**. At $B = 8$, $T = 32{,}000$, the KV cache for Llama-8B is $1 \times 32{,}000 \times 131{,}072 = 33.6$ GB — twice the BF16 weights. In that regime, weight quantization buys you very little and KV quantization buys you everything.

  Weights dominate at **large batch, short context**. At $B = 512$, $T = 512$, KV is only 34 GB for a batch of 512 — hmm, still large; but the *per-user* KV is small, so what actually limits you is the weights and the compute term, and W8A8/FP8 is the right lever.

  **Compute the ratio for your configuration before choosing.** It takes thirty seconds and it prevents the most common quantization mistake.
</Callout>

## KV cache quantization, properly

The KV cache is a different problem from weights, and the differences matter.

| | Weights | KV cache |
|---|---|---|---|
| Written | once, offline | every step, per token |
| Error lifetime | permanent, but measurable once | recomputed per request |
| Distribution | you can inspect it offline | varies per token, per request |
| Can you store extra metadata? | yes, freely | yes, but scales must be cheap to apply |

### Grouping: how many numbers share one scale

The same lesson applies. The KV cache has structure you can exploit:

- **Per-tensor.** One scale for the entire cache. Cheapest, and the worst for outliers. Rarely a good idea.
- **Per-token.** Each token's K and V vectors get their own scale. This is the standard and it is cheap, because a scale is naturally associated with a token — the engine already knows which token's row it is writing.
- **Per-head.** Each attention head gets its own scale. Finer still, and it matches the structure of attention itself.

Most production KV quantization uses **per-token**, sometimes refined to **per-head** at 4 bits. The rule of thumb from the weights lesson holds exactly: group size and quality trade against each other, and metadata is cheap here because there is only one scale per token per head, not per weight.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Why KV cache quantization damages long context specifically">
  Consider a single attention score for token $i$ attending back to token $j$:

  $$
  s_{ij} = \frac{q_i \cdot k_j}{\sqrt{d_h}}
  $$

  Both $q_i$ and $k_j$ are quantized. Now put $j = 1$ — the very first token — and ask what happens as the sequence grows to 8,000 tokens. There are now 8,000 quantized key vectors contributing to every attention distribution, each carrying its own error. The errors do not cancel: they accumulate in the softmax denominator and in the weighted sum of values.

  The symptom is not gibberish. It is **repetition** and **losing the ability to recall something stated 6,000 tokens ago** — the model attends to the wrong tokens because their keys moved slightly in a high-dimensional space. This is why KV quantization at 4 bits is much more dangerous at 128k context than at 4k.
</Callout>

### The practical settings

| KV bits | Grouping | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| FP8 (E4M3) | none needed (per-element exponent) | **Best default on Hopper+.** Halves KV memory at minimal quality cost. |
| INT8 | per-token | Safe, universally supported, and what to use off Hopper. |
| INT4 | per-token, per-head scales | Real quality cost at long context. Only with an eval that shows it is acceptable. |
| INT2 | — | Do not. |

<Callout type="real" title="Engine support, at the time of writing">
  vLLM exposes KV cache dtype directly (`--kv-cache-dtype`), accepting `auto` (model's native), `fp8`, and `fp8_e4m3` / `fp8_e5m2`. For INT8 KV it has historically gone through a calibration path. SGLang and TensorRT-LLM expose equivalent knobs.

  The thing to verify in whatever engine you use: **does the attention kernel actually support this dtype natively**, or will it dequantize the whole cache to BF16 into a workspace before the attention call? The second case gives you the memory saving and none of the speed, which is the unfused mistake from the top of this lesson, one level up.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. A quantized GEMM is only fast if the dequantization is **fused into the kernel**. Unfused, you move more bytes than BF16 and it is genuinely slower.
  2. A fused kernel loads packed tiles to shared memory, widens to BF16 in registers, and never writes a BF16 copy to HBM. That single fact is the speedup.
  3. Bytes per step = weights + KV. These are independent levers; W4 alone was 1.55× here because the KV cache was already half the bill.
  4. Compute the KV-to-weight byte ratio for your batch and context before choosing. Small batch and long context makes KV the dominant term.
  5. KV cache quantization groups per token (or per head), not per weight, because the scale has to be cheap to apply during attention.
  6. KV cache error accumulates over sequence length: 8,000 quantized keys all shift slightly. The symptom is repetition and lost long-range recall, not gibberish.
  7. FP8 KV is the best default on Hopper+ because the per-element exponent removes the scale problem entirely; INT8 per-token is the safe portable choice.
  8. Verify your engine's attention kernel is **native** in the KV dtype. A dequantize-to-BF16 workspace gives memory savings and no speed.
</KeyNotes>
