You have a 4-bit model. Someone asks whether it is "still good". That question has no useful answer, and the fact that it has no useful answer is the lesson.

Quantization does not break a model the way a bug breaks it. It does not crash, it does not produce gibberish, and it passes every smoke test you are likely to write in an afternoon. It **shifts the error distribution**: it makes the model slightly more likely to mis-copy a long string, slightly more likely to loop, slightly more willing to guess rather than say it does not know. The damage is real and it is invisible to aggregate metrics.

So the job is not "detect breakage". It is **build an instrument sensitive enough to see a 2-point regression, and then decide whether that 2 points matters to your product**.

## Stage 1: perplexity, the fast smoke test

<Term t="perplexity">Perplexity</Term> is how surprised the model is by real text it was not trained on. Formally, for a corpus of tokens $x_1, \dots, x_N$:

$$
\text{PPL} = \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(x_i \mid x_{<i}) \right)
$$

Read it as "the average number of equally likely choices the model is as surprised by as the real next token." A PPL of 6.72 means the model is about as uncertain as a 6.72-way guess.

It is worth running first, for three reasons: it takes **minutes**, it needs no labels, and it moves **before** task accuracy does. A typical 8B model at 4-bit goes from ~6.72 to ~6.89 — a 2.5% jump that tells you something went wrong even though every accuracy number is unchanged.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Perplexity is a smoke test, not a decision">
  PPL is an average over every token of every document. A model that is 0.5% better on 95% of tokens and 30% worse on the 5% that matter will show you a PPL change of about zero — and you have shipped a regression in exactly the place your users notice.

  It is also **corpus-dependent**. A model tuned on code-heavy text will score worse on a news corpus than the same model in BF16, with nothing wrong anywhere. Run PPL on text that resembles your traffic, or it is measuring the wrong thing.
</Callout>

## Stage 2: task benchmarks, the actual gate

This is where the decision gets made. The right benchmarks are not "the popular ones" — they are the ones that fail in the ways your users would describe as *the model got worse*.

| If your product is… | Watch | Why it fails first under quantization |
|---|---|---|
| Coding assistant | HumanEval, MBPP, your own held-out PRs | Exact string reproduction and long dependency chains are the first casualty |
| Math / reasoning | GSM8K, MATH | Multi-step arithmetic accumulates per-step error, and $\sqrt{L}$ is real here |
| RAG / search | Long-context QA, retrieval accuracy | Reads the KV cache hardest; hurts if the cache is quantized |
| Support / chat | Your own eval set, judged by a rubric | Conversation quality drifts in ways no public benchmark measures |
| Summarisation | ROUGE / your own factuality check | Factuality degrades before fluency does — the summary stays smooth and becomes wrong |

Run **at least three**, and always include one from your own traffic. A public benchmark tells you the community's experience; only your own eval tells you your users'.

## Sample size is not a detail

The single most common way quantization evals go wrong is not a bad benchmark. It is **too few samples**, which turns noise into a decision.

A proportion measured on $n$ samples has a standard error of roughly:

$$
\text{SE} \approx \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
$$

and a 95% interval is about $\pm 1.96 \times \text{SE}$. At $p \approx 0.6$:

| Samples | 95% interval |
|---|---|
| 100 | ±9.5 points |
| 200 | ±6.8 points |
| 1,000 | ±3.0 points |
| 5,000 | ±1.4 points |
| 20,000 | ±0.7 points |

**A 4-bit regression is usually 1–4 points.** So to see a 2-point regression you need on the order of 5,000 samples per task, not 200. If you cannot afford that, then the honest conclusion is "this configuration is within our measurement resolution", and you should say that in the report rather than declaring a 1.2-point improvement.

<Callout type="math" title="A paired test is much more sensitive than the intervals suggest">
  Those intervals assume each model is evaluated on independent samples, which overstates the uncertainty. In practice the same prompts go to both models, and the two scores are highly correlated. A **paired** test — the McNemar test on which items each model got right — typically needs 5–10× fewer samples to detect the same difference.

  This is not a nicety. It is the difference between a 5,000-sample eval and a 50,000-sample one, i.e. between hours and a week of GPU time. If your harness can do paired analysis, use it.
</Callout>

## KV cache quantization needs its own sweep

The previous lesson warned that KV cache error accumulates with sequence length. This is a measurement problem as much as a modelling one, and the fix is a specific experiment:

> **Hold the weights fixed. Vary the KV precision. Sweep the context length. Plot accuracy against length.**

That plot has a characteristic shape:

| Context | INT8 KV | INT4 KV |
|---|---|---|
| 1k | ~no change | ~no change |
| 8k | −0.5 pts | −3 to −6 pts |
| 32k | −1 to −2 pts | −10 to −15 pts |
| 128k | −2 to −4 pts | unusable |

Two practical consequences:

1. **Never evaluate KV precision at a short context.** You will get a false pass. Evaluate at or above your longest served context.
2. **A config that passes at 8k may fail at 128k.** If your product grows context, treat KV precision as a config you will re-tune, not a decision you make once.

<Callout type="mentor" title="Read the failures, not just the score">
  Aggregate scores tell you *that* something regressed. Reading twenty actual outputs tells you *what*, and that is what changes your mind about a config.

  The signatures to look for, in rough order of how often they are the real problem:
  - **Repetition** — the model loops on a phrase instead of progressing. Almost always KV quantization at long context.
  - **Lost needle** — it cannot retrieve a specific fact stated thousands of tokens back. KV cache error, again, and often worse than it looks.
  - **Late hallucination** — correct and detailed until the final clause, which it invents. Weight quantization at 4 bits, amplified by greedy decoding.
  - **Code that almost runs** — right structure, wrong identifier or off-by-one. Weight quantization, concentrated in the MLP.

  Keep a small <Term t="golden-set">golden set</Term> — a hand-reviewed set of ~50 cases with known-correct behaviour — that no automated tuning touches. It is the last line of defence, and it is what you read when a score moves and you do not know why.
</Callout>

## Writing the report that gets accepted

A quantization decision is a **behavioural change to your model**, and it should be reviewed like one. The report is short and it has a fixed shape:

```
## Quantization change: <model> BF16 → W4A16 (g128, GPTQ)

Config     model revision <sha>, quantizer gptq, group 128, weights INT4,
           activations BF16, KV cache BF16, calibration 128 sequences
Eval       task suite <name+v>, 5,000 samples/task, paired McNemar,
           greedy decoding, seed 1234, prompt format as served

Quality    GSM8K    62.5 → 61.4   −1.1 ± 0.8   not significant
           HumanEval 48.2 → 45.9  −2.3 ± 1.1   SIGNIFICANT, fails 2 of 12 accepted
           MMLU     68.4 → 67.9   −0.5 ± 0.7   not significant
           LongQA   71.0 → 68.2   −2.8 ± 1.2   SIGNIFICANT

Perf       weights 16.06 → 4.27 GB;  step 12.4 → 8.0 ms
           peak memory 34.1 → 22.4 GB;  concurrency +52%
           TTFT 41 → 38 ms;  TPOT 11.9 → 7.7 ms

Decision   ACCEPT with condition: exclude HumanEval-style tasks from the
           routing policy, or ship W8A8 for the code-serving replica.
Rollback   BF16 checkpoint retained; flag `--quantization w8a8` ready.
```

The things that make this reviewable rather than merely reported:

- **Absolute and relative numbers**, so the reader can check the arithmetic.
- **Intervals**, so "significant" is a claim with evidence.
- **A named decision**, not "here are some numbers". Someone must choose.
- **A rollback plan**, because this is a behavioural change and you will need it.

<Callout type="recap" title="The whole measurement lesson in four lines">
  Run **perplexity** first because it is cheap and moves early, but never decide on it.
  Run **task benchmarks from your own traffic**, because public benchmarks measure the community's users, not yours.
  Use **paired tests and enough samples** to resolve a 2-point regression, which means thousands, not hundreds.
  **Sweep context length** whenever the KV cache is quantized, because that damage only appears at the long end.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. Perplexity is a minutes-long smoke test that moves before accuracy does. It is corpus-dependent and it averages away exactly the rare failures you care about.
  2. The decision gate is task benchmarks, and at least one must come from your own traffic. Public benchmarks measure the community, not your users.
  3. A 4-bit regression is typically 1–4 points, so you need thousands of samples per task. At 200 samples you cannot resolve it, and must say so.
  4. Paired testing (McNemar on which items each model got right) is 5–10× more sample-efficient than independent intervals, because the two models see the same prompts.
  5. Always report absolute and relative deltas with 95% intervals, and mark which moves are significant.
  6. KV cache quantization must be swept across context length. It looks fine at 1k and collapses at 32k.
  7. Read actual failures, not just scores: repetition (KV), lost needle (KV), late hallucination (4-bit weights, greedy), near-miss code (4-bit weights, MLP).
  8. The deliverable is a report with a named decision and a rollback plan, because quantization is a behavioural change to your model.
</KeyNotes>
