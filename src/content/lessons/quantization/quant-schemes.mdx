There is a Wikipedia-shaped factoid that runs through every blog post on this topic: 4-bit quantization is 4× faster than FP16. It is wrong in an interesting way, and understanding why is basically this lesson.

The problem is that "4-bit" is not a format. It is a shorthand for **which tensor got narrower, in what way, and executed by which kernel** — and those three choices pull in different directions.

## Reading the name

Every scheme name has the same shape: **W**, the bits, **A**, the bits. Written compactly: W4A16, W8A8, FP8.

- **W** is the bit width of the **weights**. This is what the previous two lessons have been about.
- **A** is the bit width of the **activations** — the hidden states, the Q/K/V tensors, the attention output, the MLP intermediate. These are recomputed on every single step, so they are never stored, only streamed.

And here is the first surprise: **almost every 4-bit model you will meet is W4A16.** The weights are 4-bit; the activations stay in BF16. Why?

<Callout type="intuition" title="Activations are much harder to quantize than weights">
  A weight is a fixed number, chosen offline, sitting in a matrix with a handful of outliers you can go and look at. An activation is a different number on every request, from a distribution you cannot fully inspect in advance, with **outlier channels that vary per token**.

  GPTQ and AWQ exist to solve the *weight* problem well. The activation problem — getting a scale cheaply, per token, without stalling the pipeline — is why W4A16 is the default and W4A4 is not a thing anyone ships.
</Callout>

So the honest taxonomy is not "more bits = better". It is a set of four different machines:

| Scheme | Weights | Activations | Tensor cores used | Needs a fused kernel? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **BF16** | 16-bit float | 16-bit float | BF16 | no (baseline) |
| **FP8** | 8-bit float | 8-bit float | FP8 | yes (standardised) |
| **W8A8** | 8-bit integer | 8-bit integer | INT8 | yes |
| **W4A16** | 4-bit integer | 16-bit float | INT8 (after dequant) | yes |

## W4A16: shrink what you store, not what you compute

This is the scheme behind essentially every "GPTQ" or "AWQ" checkpoint. The mechanism:

1. **Offline:** quantize each weight matrix to INT4 with group size 128. Store 4-bit codes plus FP16 scales. The model is now ~4.25 bits per weight.
2. **Every forward pass:** the GEMM kernel loads 4-bit weights, **dequantizes them to BF16 inside the kernel's inner loop**, and runs a normal BF16 tensor-core matmul.

So what does W4A16 buy?

- **Memory: 4×.** 16.06 GB → about 4.27 GB.
- **Decode step: close to 4× shorter**, because decode is memory-bound and you just cut the bytes it reads.
- **Prefill: essentially unchanged.** Prefill is compute-bound at ~400 FLOP/byte; it reads the weights once and then does the math. Fewer bytes to read is irrelevant when the math dominates.
- **Compute throughput: unchanged**, because the math still happens in BF16.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="If you dequantize outside the kernel, W4A16 is a memory win and a speed loss">
  Unpack the INT4 weights to a BF16 tensor, then call `torch.matmul`. You now hold 16 GB again. You paid the unpacking cost *and* the memory-bandwidth cost of writing and reading the BF16 copy. The speedup evaporates and you may go **slower** than BF16.

  This is the single most common quantization bug in hand-rolled code, and it is why fused dequantize-and-matmul kernels (Marlin, and the kernels inside vLLM and TensorRT-LLM) exist at all. **Quantization is an engineering problem wearing a numerics costume.**
</Callout>

## W8A8: reach the integer tensor cores

W8A8 quantizes both sides to INT8. The reason to do it is not memory — 8-bit weights only save 2×, and weights are not usually the bottleneck at high batch. The reason is that it is the scheme that **actually runs on the hardware's fastest path**.

- On an A100, INT8 tensor cores do up to 624 TOPS against 312 TFLOPS for BF16 — a **2×** multiplier that no amount of weight-only 4-bit unlocks for prefill, because the activations are still BF16 and the matmul is still BF16.
- On a T4 and on modern Ampere/Hopper parts, INT8 is the well-optimised integer path.

The cost is on the activation side. Activations need a scale, and a scale that works for one token does not work for the next, so real implementations use a **per-token dynamic scale**: compute `max(|x|)` over the token's vector, derive the scale, quantize. That costs a reduction over the activation, which is why it is fused into the preceding kernel (usually RMSNorm or the previous GEMM's epilogue).

| | W4A16 | W8A8 |
|---|---|---|
| Weight memory | 4.27 GB | 8.4 GB |
| Decode step | ~4× faster | ~2× faster |
| **Prefill** | **unchanged** | **up to ~2× faster** |
| Quality risk | low (weights only) | moderate (activations too) |
| Complexity | moderate | high (dynamic per-token scales) |

<Callout type="math" title="Reading that table as an engineer, not a shopper">
  If your bottleneck is **decode latency at low batch** — which is the interactive-chat case — W4A16 wins clearly, because decode reads weights and you cut them 4×.
  If your bottleneck is **prefill throughput** — long prompts, RAG, summarisation, offline batch — W4A16 does nothing for you, and W8A8's 2× on the compute term is the entire game. That is why they coexist and why engines expose both.
</Callout>

## FP8: a different mechanism, not a narrower INT8

FP8 looks like "8-bit" and gets filed next to INT8. It should not be. Its structure is completely different, and the difference is the reason it is the highest-quality scheme available:

$$
\text{INT8: } \quad q = \operatorname{round}\!\left( \frac{x - z}{s} \right), \quad s \text{ fixed per group}
\qquad
\text{FP8: } \quad q = \operatorname{round}\!\left( \frac{x}{2^{e_x - e_{\max}}} \right), \quad e_x \text{ kept per element}
$$

- **INT8 has one scale for many numbers.** A single outlier forces a coarse grid on everything.
- **FP8 keeps its own exponent per element**, exactly like BF16 but shorter. Its format is 1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits (E4M3), giving it the same wide range as BF16.

Consequences:

| | INT8 | FP8 E4M3 |
|---|---|---|
| Range | set by the scale | built in, per element |
| Needs group scales | yes | **no** |
| Metadata overhead | $12/(gb)$ | **zero** |
| Outlier tolerance | poor without fine groups | good, like BF16 |
| Relative precision | $\approx 2^{-b}$ uniform | $\approx 2^{-3}$ near each value |

FP8 E4M3 keeps **3 mantissa bits**. INT8 with a per-group scale keeps *at least* 7 bits of information within a group (as computed in the previous lesson). So FP8 is genuinely the **less precise** of the two in mantissa terms — it wins because it spends bits on range instead of on a shared scale, and because range is what real activations need.

Where it runs: **NVIDIA Hopper (H100, H200) and Blackwell have dedicated FP8 tensor cores** at 2× the BF16 rate. That is where FP8 is the default for training and increasingly for inference. On an A100 or a T4 there is no FP8 tensor-core path at all, so FP8 buys you the memory saving and nothing else — which is a fine reason to use it, but not a speed one.

## The KV cache gets its own decision

Weights are half the story in a memory-bound service. The other half, as Lesson 11.1 computed, is the KV cache, and it is quantized **independently**:

| KV scheme | Bytes/token (8B) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| BF16 | 131,072 | default |
| FP8 | 65,536 | per-tensor scale, works well; native on Hopper+ |
| INT8 | 65,536 | per-token scale; slightly better than FP8 at 8 bits in practice |
| INT4 (per-head or per-token) | 32,768 | bigger quality cost; group-wise scales are essential |

Crucially, KV quantization and weight quantization are **orthogonal**. W4 + KV8 is a real and popular combination: it is the configuration most often chosen for long-context serving, because it halves *both* terms in the decode step's byte count.

<Callout type="real" title="A caution on KV cache quantization you should know before shipping it">
  Quantizing the KV cache does not introduce a small, uniform error. It degrades **attention**, and attention errors compound over sequence length: by token 8,000 you have accumulated error from 8,000 quantized keys. The practical consequences people see are a rising rate of repetition and a degradation in long-range recall, not gibberish.

  The mitigation is grouping: **per-token** scales (each token's K and V get their own) rather than per-tensor, and for 4-bit KV, per-head groups. And it is why FP8 KV, which needs no scale management at all, is the safer first move.
</Callout>

## How to actually choose

Ignore which scheme is fashionable. Work through these four questions in order:

1. **What is my bottleneck?** Decode latency at low batch → weight memory → W4A16. Prefill throughput → activations → W8A8 or FP8. Both, and long context → W4 + KV8.
2. **What hardware do I have?** FP8 needs Hopper or newer for the speed; on an A100 or T4 it is a memory-only win. INT8 needs a good integer GEMM path, which Ampere and later have.
3. **How much quality budget do I have?** If the product is a coding assistant where a wrong token costs a debug session, buy FP8 W8. If it is summarising thousands of documents where occasional awkwardness is invisible, W4 is free money.
4. **Do I have the kernel?** An unsupported scheme is a 1.3× win at best, because you will be dequantizing outside the fused GEMM. Check what your engine actually implements before choosing.

<KeyNotes>
  1. A scheme name is W bits for weights, A bits for activations. The two are independent decisions, as is the KV cache.
  2. W4A16 is the standard 4-bit recipe because activations are much harder to quantize than weights — they vary per token and have outliers that move.
  3. W4A16 gives ~4× less weight memory and ~4× shorter decode steps, but leaves prefill unchanged, since prefill is compute-bound in BF16.
  4. If you dequantize outside the GEMM, W4A16 is a memory win and a speed *loss*. Fused kernels are mandatory.
  5. W8A8 is the scheme that reaches the INT8 tensor cores, so it is the prefill/throughput lever — 2× on the compute term where W4A16 gives nothing.
  6. FP8 is a float with a per-element exponent: no group scales, no metadata, outlier-tolerant, and 2× tensor-core rate on Hopper and newer. On older GPUs it is a memory-only win.
  7. KV quantization is orthogonal and independently worth it. W4 + KV8 halves both terms and is the standard long-context configuration.
  8. Pick by bottleneck, then hardware, then quality budget, then kernel support — in that order. Never by fashion.
</KeyNotes>
