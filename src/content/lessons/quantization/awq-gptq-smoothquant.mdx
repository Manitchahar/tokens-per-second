Round-to-nearest — quantize each weight on its own, with one scale per group, clip to range, done — is what you get for free. At 8 bits it is genuinely fine. At 4 bits group-128, it produces models that are noticeably worse: they repeat themselves, they lose the ability to follow long instructions, and they fall apart on arithmetic.

None of that is mysterious once you look at *why* RTN is weak. RTN makes two assumptions that are both false:

1. **Every weight is equally important.** False. A tiny fraction of weight channels carry a large share of the output's magnitude.
2. **The rounding errors are independent.** False. Quantizing one column changes the best choice for its neighbours, and RTN ignores that.

Three methods fix this, and they are worth learning as three distinct ideas, because they generalise far beyond 4-bit.

## AWQ: protect the channels that matter

Activation **AWQ** (*Activation-aware Weight Quantization*) makes the first observation concrete. For one output channel $j$ of a linear layer, its contribution to the output is $W_{:,j} \cdot X_{:,j}$ — the weight column dotted with the activation column. The magnitude of what this channel contributes is roughly:

$$
\text{salience}_j = \lVert W_{:,j} \rVert \cdot \lVert X_{:,j} \rVert
$$

And empirically, in trained models this product is **extremely** skewed: 1% of channels often carry 20–50% of the total salience. Those channels are not the largest weights — they are the weights whose *input activations* are large, because that specific weight gets multiplied by a big number on every token.

The key move: **you do not have to protect them with more bits.** You have to protect them with a **finer effective grid**. AWQ inserts a per-channel scale that divides out the activation magnitude before quantizing, and folds the reciprocal back into the weight after:

$$
\hat{W}_{:,j} = \left( s_j \cdot \operatorname{quant}\!\left( \frac{W_{:,j}}{ s_j },\ b \right) \right), \qquad s_j = \left( \lVert X_{:,j} \rVert^{\alpha} \right)^{\frac{1 - \alpha}{n}} \text{ (approximately)}
$$

In plain words: AWQ rescales each channel so that the channels which matter most get a **narrower quantisation range** (finer steps, more of their range used), and the channels that do not matter get a coarser one. Same bit budget, same storage, better fidelity where the output is listening.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why this works when uniform rounding does not">
  With one scale for the whole group, the grid step is set by the largest weight in the group — you spend your precision on the biggest number, whether or not the output cares. AWQ says: no. Spend it on the number that reaches the output most strongly, and let the others round to zero. A 4-bit grid used intelligently beats a 4-bit grid used passively; the bit budget never changed.
</Callout>

AWQ needs a **calibration set** — a few hundred representative prompts — to measure the activation statistics $\lVert X_{:,j}\rVert$. That is the only data-dependent part, and it costs minutes, not hours.

## GPTQ: fix the error you already made

GPTQ attacks the second false assumption, and it is the most elegant of the three. Its setup: quantize the weight matrix **one column at a time**, left to right. After quantizing column $i$, you know exactly how much error you just introduced, $\delta_i = W_{:,i} - \hat{W}_{:,i}$. Instead of letting that error sit there, GPTQ **pushes it onto the columns you have not quantized yet**, weighted by how little each of those columns matters to the output.

That "how little" weight is the crux, and it comes from the **inverse Hessian** of the layer's input activations, $\mathbf{H}^{-1} = (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)^{-1}$. This is the same object you met in LoRA-adjacent theory and in second-order optimisation: the Hessian of the loss with respect to the weights is approximated by the second moment of the inputs, $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top$. Its inverse tells you which directions in weight space the outputs are **least** sensitive to — so you are free to dump error there.

$$
\mathbf{H}^{-1} \text{ from calibration data} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{round, then compensate the not-yet-quantized columns}
$$

The whole algorithm, in pseudocode (this is close to the reference implementation's structure):

```python
# one weight matrix W [out, in], calibration inputs X
Hinv = inverse(X @ X.T + damp * I)          # damp = 1% of mean diagonal, for stability
Wq   = zeros_like(W)
for col in range(W.shape[1]):                 # left to right
    w = W[:, col]
    q = quantize_round_to_nearest(w)          # per-group scale
    Wq[:, col] = q
    err = (w - q) / Hinv[col, col]            # the error we just made, Hessian-scaled
    W[:, col+1:] -= err.outer(Hinv[col, col+1:])   # push it forward, weighted
# block the columns in practice so Hinv is never inverted one element at a time
```

The consequence worth noting: GPTQ lets the algorithm be **worse on individual weights and better on the model**, because it optimises the output, not the weights. That is the whole philosophy, and it is why its weight-space error can look worse than RTN's while its output error is better.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="GPTQ and AWQ are not alternatives — most people use both ideas, and GPTQ is order-sensitive">
  **GPTQ is greedy left-to-right**: a later column can only push error *forward*, never back. So column order matters. In practice quantizers process columns in blocks (typically 128 columns), compute the error within a block, and use that block's inverse-Hessian to settle it — which is why "act-order" (processing columns in order of decreasing Hessian diagonal) became a standard refinement.

  **AWQ and GPTQ compose.** Run AWQ's channel scaling first, then GPTQ on the rescaled matrix, and you get the best of both. This is the default in most production stacks at the time of writing: AWQ-style scaling as a pre-processing step, GPTQ-style error compensation as the quantizer.
</Callout>

## SmoothQuant: move the difficulty, don't fight it

The third method is the most conceptually different, and the one that changes how you think about activation quantization.

The problem it solves: **activations have outliers**, and worse, the outlier channels are *consistent* — the same few channels spike on almost every token. INT8 activations with a single per-tensor scale must stretch to cover those spikes, so every ordinary value is quantized on a coarse grid.

SmoothQuant's move is **mathematically equivalent migration**. For each channel, choose a scale $s_j$ and define:

$$
\hat{X}_{\cdot,j} = s_j \, X_{\cdot,j}, \qquad \hat{W}_{j,\cdot} = \frac{W_{j,\cdot}}{s_j}
$$

Because the product is unchanged — $(s_j X_{j})(W_j / s_j) = X_j W_j$ — **the output of the layer is exactly the same in exact arithmetic.** You have not approximated anything. You have chosen to represent the multiplication in a way where the awkward part (the big activations) has been shrunk and the awkward part it creates (the big weights) is handled by per-channel quantization, which weights handle gracefully.

The smoothing strength $\alpha \in [0,1]$ sets how aggressively you migrate:

$$
s_j = \max\lvert X_{\cdot,j}\rvert^{\alpha} \; \big/ \; \max\lvert X_{\cdot,j}\rVert^{\alpha}_{\text{norm}}
$$

- $\alpha = 0$: no smoothing (normal W8A8; activations keep their outliers).
- $\alpha = 1$: migrate everything (bad: weights inherit all the outliers).
- $\alpha \approx 0.5$–${}0.8$ for W8A8; the paper's sweet spot for hard quantisation (INT4/INT3) is higher, pushing *most* of the difficulty into the weights.

<Callout type="math" title="Why SmoothQuant is exact where AWQ and GPTQ are approximate">
  AWQ scales the weights and **lets the output change slightly** (it approximates — it accepts a little output error in exchange for protecting the salient channels). GPTQ scales nothing but **changes the weights** to compensate rounding (also approximate). SmoothQuant's transformation is an **exact algebraic identity** — the layer computes the identical function in infinite precision — and its only approximation is the ordinary quantization that follows.

  That is why SmoothQuant is especially valuable at very low activation bits (INT4, INT3), where you need every bit of headroom, and why it composes cleanly with AWQ's channel scaling (both are per-channel rescalings; one targets weights, the other targets activations).
</Callout>

## What it costs to actually run these

These are not free algorithms, and a production choice has to weigh them. The table below is honest about the real trade-offs, at the time of writing.

| | RTN | AWQ | GPTQ | SmoothQuant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calibration data | none | ~128 sequences | ~128 sequences | ~512 sequences |
| Calibration time | seconds | ~10–30 min | ~10–60 min | ~20–60 min |
| Second-order work | none | none | inverse Hessian per layer | none |
| Quality at 4 bits | poor | good | very good | good (esp. with AWQ) |
| Composition | baseline | composes with GPTQ | composes with AWQ | composes with AWQ / GPTQ |
| Needs custom kernel? | yes | yes | yes | yes (fused scale-fold) |

The pattern: **all three need a fused kernel**, because all three fold a scale into the GEMM. None of them is a "quantize then run normally" story. And **all three need calibration data**, which is a real operational cost — you must pick a set of prompts that represent your traffic, and a set tuned for one product is wrong for another.

<Callout type="mentor" title="How to choose in practice">
  If you are shipping 4-bit weights, use **AWQ-style scaling as a preprocessing step and GPTQ as the quantizer** — that combination is the most common best-quality default and is what most published 4-bit checkpoints use. Reach for **SmoothQuant** when you need low-bit *activations* (W4A4 or W8A8 with hard prompts) or when activation outliers are visibly hurting you.

  Whatever you pick: calibrate on **your own prompts**, evaluate with **your own eval** (next lesson), and always keep the BF16 model around as a fallback. Quantization is a change to the model's behaviour, and the right way to ship one is the same as any other behavioural change: canary it, and be able to roll it back.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. RTN fails at 4 bits because it assumes all weights are equally important and that rounding errors are independent. Both are false.
  2. AWQ weights salience by $\lVert W_{:,j}\rVert \cdot \lVert X_{:,j}\rVert$ — often 1% of channels carry a large share of the output — and spends a finer grid there, not more bits.
  3. GPTQ quantizes left to right and pushes each column's rounding error onto later columns, weighted by the inverse Hessian of the calibration inputs. It optimises the output, not the weights.
  4. Because GPTQ is greedy and forward-only, column order matters; block-wise processing and act-order are the standard fixes.
  5. AWQ and GPTQ compose — AWQ scaling as preprocessing, GPTQ as quantizer is the common best-quality default.
  6. SmoothQuant's rescaling is an **exact algebraic identity**; only the quantization that follows is approximate. It is therefore especially strong at very low activation bits.
  7. $\alpha$ is the smoothing dial: 0 does nothing, 1 dumps all outliers into the weights, and 0.5–0.8 is the W8A8 sweet spot.
  8. All three need a fused scale-folding kernel and a calibration set. There is no "quantize and run normally" version of any of them.
</KeyNotes>
