Module 03 built the transformer: embeddings, attention, the MLP, the residual stream, all the way to a sampled token. Module 3B showed what scale buys and how to spend a compute budget. Now you are about to download a real model and run it. Before you do, it is worth asking a question that did not have a happy answer until 2023: **whose model?**

In 2022 the strongest language models, GPT-3 and its successors, PaLM and Chinchilla, could only be reached through an API or not at all. You could send text and get text back. You could not see a weight, measure a kernel, change the batch size, quantize anything or run it on your own GPU. For an inference engineer that is a wall: **you cannot optimize what you cannot run.** Almost everything in Parts 2–8 of this course exists because that wall came down, and the model that brought it down was LLaMA.

## The wall, and the one-sentence fix

LLaMA (Touvron et al., Meta AI, February 2023) is a family of four models, 7B, 13B, 33B and 65B parameters. Its headline sentence is the one people still quote: **LLaMA-13B outperforms GPT-3 (175B) on most benchmarks, while being more than 10× smaller.** And it did that with three deliberate choices:

1. **Only public data.** No private corpora: CommonCrawl web text (67%), C4 (15%), GitHub, Wikipedia, books, ArXiv and StackExchange (4.5%, 4.5%, 4.5%, 2.5%, 2%). Anyone with enough compute could, in principle, assemble the same mix.
2. **Many more tokens than "necessary".** **1.0 trillion** training tokens for the 7B and 13B models, **1.4 trillion** for 33B and 65B.
3. **A transformer block tidied up** with four changes that had each been shown to help elsewhere. Every Llama, Qwen, Mistral and DeepSeek model since uses some version of them.

Then the weights were released to researchers, and within days they were everywhere. Take the three choices in turn, starting with the one you can see.

## The architecture: four changes to the 2017 block

You already met every piece of the LLaMA block in Module 03. What is new here is seeing which parts were *changed* relative to the 2017 Transformer, and why.

<Scrolly>
  <ArchDiff client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. The 2017 block">
    The original Transformer adds a **sinusoidal position vector** to each token's embedding once, at the input. Each sublayer (attention, then a feed-forward network) is followed by **add the residual, then LayerNorm**: the norm sits *after* the add, on the main path. The feed-forward network is two matrices with a **ReLU** between them, 4× wider than the model.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. Change 1: normalise first (pre-norm)">
    LLaMA normalises the **input** of each sublayer instead of its output: $x + \mathrm{Attn}(\mathrm{Norm}(x))$. The residual stream is never normalised on its way up, so there is a clean, unbroken path from the embedding to the output. This is the pre-norm idea from 03.7, and its payoff is **training stability** in deep stacks. LLaMA credits it to GPT-3.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Change 2: LayerNorm becomes RMSNorm">
    LayerNorm subtracts the mean, divides by the standard deviation, then applies a learned gain **and** a bias. <Term t="rmsnorm">RMSNorm</Term> skips the mean and the bias: divide by the root-mean-square, multiply by a gain. Less arithmetic, one fewer reduction, and it trains as well. You computed it by hand in 03.7.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Change 3: ReLU becomes SwiGLU">
    The ReLU feed-forward network becomes <Term t="swiglu">SwiGLU</Term>: a SiLU-gated product of two branches, then a down-projection. That is **three** matrices instead of two, so to keep the same parameter budget the width shrinks from ${}4d$ to $\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 4d$. For LLaMA-7B: $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 16{,}384 = 10{,}923$, rounded up to a multiple of 256: **11,008**.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Change 4: positions become rotations (RoPE)">
    Nothing is added at the input any more. Instead, every layer **rotates its query and key vectors** by an angle that grows with the token's position (<Term t="rope">RoPE</Term>, 03.4). The dot product of a rotated query and key depends only on how far apart the two tokens are. Values are never rotated.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Same parameter budget">
    Two 4096 × 16,384 matrices hold **134.2M** weights; three 4096 × 11,008 matrices hold **135.3M**. The ⅔ rule means SwiGLU is not "a bigger MLP": the gain comes from the gating, at the same size.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. LLaMA-7B, in numbers">
    Stack 32 of these blocks with $d = 4096$, 32 attention heads, a **32,000-token** vocabulary and a **2,048-token** context, and you have LLaMA-7B: **6.7 billion parameters**. Note one thing that is *not* here yet: every LLaMA-1 model uses plain multi-head attention, one KV head per query head. GQA arrives later.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

### Check the 6.7 billion yourself

LLaMA does not tie its embedding table to its output head, and its linear layers have no bias terms. With $d = 4096$, 32 layers, FFN width 11,008 and vocabulary 32,000:

| part | shape | parameters |
|---|---|---|
| embedding table | 32,000 × 4,096 | 131.1 M |
| attention per layer: $W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o$ | 4 × (4,096 × 4,096) | 67.1 M |
| SwiGLU per layer: gate, up, down | 3 × (4,096 × 11,008) | 135.3 M |
| **× 32 layers** | | **6,476.0 M** |
| RMSNorm gains (2 per layer + 1 final) | 65 × 4,096 | 0.3 M |
| output head | 4,096 × 32,000 | 131.1 M |
| **total** | | **6,738 M ≈ 6.7 B** |

The paper's own table lists 6.7B. In BF16 that is 13.5 GB of weights, which is the first number that mattered to everyone who downloaded it.

<Callout type="math" title="Where 11,008 comes from, exactly">
  Meta's reference code computes the hidden width as two thirds of ${}4d$, then rounds **up** to a multiple of 256 so the matrix shapes stay friendly to GPU tiles:

  $$
  \left\lfloor \tfrac{2}{3} \times 4 \times 4096 \right\rfloor = 10{,}922 \;\longrightarrow\; 256 \times \left\lceil \tfrac{10{,}922}{256} \right\rceil = 256 \times 43 = 11{,}008
  $$

  Do the same for LLaMA-13B ($d = 5120$): $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 20{,}480 = 13{,}653$, rounded up to **13,824**. You will see 13,824 in that model's config.
</Callout>

<Callout type="intuition" title="None of the four changes was new">
  Pre-normalisation came from GPT-2 and GPT-3, RMSNorm from Zhang & Sennrich (2019), SwiGLU from Shazeer (2020) and PaLM, and rotary embeddings from Su et al. (2021) via GPT-Neo. LLaMA's architectural contribution was **picking the four that worked and putting them in one clean, documented block**. That block became the default, which is why "Llama-style" in a model card tells you almost everything about its shapes.
</Callout>

## The training choice: past Chinchilla, on purpose

Module 3B's Chinchilla lesson gave a rule of thumb for a **fixed training budget**: the lowest loss comes from roughly **20 training tokens per parameter**. By that rule a 7B model "should" see about 140 billion tokens. LLaMA-7B saw 1.0 trillion, about **150 tokens per parameter**, seven times past the compute-optimal point.

Why spend training compute "inefficiently"? Because the paper is explicit about a different goal: the best performance at a given **inference** budget. Chinchilla answers "what is the best model I can train for this many FLOPs?" LLaMA asks "what is the best model I can **serve** cheaply?" Those have different answers, because training is paid once and inference is paid on every token, forever. The paper also notes that the 7B model's performance was **still improving after 1T tokens**.

Do the arithmetic with $C \approx 6ND$ from Module 3B:

| model | parameters $N$ | tokens $D$ | training compute ${}6ND$ | weights in FP16 | FLOPs per generated token $\approx 2N$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3 | 175 B | 300 B | ${}3.2 \times 10^{23}$ | 350 GB | 350 GFLOP |
| LLaMA-13B | 13 B | 1.0 T | ${}7.8 \times 10^{22}$ | 26 GB | 26 GFLOP |
| LLaMA-65B | 65.2 B | 1.4 T | ${}5.5 \times 10^{23}$ | 130 GB | 130 GFLOP |

LLaMA-13B cost about **a quarter** of GPT-3's training compute, matched or beat it on most benchmarks, and then costs about **13× less per generated token** forever after. At 350 GB, GPT-3's weights need at least five 80 GB GPUs just to be held; LLaMA-13B's 26 GB fit on one, and at 4 bits (6.5 GB) on a gaming card. Module 3B's [over-training lesson](/learn/scaling/beyond-chinchilla/) generalises this: when you expect to serve a model a great deal, you should train it smaller and longer than Chinchilla says.

<Callout type="mentor" title="This is the most important idea in the lesson for an inference engineer">
  Decode time is set by reading the weights (Module 02's bandwidth limit), so a model's serving cost scales with its **size**, not with how long it was trained. Every extra training token is a one-time cost that makes a *fixed-size* model better. LLaMA turned that into a strategy, and every serious open model since (Llama-3-8B on 15T tokens, Qwen2.5-7B on 18T) trains small models on enormous token counts for exactly this reason.
</Callout>

## The release, and what it unlocked

Meta published the paper and offered the weights to researchers under a **non-commercial licence**, by application. Within about a week the weights had leaked publicly and were on every file-sharing site. Whatever one thinks of that, the effect on the field was immediate, because for the first time a model competitive with GPT-3 was **a file you could load**:

- **llama.cpp** (March 2023) ran LLaMA in plain C/C++ on a laptop CPU and Apple silicon, using 4-bit quantization to fit 7B into about 4 GB. Consumer hardware became a serving target.
- **Alpaca** (Stanford, March 2023) finetuned LLaMA-7B on 52,000 instruction-following examples generated by an OpenAI model, for a few hundred dollars, and got a chatty assistant. Instruction tuning, which Module 3C explains, stopped being a big-lab activity.
- **Vicuna**, **Koala** and dozens more followed within weeks, finetuned on shared conversations. **LoRA** and later **QLoRA** let people finetune 7B–65B models on a single GPU (Module 26 covers serving many LoRAs at once).
- **Serving engines** appeared to run these models efficiently for many users. vLLM's paper (2023) evaluated on OPT and LLaMA models, and the techniques in Parts 3–6 of this course were developed, benchmarked and compared on Llama-shaped weights.

The reason this course can teach you to profile a decode step, write a kernel or size a KV cache on real weights is that open models exist. LLaMA is where that started.

## The lineage: Llama-2 and Llama-3

| | LLaMA-1 (Feb 2023) | Llama-2 (Jul 2023) | Llama-3 (Apr 2024) |
|---|---|---|---|
| sizes | 7B, 13B, 33B, 65B | 7B, 13B, 70B | 8B, 70B (3.1 adds 405B) |
| training tokens | 1.0T / 1.4T | 2T | over 15T |
| context | 2,048 | 4,096 | 8,192 (3.1: 128K) |
| vocabulary | 32,000 | 32,000 | 128,256 (the "128K" tokenizer) |
| attention | MHA everywhere | MHA for 7B/13B, **GQA for 34B and 70B** | **GQA at every size** |
| licence | non-commercial research | community licence, commercial use allowed | community licence |
| chat models | none from Meta | Llama-2-Chat | Llama-3-Instruct |

Three rows matter for serving more than any benchmark:

- **GQA.** Llama-2 introduced grouped-query attention for its larger models (a 34B variant was trained with it but not released; the 70B shipped with it). Llama-3 used it at every size: Llama-3-8B has 32 query heads sharing **8 KV heads**. You learned in 03.6 that this cuts the KV cache by the group factor; for Llama-3-8B that is 4×, and it is a large part of why an 8B model can hold many long conversations on one GPU.
- **The 128K vocabulary.** Llama-3 moved from 32,000 tokens to 128,256. More tokens per vocabulary means **fewer tokens per sentence** (Meta reported up to 15% fewer than Llama-2 on the same text), which is fewer decode steps and less KV cache for the same request. The price is a bigger embedding table and output head: 128,256 × 4,096 = 525M parameters each, together 13% of the 8B model, and the output head is a final matrix multiply that every decode step must read.
- **Context.** 2K → 4K → 8K → 128K. Every doubling of context is a doubling of the worst-case KV cache per request, which is the subject of Module 20.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The family name does not tell you the shapes">
  "Llama" covers models with and without GQA, with 32K and 128K vocabularies, and with 2K to 128K contexts. LLaMA-1-7B in BF16 holds **512 KiB** of KV cache per token (32 layers × 32 KV heads × 128 × 2 × 2 bytes). Llama-3-8B holds **128 KiB** (8 KV heads instead of 32). Same family, same layer count, 4× different memory per token. Always read `config.json`: `num_key_value_heads`, `vocab_size` and `max_position_embeddings`.
</Callout>

## Open weights is not open source

"Open" gets used loosely, and the differences can end a project. There are really three separate things a model release can give you:

| what is released | what it lets you do | examples |
|---|---|---|
| **weights** only, under a custom licence | run, finetune and serve it, within the licence's terms | LLaMA-1 (research only), Llama-2/3 (community licence) |
| **weights** under a standard permissive licence | essentially anything, including commercial use, without special conditions | Mistral-7B (Apache 2.0), most Qwen2.5 sizes (Apache 2.0) |
| weights **plus** training code **plus** training data | reproduce, audit and modify the training itself | AI2's OLMo, EleutherAI's Pythia |

Only the last row is open source in the sense the term has for software: you get the "source" (data and code) needed to rebuild the artefact. The Open Source Initiative published a definition for AI systems in 2024 along these lines, and custom licences with usage restrictions, like Llama's, do not meet it. For running and serving a model, though, **open weights is what you need**: the numbers in the checkpoint are yours to load, measure and optimize.

When you pick a model, read the licence once for three things:

- **Commercial use.** LLaMA-1 forbade it; Llama-2 and later allow it. Apache 2.0 and MIT allow it.
- **Scale and field-of-use conditions.** The Llama-2 licence, for example, requires companies whose products had more than 700 million monthly active users to ask Meta for a separate licence, and all Llama licences come with an acceptable-use policy.
- **Conditions on outputs and derivatives.** Some licences restrict using the model's outputs to train other models, or require derivative models to carry the original name or licence.

Licences change between versions and even between sizes of one release, so check the model card of the exact checkpoint you deploy, at the time you deploy it.

<Callout type="real" title="Now read the paper">
  **"LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models"**, Touvron et al., 2023. [arXiv:2302.13971](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13971).

  **Read:** the introduction (the inference-budget argument against Chinchilla's rule), **Section 2** in full (2.1 data, 2.2 architecture, 2.3 optimizer, 2.4 efficient implementation; it is short), Table 2 (the shapes and learning rates) and Figure 1 (training loss still falling at 1T tokens).

  **Skim:** Section 3, the benchmark results. Look at the tables to see where 13B passes GPT-3 and where it does not. **Skip** unless curious: Sections 4–7 (instruction finetuning, bias and toxicity, carbon footprint, related work), though the carbon section's GPU-hour table is a nice exercise in ${}6ND$.

  **You will now recognise:** pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU with its ⅔ · 4d width, RoPE, AdamW with $\beta_2 = 0.95$ and a cosine schedule (03.2), the 4M-token batch, and in 2.4 an attention implementation that avoids storing the full attention matrix, the idea Module 14 builds FlashAttention from. The section that trained the 65B model on 2,048 A100s at about 380 tokens per second per GPU, roughly 21 days for 1.4T tokens, is ${}6ND$ arithmetic you can now check yourself.
</Callout>

  3. SwiGLU has three matrices, so its width is ⅔ · 4d rounded up to a multiple of 256: 11,008 for LLaMA-7B (13,824 for 13B). That keeps the FFN at about 135M parameters per layer, the same budget as the 2017 FFN.
  4. LLaMA-7B: 32 layers, d = 4096, 32 heads (MHA), vocab 32,000, context 2,048, **6.7B parameters**, 13.5 GB in BF16.

  6. Llama-2 added GQA for its largest models; Llama-3 uses GQA at every size and a 128,256-token vocabulary (fewer tokens per sentence, but a 525M-parameter output head on the 8B model).
  7. **Open weights ≠ open source.** Weights let you run and serve a model; open source in the full sense also releases training code and data.
