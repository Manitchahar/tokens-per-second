Picture a coffee shop with one barista. Twenty people walk in at once. The barista makes one coffee at a time, perfectly, without rushing: twenty seconds each. The next twenty people are standing there the whole time, and the twentieth person's coffee is forty minutes late.

That is not a barista problem. It is a **batching** problem, and it is the situation your GPU is in the moment two people send a message at the same time.

This module is about that. By the end of it you will be able to say, with arithmetic, how many conversations one H100 can serve at once, which knob to turn to serve more, and what each knob costs the users.

## The GPU is a machine for wasting its arithmetic

Open Module 02's roofline again in your head. A decode step for one request must read **every weight in the model** — 16 GB for Llama-3.1-8B in BF16 — and it does about 16 GFLOP of math with them. That is roughly **1 FLOP per byte**, far to the left of the ridge point on any modern GPU.

So a decode step with one user in it spends about 99% of its life **moving bytes**, and the FLOP/s the machine advertises are almost entirely idle. That is not a bug in your code. It is the shape of the problem.

<Callout type="math" title="What one decode step costs">
  For a batch of $B$ sequences, each with about $c$ tokens of context, on GPU $g$:

  $$
  t_{\text{mem}} = \frac{\underbrace{M \cdot w}_{\text{weights}} + \underbrace{B \cdot c \cdot k}_{\text{KV cache}}}{g.\text{bw} \cdot \text{MBU}}
  \qquad
  t_{\text{math}} = \frac{2 \cdot M \cdot B}{g.\text{flops} \cdot \text{MFU}}
  $$

  $$
  t_{\text{step}} = \max(t_{\text{mem}},\ t_{\text{math}}) + \text{overhead}
  $$

  where $M$ is the parameter count, $w$ bytes per weight, $k$ bytes of KV per token per sequence. The weights term does **not** depend on $B$. That single fact is the whole module.
</Callout>

Put Llama-3.1-8B on an H100 and evaluate it with $M = 8.03 \times 10^9$, $w = 2$, $k = 131{,}072$ (128 KiB of KV per token — Module 05 derived that), bandwidth of 3.35 TB/s at 80% efficiency, and about 1.5 ms of fixed overhead per step:

| Batch $B$ | Weight read | KV read | Math | $t_{\text{step}}$ | Throughput | Per user |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6.0 ms | 0.02 ms | 0.03 ms | **7.5 ms** | 133 tok/s | 133 tok/s |
| 8 | 6.0 ms | 0.17 ms | 0.24 ms | **7.7 ms** | 1,040 tok/s | 130 tok/s |
| 32 | 6.0 ms | 0.68 ms | 0.95 ms | **8.2 ms** | 3,900 tok/s | 122 tok/s |
| 128 | 6.0 ms | 2.7 ms | 3.8 ms | **10.2 ms** | 12,500 tok/s | 98 tok/s |

Look down the "per user" column. Sixteen times the batch, and each individual user is barely slower. Eight users cost the GPU an extra 0.2 ms. **That surplus time is the entire budget for serving everyone else.**

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why this happens">
  The 16 GB of weights must cross HBM once per step no matter what. If only one request is decoding, every one of those bytes feeds exactly one multiply-accumulate. If 128 requests are decoding, those same bytes feed 128 of them. The memory system does not care; the arithmetic units finally get fed.

  You are not making the machine faster. You are **stopping it from leaving the FLOPs unused**.
</Callout>

## The first honest attempt: static batching

The obvious fix, and the one every early engine shipped, is **static batching**: gather up to $B$ requests, run them as one batch, and when the whole batch finishes, go and gather the next one.

  <Step title="2. The wait is the cost">
    Eight requests arrived together, so seven sit in the hatched queue while exactly one runs. The step time is a lovely 7.5 ms and the throughput is a dreadful 133 tokens/s for everybody. Over the whole wave, only about a quarter of the wall-clock time has more than one request running. **Queueing delay, not speed, is what users feel.**
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Static batching">
    Switch to policy B: all eight go into one batch. Now the queue is empty at $t = 0$, the weights are read once for all eight, and the wave finishes roughly **four times faster**. This is the fix that made LLM serving economically interesting.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. The step barely moved">
    Here is why it worked. The two blue bars compare a step with one request against a step with all of them: 7.5 ms versus about 7.7 ms. Eight times the output for 3% more time. Batching does not speed up the GPU; it stops the GPU from throwing away its arithmetic.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Everyone finishes together">
    But the batch cannot end until its **slowest** member ends. The red tails are the seats held by requests that already finished: they occupy a row of the batch, they read their KV cache, and they produce nothing. Real answers are ragged, so real batches have tails.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Counting the waste">
    Add it up: $n$ slots held for $\max$ steps each is $n \cdot \max$ slot-steps, of which only $\sum$ produce a token. With the lengths in the visual, more than **a third of every step is spent on seats nobody is sitting in.** That is the number an inference engineer goes after.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. Padding makes it worse">
    And it is structural, not a policy choice. A dense batch is a rectangle — $[n \times \text{maxLen}]$ — so a 30-token answer has to be padded out to the longest one and its positions masked. The dashed outlines show the padding. Nothing in the tensor shapes lets you shrink the batch in the middle of the run.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. The fix, previewed">
    The green dashed outline is the same work done **perfectly packed**: every freed seat filled on the very next step, so the wave is done in about two-thirds of the time. Getting from the red version to the green one is the entire subject of the next lesson.
  </Step>

## The arithmetic of the waste

The waste has a closed form, and it is worth having in your head because it tells you how bad a workload is before you run it.

With $n$ requests of lengths $\ell_1, \dots, \ell_n$, static batching runs $\max_i \ell_i$ steps:

$$
\text{slot-steps} = n \cdot \max_i \ell_i
\qquad
\text{useful} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell_i
$$

$$
\text{waste} = 1 - \frac{\sum_i \ell_i}{n \cdot \max_i \ell_i}
$$

For a batch of eight whose lengths are 96, 40, 72, 28, 60, 84, 52 and 88 tokens: the longest is 96, so the engine runs 96 steps and pays for $n \cdot \max = 8 \times 96 = 768$ slot-steps, of which 520 are useful. Waste: **32%**. If the same eight lengths arrived in a batch of four, the waste barely changes — but you pay twice as many batches, so twice as many long tails.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Why 'just use a smaller batch' is not a fix">
  Smaller batches do not remove the waste; they move it. With batch size 1 every tail is 100%: you are back to the naive server. With a large batch the *fraction* of waste falls (the longest request in 64 samples is closer to the average) but the *duration* of each tail grows, and TTFT for the unlucky requests gets worse. There is no batch size that is free.
</Callout>

## What static batching does to the four numbers

Recall Module 00's four numbers. Static batching moves them in a very particular way, and it is worth writing out because every later lesson is a refinement of this table.

| | Batch 1 | Static batch of $n$ |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first token | Best possible: no queue, prefill only | **Worst**: your request waits for its whole group to start, and behind everyone already in the batch |
| Time per output token | Best: ~7.5 ms | Slightly worse: ~7.7 ms at $n = 8$ |
| Throughput | Terrible: 133 tok/s | Good: ~1,040 tok/s at $n = 8$ |
| Memory | Tiny: no KV for anyone else | Bounded but coarse: the KV of the whole group, all at once |
| Fairness | Perfect: one user at a time | Terrible: the longest request in the group decides when everyone else's answer stops |

## Dynamic batching: a waiting window

Static batching has an assumption that online traffic breaks: that a full batch will arrive. Picture a server with a fixed batch size of ten. Nine requests arrive in the first second; the tenth arrives five minutes later. The static batcher holds the nine for five minutes, because it promised to run them as a group. Every one of those users sees a five-minute time to first token, and the GPU idles the whole time.

<Term t="dynamic-batching">Dynamic batching</Term> fixes that by putting an upper bound on the wait. The server starts a **window** when the first request arrives at an empty server, and closes it on whichever comes first:

- the batch reaches its maximum size $B$, or
- the oldest request has waited $D$, the <Term t="max-queue-delay">max delay</Term>.

Then it runs the batch, and the next request opens a new window. $B$ controls how much work a step contains; $D$ controls how long a request may be delayed to get there.

This is what classic model servers do. At the time of writing, NVIDIA's Triton Inference Server exposes it as `dynamic_batching` with `max_batch_size` and `max_queue_delay_microseconds`; you will read a real `config.pbtxt` in Module 17's "Multi-model serving & Triton Inference Server". The idea predates LLMs and is still the right default for models that do one fixed-size forward pass per request.

### The queueing arithmetic

If requests arrive at an average rate $\lambda$ requests per second, a window of length $D$ seconds contains about $\lambda D$ arrivals. One more request — the one that opened the window — is also in the batch, so

$$
B_{\text{expected}} \approx \min\left(B,\ 1 + \lambda D\right)
$$

The formula is an approximation: it ignores the time the server spends running the previous batch and assumes arrivals are spread evenly. It is still the right first estimate, and it shows the two regimes clearly. When $\lambda D$ is small the batch is tiny and the window mostly adds latency. When $\lambda D$ is large the batch is capped by $B$, and the window rarely matters.

The latency cost is bounded by construction: no request waits more than $D$ before its batch starts. Under light traffic, when the window closes on time rather than on size, a request's average wait is about $D/2$, because arrivals are spread across the window.

Take a concrete case: $\lambda = 200$ requests/s, $D = 5$ ms, $B = 8$. Then $\lambda D = 1$, so the expected batch is $\min(8, 2) = 2$. To see what that buys, model the service time of a batch of $n$ as

$$
s(n) = 1.0\ \text{ms} + 0.5n\ \text{ms}
$$

One millisecond is fixed cost per batch — launching the work, reading the weights — and half a millisecond per request is the actual arithmetic. A single request then takes 1.5 ms, so one-at-a-time serving tops out at 1 / 0.0015 ≈ 667 requests/s. A batch of two takes 2.0 ms and delivers two responses, so it tops out at 2 / 0.002 = 1,000 requests/s.

| Policy | Batch | Service | Capacity | Added wait |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No batching | 1 | 1.5 ms | 667 req/s | 0 |
| Dynamic, $D = 2$ ms | about 1.4 | about 1.7 ms | about 820 req/s | about 1 ms |
| Dynamic, $D = 5$ ms | 2 | 2.0 ms | 1,000 req/s | about 2.5 ms |
| Dynamic, $D = 10$ ms | 3 | 2.5 ms | 1,200 req/s | about 5 ms |

Capacity there is the most requests per second the server can sustain. Read the table carefully, because the first column hides a trap. At the stated arrival rate of 200/s, **throughput does not change at all** — the server is arrival-limited, not capacity-limited, so it still serves 200 requests/s whether it batches or not. What changes is latency: about 1.5 ms alone versus about 4.5 ms with batching (2.5 ms waiting plus 2.0 ms of service). Dynamic batching is not a way to make a lightly loaded server faster. It is a way to buy **headroom**: 1,000 requests/s of capacity instead of 667, paid for with a few milliseconds on every request.

The cap $B = 8$ matters at higher load. At 1,400 requests/s with $D = 5$ ms the window fills by count before it fills by time, the batch becomes 8, and capacity reaches 8 / 0.005 = 1,600 requests/s. That is the trade in one sentence: **the window converts idle throughput into latency**. Choose $D$ from the latency budget you can afford, then check that the batch it produces is big enough to matter.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Why this is right for classifiers and wrong for chat">
  A classifier, an embedding model or a reranker does one forward pass per request. Every request has the same shape, every response takes the same time, and a batch of eight is eight independent pieces of work that finish together. Waiting 5 ms to double the batch is close to free.

  An LLM generation request is not one forward pass. It is a stream of hundreds of decode steps, and a batch formed at step 0 is committed until its **slowest** member finishes. Requests have different prompt and output lengths; the short ones finish early and their seats go idle, exactly the static-batch tail from the previous section. A new request also cannot join a batch mid-stream without changing its shape. Dynamic batching therefore fixes the *wait to form* a batch while leaving the *tail* untouched. The next lesson removes the commitment itself.
</Callout>

<Callout type="real" title="What this looked like in practice">
  For years the standard stack was Hugging Face `generate` in a loop, with a batching layer in front of it (a queue, a fixed batch size, a semaphore). It worked. It was also responsible for an enormous amount of idle GPU time on every chat deployment in the world, which is the gap the next lesson closes.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mentor" title="The habit to build">
  Whenever you look at a serving system, ask two questions: **what fraction of GPU time produces a token somebody wanted?** (the waste number) and **how long is the longest wait?** (the tail). Every scheduling idea in this module is a way to move one of those two numbers.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. Decoding one request at a time runs the GPU at about 1 FLOP per byte: it reads all 16 GB of weights to produce one token and leaves the math units idle.
  2. $t_{\text{step}} = \max(\text{weights} + \text{KV read}, \ \text{math}) + \text{overhead}$. The weight term does not grow with batch size, so the step time is nearly flat for small batches.
  3. On an H100 with Llama-3.1-8B, batch 1 gives ~7.5 ms/token and ~133 tok/s. Batch 8 gives ~7.7 ms/token and ~1,040 tok/s. Eight users for 3% more time.
  4. **Static batching** forms one batch and runs it until the slowest member finishes. It fixes the idle-GPU problem and creates a tail-latency problem.
  5. Static waste $= 1 - \frac{\sum \ell_i}{n \cdot \max \ell_i}$. On ragged real traffic this is routinely 30–50% of every step.
  6. Padding is why you cannot shrink a batch mid-run: the batch is a rectangle, $[n \times \text{maxLen}]$, and short rows are masked but still occupied.
  7. Static batching wrecks TTFT and fairness while helping throughput and memory predictability; every better policy trades against these rows. Log the fraction of GPU time that produces a wanted token and the longest wait in the queue.
  8. **Dynamic batching** opens a window on the first request and closes it at $B$ requests or after $D$. Expected batch $\approx \min(B, 1 + \lambda D)$ and added wait is at most $D$; it buys capacity headroom for fixed-shape models and leaves the LLM batch tail untouched.
</KeyNotes>
