Two long requests are decoding. Each will hold its seat for 200 steps. A third request arrives at step 5 and wants twenty tokens — it could be done in twenty steps, a fraction of a second.

It will wait 195 steps. Not because the GPU is slow, and not because the scheduler is broken, but because **both seats are occupied and neither seat is coming free soon.** That is a queue, and from here on out the hard part of serving is not arithmetic. It is deciding who waits.

Every serving engine contains a queue, whether or not anyone named it one. And the queue is where the engineering decisions that show up in your pager actually live.

## The queue is a policy

A scheduler makes exactly two decisions, every step:

1. **Who gets the next free seat?** (admission order)
2. **What happens when someone leaves or needs more room?** (eviction)

Everything else is bookkeeping. Both decisions are policy, and each has a small number of well-understood options. Here is the whole menu.

| Policy | Rule | Good for | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FIFO** | first in, first out | general traffic; the safe default | one very long request blocks everyone behind it |
| **Priority** | highest priority first | paid tiers, interactive vs batch | high-priority traffic never stops, and low-priority requests starve |
| **Shortest-first** | shortest job first | minimising average wait | long requests starve, exactly as above |
| **Aging** | priority rises the longer you wait | fairness you can prove | needs a scheduler willing to be clever |

  <Step title="2. The hatched bar is the wait">
    Every request starts hatched, at the step it arrives, and turns solid the moment it is admitted. **The length of the hatched bar is that user's TTFT.** No amount of GPU speed shortens it; only the queue does.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. FIFO is fair, and blind">
    Under first-come-first-served, R1 was queued before the flood arrived, so it goes next at step 200. It waits 195 steps. The high-priority requests that arrived later wait longer or shorter purely by accident of arrival order — nobody decided that, it just happened.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. FIFO's weakness: the long request">
    The real cost of FIFO is not unfairness, it is **blocking**. A 200-token request holds its seat for 200 steps because it is in it, not because it needs to. Anyone arriving during those 200 steps waits behind a job that may have already streamed 190 tokens.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Priority spends fairness">
    Switch to priority and R1 waits 275 steps instead of 195. It got *worse*, deliberately: the flood of high-priority work is admitted ahead of it every single time a seat frees. **This is the whole trade.** Priority does not create capacity; it reorders who gets it.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Starvation is the failure mode">
    Push that further and R1 never runs at all. A request that never starts has infinite TTFT, and a p99 that is not a number. Starvation is what separates a priority queue from a fair one, and it is why you never ship raw priority.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. The fix: aging">
    **Aging** gives every waiting request a priority that rises with its wait. The longer you sit, the more you outrank the newcomers. Starvation becomes impossible, because after long enough the starved request outranks everything.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. Cancellation is free money">
    Now watch the cancellation run. R0 disconnects at step 60 and its seat is free *on that same step*, not at step 200. In the visual this collapses the whole run. In production, when a user closes the tab, the engine that notices and frees immediately is measurably better than one that finishes the request first.
  </Step>

  <Step title="9. Preemption, and what it costs">
    Preemption is the emergency valve: when a new request needs more KV memory than is free, the engine evicts a running sequence to make room. Here it evicts and then **pays to replay every token that sequence had already streamed.**
  </Step>

  <Step title="10. Choose on purpose">
    Every row of the table is someone else's pain moved to a different column. Choosing a policy is not finding the best one; it is deciding **who you have promised to serve first**, and then measuring what that promise costs the people you did not promise.
  </Step>

## Fairness, quantified

Fairness is not a feeling here; it is the spread of waits. Take a queue of $n$ requests on $s$ seats, each wanting $\ell_i$ tokens. Under FIFO with all requests arriving together, the first $s$ start immediately and the $(s+1)$-th waits until the shortest of the first $s$ finishes:

$$
\text{wait}_k \approx \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} \ell_i}{s}
$$

With $s = 4$ seats and eight 40-token requests, that gives exactly what the visual shows: the first four admitted at step 0, the next four at step 40. Everyone waits either 0 or 40 steps. Perfectly fair, and the p99 is exactly the p50.

<Callout type="math" title="Fairness is not the same as speed">
  FIFO makes *every* request equally miserable, which is the definition of fair and also the definition of a bad user experience. The engineering goal is rarely equal waits — it is usually **bounding the worst wait**, which is a p99 problem. That is why real systems age the queue: to cap the maximum rather than to equalise the average.
</Callout>

## Cancellation: the cheapest win in serving

Cancellation deserves more attention than it gets, because it is the only policy that costs nothing and returns something.

When a client disconnects, the engine learns it through one of two paths: the HTTP connection drops, or — far more often — the streaming response simply stops being read. Either way, the engine has a sequence that will never be looked at again and is still holding KV cache, still occupying a seat, still consuming memory.

The only question is how quickly it notices.

| Response | Seat freed | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Finish the request first | up to seconds of wasted work | a seat and its KV, for nothing |
| Free on the next step | ~10–30 ms | nothing |

<Callout type="real" title="What this means in practice">
  Aborting a request on client disconnect is standard in modern engines — vLLM, among others, detects a dropped request and stops decoding it rather than finishing into the void. The effect is not subtle: on chat workloads with plenty of tab-closing, it can be a meaningful fraction of total work. If your engine does not do this, it is one of the cheapest wins available to you.
</Callout>

## Preemption: eviction and its price

Preemption is what happens when admission fails. A new request needs $b$ KV blocks, only $b - 1$ are free, and the engine must either reject the request, stall the whole batch until memory frees up, or take a seat from someone who already has one.

**Recompute preemption** takes the seat. The evicted sequence's KV blocks are freed and it goes to the back of the queue. When it is re-admitted, its KV cache is empty, so the engine must replay the prefix it had already streamed — and that replay is real GPU work producing tokens the user has already seen.

The accounting, on the visual's preemption run:

$$
\text{overhead} = \frac{\text{recomputed tokens}}{\text{tokens wanted}} \times \text{work already done}
$$

Evicting a sequence that had streamed 100 tokens costs 100 tokens of recompute. Evicting one that just started costs almost nothing. So the eviction policy is obvious: **evict the newest, not the oldest.**

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Why preemption is a last resort">
  In the visual, running with one fewer seat than the memory budget allows took the workload from 300 steps to 357 — a 19% slowdown — and paid 77 tokens of recompute, for no benefit at all. Preemption is not free throughput. It is the mechanism by which the engine avoids an out-of-memory crash, and it should fire on the margin, not as a routine event.

  A well-tuned engine should preempt rarely. If your preemption counter is climbing steadily, the real problem is upstream: your memory budget is set for an average context length that your traffic no longer has.
</Callout>

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why the newest sequence is the right victim">
  The cost of evicting a sequence is proportional to how much it has already generated, because that is how much has to be replayed. The newest sequence has generated the least. So "evict the newest" is not arbitrary — it is the choice that minimises total recompute, which is the standard shortest-remaining-work argument from OS scheduling.
</Callout>

## Choosing a policy

A defensible default for a production chat service, and the reasoning for each part:

1. **FIFO with aging as the base.** Ageing gives you a bounded worst-case wait without needing a business priority. The bound is roughly `max_queue_depth × average_request_time`, which is a number you can state in an SLO.
2. **Priority only where you can justify it.** A paid tier is a real business reason. Cap it: a high-priority request that has waited more than some threshold drops back to normal, so one impatient user cannot stall the queue.
3. **Never pre-empt preemptively.** Set the memory budget from your *p95* context length, not the maximum, so preemption is a safety net rather than a routine cost.
4. **Cancellation on, always.** It is free, it is strictly better, and there is no configuration in which turning it off helps.

<Callout type="mentor" title="The habit this lesson builds">
  Every scheduling decision is a decision about **who waits**, and the way to make it defensible is to write down whose pain you are moving and why. If you cannot name the group you are disadvantaging, you have not thought about the policy yet — you have just shipped the default.
</Callout>

<KeyNotes>
  1. A scheduler makes two decisions per step: who gets the next seat, and who gets evicted. Both are policy.
  2. FIFO is the safe default and is perfectly fair, but a long request blocks everyone behind it for as long as it runs.
  3. Priority reorders who gets capacity; it does not create any. In the visual, the low-priority request waits 275 steps instead of 195.
  4. Starvation happens when high-priority traffic never stops; **aging** (priority rising with wait) is the standard cure and bounds the worst wait.
  5. Fairness is the spread of waits, not the average. Equal waits are fair and often a bad experience — bound the p99 instead.
  6. Cancelling on client disconnect is free: it returns a seat and its KV cache, and is the cheapest throughput win in serving.
  7. Preemption evicts a sequence and replays the tokens it already streamed, so the cost is proportional to how much it had generated.
  8. Evict the newest sequence: it has generated the least, so it is the cheapest to rebuild. Preemption should be a safety net, not a routine event.
</KeyNotes>
