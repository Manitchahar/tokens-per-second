Every vLLM deployment has a config file that somebody wrote in a hurry. The good news: it is only four numbers that matter, and all four can be computed before you touch a GPU. The bad news is that they interact, and a config that is wrong in one place is usually wrong in three.

This lesson is the practical one. We take **Llama-3.1-8B on one H100** — the setup you have been using since Module 02 — and derive every number from the memory budget and the workload.

## The method, before the knobs

Three rules, all of them from Module 06 · Measure Like a Pro:

1. **Change one flag at a time.** These four interact, so a config where you moved all four and got 30% more throughput has taught you nothing.
2. **Fix the workload first.** Tune against a *fixed* request-length distribution and a fixed output length. A benchmark that grows the prompts every run is measuring the workload, not the engine.
3. **Write the operating point down.** Model, GPU, dtype, the four flags, the four numbers (p50/p95 TTFT, TPOT, output tok/s) and the date. Your <Term t="operating-point">operating point</Term> is that record. Module 25 · Inference Economics and Module 23 · Routing & Autoscaling both depend on you being able to look it up.

  <Step title="1. Read the log first">

  <Step title="3. gpu_memory_utilization: leave room">

  </Step>

## 1 · `max_model_len`: the promise, not the setting

Nothing in the decode loop scales with <Term t="max-model-len">`max_model_len`</Term>. It is a **promise about the worst case**, not a speed setting: it decides how many longest-case requests can be resident in the KV pool at once.

The KV cost of a request is exactly its context length times 128 KB (Llama-3.1-8B in BF16, from Module 05). So the worst case is linear and completely predictable:

$$
\text{KV per request} = \text{max\_model\_len} \times 128\ \text{KB}
$$

With a 52.7 GB pool:

| `max_model_len` | Worst case per request | Fits at once |
|---|---|---|
| 32,768 | 4.19 GB | **12** |
| 16,384 | 2.10 GB | **25** |
| 8,192 | 1.05 GB | **49** |
| 4,096 | 0.52 GB | **98** |

<Callout type="mentor" title="How to choose it, in four steps">
  Pull the real token histogram from your logs — `usage.prompt_tokens` plus `usage.completion_tokens`, at the p99, not the average. Set `max_model_len` to that, rounded up, plus a margin. Reject anything longer **at your gateway**, with a 400, rather than letting it reach the engine. Only promise 32k if you genuinely serve 32k.
</Callout>

The thing that makes this counter-intuitive: `max_model_len` does not slow a short request down **at all**. Nothing in the decode loop scales with it. It only decides how many longest-case requests can be resident together. Setting it to the model's advertised maximum is giving away concurrency for a case you will never see.

## 2 · `gpu_memory_utilization`: how much of the card is yours

<Term t="gpu-memory-utilization">`gpu_memory_utilization`</Term> is a fraction of the GPU's **total** memory that vLLM may plan to use. Inside that budget sit the weights, the activation reserve and the KV pool. Move it and you move the pool one-for-one.

$$
\text{pool} = \text{util} \times M - \text{weights} - \text{reserve}
$$

<Callout type="math" title="The same H100 at three settings">
  Weights 16.06 GB, reserve ≈ 3.2 GB, so the pool is $(0.90 - 0.24) \times 80 = 52.7$ GB at `0.90`.

  | util | budget | KV pool | tokens of context | 8k-token requests |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.60 | 48.0 GB | 28.7 GB | 219k | 26 |
| 0.90 | 72.0 GB | 52.7 GB | 402k | 49 |
| 0.95 | 76.0 GB | 56.7 GB | 433k | 53 |

  The last row buys you 8% more concurrency and a real chance of `CUDA out of memory` during startup, because 4 GB of headroom is not enough for the CUDA context, NCCL buffers and fragmentation.
</Callout>

Rules of thumb:

- **Dedicated GPU:** `0.90` is the default at the time of writing and it is a good default.
- **GPU shared with training or another process:** drop to `0.4`–`0.6` and subtract the other tenant's live usage by hand. vLLM measures free memory at startup, but its plan is still based on the *total*.
- **Never lower it to "save memory" on a dedicated card.** Unused memory is not a virtue on a machine nobody else is using.
- **Watch `vllm:gpu_cache_usage_perc`.** Above ~90% you are one long request away from preemption; below ~50% you are paying for a pool you are not using.

## 3 · `max_num_seqs`: where the curves meet your SLO

<Term t="max-num-seqs">`max_num_seqs`</Term> is the flag people set to round numbers, and it is the one where the arithmetic is most worth doing. Take **Llama-3.1-8B on an H100 at 2,048 tokens of context per user** (a mid-length chat). Per sequence, the KV read on every decode step is **268 MB** — 2,048 tokens × 128 KB — on top of the 16.06 GB of weights.

| Sequences running | Step time | Per user | Total throughput | GPU memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7.6 ms | 132 tok/s | 132 tok/s | 19.5 GB |
| 8 | 8.3 ms | 121 tok/s | 965 tok/s | 21.4 GB |
| 32 | 10.7 ms | 93 tok/s | 2,991 tok/s | 27.9 GB |
| 64 | 13.9 ms | 72 tok/s | 4,603 tok/s | 36.4 GB |
| 128 | 20.3 ms | 49 tok/s | 6,304 tok/s | 53.6 GB |
| 226 | 30.1 ms | 33 tok/s | 7,502 tok/s | 79.9 GB |
| 512 | — | — | — | **does not fit** |

Every row is the same three lines of arithmetic:

$$
t = \frac{16.06\ \text{GB} + B \times 0.268\ \text{GB}}{3.35\ \text{TB/s} \times 0.8} + 1.5\ \text{ms}
$$

for the step time, $\frac{1}{t}$ for one user and $B/t$ in total. At $B = 226$: $(16.06 + 60.7)/2680 = 28.6$ ms of memory traffic, plus 1.5 ms of fixed overhead, so **30.1 ms**, and $\frac{1}{0.0301} = 33$ tokens per user. Memory runs out in the same place, because $(16.06 + 226 \times 0.268 + 3.2) = 79.9$ GB.

Read the table as a sentence: **per-user speed falls slowly, total throughput rises fast, and memory ends the story at 226.**

<Callout type="math" title="Pick the cap from your SLO, not your feelings">
  Module 06 defined goodput. If the promise is "no user sees fewer than 30 tokens per second", find the row where per-user speed is still above 30: that is **226 concurrent users** on this GPU — and that is also exactly where the KV pool runs out, so on this hardware your latency promise and your memory ceiling happen to agree.

  $$
  \text{goodput}_{\max} \approx 226 \times 33 = 7{,}500\ \text{output tokens/s}
  $$

  Set `max_num_seqs` near that number and you have an operating point: the SLO holds, and the throughput is the best you can get without breaking it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Raising max_num_seqs feels free, and is not">
  Between 32 and 226 sequences, per-user speed drops from 93 to 33 tok/s — a 2.8× slowdown for one user — while total throughput goes from 2,991 to 7,502, only 2.5×. That is a bad trade for an interactive product and a good one for batch summarization. Same GPU, same model, same flags: **the workload decides which is right.** If you cannot say which kind of product you are serving, you cannot set this flag.
</Callout>

## 4 · `max_num_batched_tokens`: the anti-footgun

<Term t="max-num-batched-tokens">`max_num_batched_tokens`</Term> is the engine's per-iteration token budget: the most tokens, prompt and output together, that one forward pass may process. It decides whether a long prompt gets one iteration to itself or several with decode steps in between.

One engine iteration processes at most this many tokens, counting prefill and decode together. A 4,096-token prompt at a budget of 8,192 occupies one iteration and blocks everyone else's decode for the whole of its ~130 ms. At a budget of 1,024 it is four iterations with decode steps interleaved: nobody's stream stalls, but that one user's TTFT grows by the time it waited for its turn.

| budget | iterations for a 4,096-token prompt | TTFT for that prompt | Effect on everyone else |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8,192 | 1 | ~130 ms, uninterrupted | blocked for 130 ms |
| 2,048 | 2 | ~135 ms + queueing | barely affected |
| 1,024 | 4 | ~145 ms + queueing | smooth |
| 512 | 8 | ~160 ms + queueing | smooth, slightly worse ITL |

Rules of thumb: leave it at the default for uniform workloads; drop it to **1,024–2,048** if your p99 prompt is long and p95 TTFT is the thing users complain about. Defaults have moved between versions, so read them from `--help` rather than from a blog post. The mechanism behind this is **chunked prefill**, which is the whole subject of Module 10 · Prefill & Prefix Optimization.

## 5 · Measure, then decide

The loop, and the exact numbers to record:

```text
# before and after every change
p50 / p95 TTFT          ms
p50 / p95 TPOT          ms
output tokens/s         total, at a stated concurrency
prompt tokens/s         total
GPU KV cache usage      %
preemptions             vllm:num_preemptions_total
```

| Symptom | First thing to check | Likely fix |
|---|---|---|
| TTFT high, TPOT fine | `num_requests_waiting` | More capacity, or raise `max_num_seqs` if TPOT has headroom |
| TPOT high | `Running` pinned at `max_num_seqs` | Raise the cap if memory allows, else accept it |
| KV usage above 90% | `gpu_cache_usage_perc` | Lower `max_model_len`, raise util carefully, or add GPUs |
| Preemptions climbing | pool vs traffic | Same as above; preemption is a memory symptom |
| TTFT fine, TPOT jumps at load | prefill bursts | Lower `max_num_batched_tokens` (Module 10) |

## The other flags, briefly

Not tuning knobs, but you will meet them in `--help` (names and defaults are version-specific; check yours at the time of reading):

| Flag | Why you would use it |
|---|---|
| `--dtype` | `bfloat16` on Ampere and newer, `float16` on older cards such as the T4. FP8 is not a `--dtype`: use `--quantization fp8` for the weights and `--kv-cache-dtype fp8` for the cache; both are fast on GPUs with FP8 tensor cores (Ada Lovelace, Hopper, Blackwell). |
| `--quantization` | `awq`, `gptq`, `fp8` and friends. This is the flag that shrinks the weights, and therefore grows the pool. Module 11. |
| `--tensor-parallel-size` | Shard the model across GPUs when it does not fit on one. Module 18 · Multi-GPU Parallelism. |
| `--enable-prefix-caching` | Reuse a request's prefix across requests. Module 10. |
| `--block-size` | KV block size in tokens (16 in the V1 engine at the time of writing). Changing it changes block-table overhead. |
| `--enforce-eager` | Skip CUDA graph capture, with <Term t="enforce-eager">`enforce_eager`</Term>. Slower per step, but it is the difference between running and not running on small or old GPUs — including the free Colab T4. |
| `--api-key`, `--served-model-name` | Guard and rename the endpoint. Module 24 · Observability, Reliability & Security. |
| `--disable-log-requests` | Do not log every prompt. Nobody wants a 200 KB prompt in their log aggregator. |

<KeyNotes>
  1. Four flags matter: `max_model_len`, `gpu_memory_utilization`, `max_num_seqs`, `max_num_batched_tokens`. Compute each one's consequence before setting it.
  2. `max_model_len` is a promise, not a performance setting. Pool ÷ KV-per-request gives worst-case concurrency; on an H100 with a 52.7 GB pool, 8,192 tokens gives 49 requests where 32,768 gives 12.
  3. `pool = util × total memory − weights − reserve`. Raise it for a bigger pool, but leave 4–8 GB of headroom or you OOM at startup.
4. `max_num_seqs` is where per-user speed meets total throughput. Llama-3.1-8B on an H100 at 2k context: 32 concurrent → 93 tok/s each; 128 → 49 tok/s each; past ~226 it does not fit at all.
5. Set the cap from your SLO, not a round number. With a 30 tok/s promise, about 226 concurrent users at roughly 7,500 output tok/s is the operating point.
  6. `max_num_batched_tokens` bounds how many tokens one iteration processes. Lower it when long prompts make everyone's TTFT jittery; the cost is that the long prompt waits longer too.
  7. Record the operating point: model, GPU, dtype, flags, p50/p95 TTFT and TPOT, throughput, KV usage, date. Everything downstream — autoscaling, cost, capacity — reads that record.
  8. Change one flag per run. Four changed at once is four hypotheses and one data point.
</KeyNotes>
