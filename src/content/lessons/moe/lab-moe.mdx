## Half one: the simulator

Four controls: the model, the batch size, how sharp the router is, and the capacity factor. Everything else follows.

The three numbers that matter are in the readout, and they are three different questions:

- **Imbalance** is a *straggler multiplier*. At 2.0×, the busiest card does twice the work of an evenly loaded one, and in a synchronous step it sets everyone's latency.
- **Gini** is a *reportable summary*. 0 is perfect equality, 1 is one expert taking everything.
- **Overflow** is the *quality cost* of the capacity factor — the tokens you are dropping to get rectangular buffers.

  <Step title="3. Read the load, not the mean">
    The dashed line is the perfectly balanced share. The interesting bar is the tallest one, compared against that line. **A mean load tells you nothing** — two models with identical means can differ by 10× in straggler risk.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Turn it into one number">
    The Lorenz curve bows below the diagonal; the area between them, normalised, is the Gini coefficient. Above about 0.3 you should expect stragglers. Below 0.10, be suspicious — a router that balanced is a router that never specialised.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Add the capacity constraint">
    Each expert gets $c \times$ the balanced share in slots. Push the capacity factor down and red bars appear. The predicted overflow is exactly $\max(0, 1 - c/I)$ of the hot expert's tokens — check the simulator's number against that.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Then pay for it on the wire">
    The previous lesson's formula, with your measured imbalance plugged in: $f_{\text{remote}} = (1 - 1/g)\cdot I$. Imbalance is not a free inefficiency — it multiplies communication in every MoE layer, forever.
  </Step>

### 1. Build a mixture-of-experts layer

Section 1 implements the whole mechanism: router matmul, softmax over all experts, top-k selection, per-expert MLP, and the weighted combine. Experts are stacked so one batched matmul runs them all — a real kernel gathers each expert's tokens and runs a dense GEMM, but the arithmetic is the same.

**Expected output:** the routed-assignment count sums to `tokens × k`, the output shape is preserved, and the load is near-uniform because the router bias starts at zero. **That uniformity is artificial** and section 2 destroys it.

Two things to check by hand, because they are the two bugs you will otherwise hit later: that the top-k probabilities are **renormalised to sum to 1** over the selected experts, and that the combine step indexes `topi` against the expert axis correctly.

### 2. Give the router favourites

Section 2 sweeps the router bias from 0 to 4 and prints imbalance, minimum load, Gini and top-3 share at each level.

**What to expect.** A monotone climb. At zero bias, imbalance sits near 1.0 and the Gini is small. By a bias standard deviation of 4, the hottest expert holds several times its share.

**The thing to notice:** the top-3 share saturates well below 100%. A healthy MoE uses most of its experts. If one expert took 90% of your tokens, that model is a dense model wearing a costume — and its sparsity number is a lie.

### 3. Capacity factor and overflow

Section 3 applies a hard per-expert capacity across a range of factors and prints the measured overflow rate next to the closed-form prediction $\max(0, 1 - c/I)$.

**Expected:** the two columns agree once you account for the fact that the prediction is about the *hot expert* while the measurement divides by *all* assignments — they should differ by roughly the hot expert's share of traffic. If they disagree by much more than that, you have a bug in the capacity code.

Find the smallest capacity factor with zero overflow. It should equal your measured imbalance $I$ exactly.

### 4. Train a router with the auxiliary loss

Section 4 implements the Switch-Transformer load-balancing loss,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha\, E \sum_{e} f_e \, P_e
$$

and trains a router on it alone, printing the imbalance at intervals.

**Expected:** imbalance falls toward 1.0 and Gini toward 0. Uniform load is this loss's global minimum, so with nothing pushing the other way that is where the router goes.

**Then run the experiment that matters.** Set `alpha = 0.0` and re-run: nothing changes. A router with no pressure toward balance keeps whatever preferences its initialisation had. That is the point — **the auxiliary loss is the only reason a trained MoE uses all of its experts.**

The real training run minimises $\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}$, so $\alpha$ is a dial between specialisation and balance. Too low and experts die; too high and every expert becomes an identical copy of the others, and you have paid for capacity you never used.

### 5. Imbalance becomes communication

Section 5 takes your measured imbalance into the all-to-all formula from the previous lesson and prints the volume at five imbalance levels. Then it measures the local grouped expert GEMM so the communication number has something real to be compared against.

**Expected:** the grouped GEMM takes a few milliseconds; the all-to-all over 32 layers takes a fraction of a millisecond at NVLink speeds. So at that batch, the layer is still **locally compute-bound** — which is why MoE works well at high batch.

It also prints the arithmetic intensity. Compare it to the T4's ridge point, $65\text{ TFLOP/s} \div 320\text{ GB/s} \approx 203$ FLOP/byte. A T4 has no BF16 tensor cores, which makes it a poor card for expert-heavy work — worth knowing before you benchmark on one and draw conclusions about an H100.

Then sweep batch size to find where communication stops hiding behind local compute. **That crossover is the single most useful number in this lab**: it is the "when does MoE become communication-bound" answer for your GPU.

### 6. Read a real trained MoE

Section 6 loads the published config of Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, prints its expert count, top-k, intermediate width and shared expert, then derives the expert bank size, active parameters and sparsity from the config — the same arithmetic you did by hand for Mixtral, now run against a real file.

**Expected:** a large expert count with a small active count, a narrow MoE intermediate width, and a **shared expert**. That last one matters for serving: routed experts are not guaranteed to fire for every token, so a small always-on expert provides a floor of work per token. It is a serving decision as much as a modelling one.

**What to measure on real router weights:** the per-expert row norms of `mlp.gate.weight`. A trained router with genuine favourites has rows whose norms differ substantially. Rows with near-identical norms have never differentiated — the signature of an under-utilised MoE.

### 7. When it goes wrong

Five ways an MoE layer produces a plausible model instead of a crash. The full list is at the end of the notebook; the two that have shipped more than once:

**Measuring imbalance on the wrong axis.** Counts must be per *expert* across *all* tokens. Per-token counts are always exactly $k$ — perfectly balanced by construction — so the metric reads zero imbalance on a catastrophically imbalanced router, and the bug is invisible. Check the metric against a case where you know the answer.

**Softmax taken over the selected experts instead of all of them.** This silently renormalises before selection and changes every gate value. The order is fixed: softmax across all $E$, then take top-$k$, then renormalise over the $k$.

<Callout type="mentor" title="The habit this lab is really building">
  Every section ends with a closed-form prediction printed next to a measurement. When they disagree, your first move is **not** to adjust the number to match — it is to find out which of the two is wrong, and why.

  This is the same discipline as Module 06's reproducible benchmarks, applied to routing. Load balance is the parameter most likely to differ between your benchmark traffic and production traffic, and it is the one that quietly determines whether your MoE deployment is fast or mysteriously slow.
</Callout>

**Answers, once you have done the work:**

1. Overflow of the hot expert is $\max(0, 1 - 1.25/2.4) = 47.9\%$. As a fraction of all assignments it is smaller by roughly the hot expert's traffic share, $1/8$ for Mixtral, giving about 6% overall — which is why the measured and predicted columns differ by that factor.
2. With no task loss, every expert becomes an identical copy — the router has no reason to differentiate, so the model has $E$ copies of one expert and none of the capacity advantage that motivated the design. Perfect balance without specialisation is a dense model with extra memory and an extra matmul. **Balanced is not the goal; balanced *and* specialised is.**
3. Software: overlap the all-to-all with independent compute, and schedule so a token's expert work is not serialised behind its dispatch — this moves the crossover down by hiding communication. Hardware: a faster or wider fabric (NVLink to NVSwitch, more links per GPU), which lowers the per-byte cost directly. Note that a faster GPU makes it *worse*, because local compute shrinks faster than the wire does.
4. Measure whether the router's probabilities have collapsed — a Gini of 0.08 with a mean probability near $1/128$ means balance, but if the probabilities are near-uniform while the *assignments* are balanced too, check whether expert weight row norms differ. Experts with identical norms have never differentiated regardless of what the router says. Also confirm your traffic distribution resembles the router's training distribution; a router evaluated on out-of-distribution prompts can look balanced and behave badly.
5. The straggler multiplier — imbalance $\max_e c_e / (kT/E)$ — is the number, because it is the direct multiplier on the step time of the busiest card, which in a synchronous step *is* the step time. Page when it exceeds about 1.5 for a sustained window, since above that a significant fraction of your MoE speedup is being erased by one expert. Gini is the better trend metric for dashboards; imbalance is the better paging threshold, because it maps to latency while Gini only maps to it loosely.

<KeyNotes>
  1. An MoE layer is a router matmul, a softmax across all experts, a top-k selection, a per-expert MLP, and a weighted combine. The top-k probabilities must be renormalised over the selected experts, or the layer's contribution is silently scaled wrong.
  2. Imbalance — $\max_e c_e$ divided by the balanced share — is a straggler multiplier. In a synchronous decode step the busiest card sets everyone's latency, so this number maps directly to tokens per second.
  3. The Gini coefficient summarises load as one reportable number: 0 is perfect equality, 1 is one expert taking everything. Above 0.3 expect stragglers; below 0.10 suspect the router never specialised.
  4. A capacity factor of $c$ gives each expert $\lceil c\,kT/E \rceil$ slots. The hot expert overflows by exactly $\max(0, 1 - c/I)$ of its tokens, so zero overflow requires $c \geq I$. The common default of 1.25 is a starting point, not a constant.
  5. The auxiliary loss $\alpha E \sum_e f_e P_e$ is the only thing that stops a trained MoE from collapsing onto a few experts. With it, load goes uniform; without it, dead experts are the default outcome.
  6. Balance alone is not the goal. A router trained on the auxiliary loss alone produces experts that are identical copies, which is a dense model with extra memory and an extra matmul. The training trade-off is specialisation bought per unit of balance, controlled by $\alpha$.
  7. Imbalance multiplies all-to-all traffic: $f_{\text{remote}} = (1 - 1/g)\cdot I$. The same router imbalance that hurts compute hurts communication in every MoE layer, forever.
  8. At high batch on a fast fabric the MoE layer is still locally compute-bound, and the all-to-all hides behind the GEMM. At low batch, or on PCIe, the collective's latency is exposed and the layer becomes communication-bound. Find your crossover by sweeping batch size.
  9. Measure imbalance per expert across all tokens. Per-token counts are always exactly $k$, which makes the metric read zero imbalance on a catastrophically imbalanced router.
  10. A shared expert exists for a serving reason: routed experts are not guaranteed to fire for every token, so a small always-on expert provides a floor of work per token when the router is unpredictable.
</KeyNotes>
