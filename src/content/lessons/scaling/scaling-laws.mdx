Suppose it is 2020 and you have the budget for one very large training run. You could train a 10B model or a 100B model. The 100B model costs ten times as much. Will it be ten times better? Twice as good? Barely different? You get one attempt, and it costs millions.

The obvious approach is to train both small and look. Train a 10M, a 100M and a 1B model, see how good each one is, and guess. The trouble is the guessing. If you plot the results the way you would plot anything else, you get a curve that drops steeply and then flattens, and no amount of squinting tells you whether it is about to stop improving or will keep going for another hundred-fold.

In January 2020, a group at OpenAI (Kaplan, McCandlish and others) found that the guessing is unnecessary. Plotted the right way, the results are not a curve at all. They are a **straight line**, and a straight line you can extend. That line is a <Term t="scaling-law">scaling law</Term>. This lesson is about that line: why it is straight, what its slope means, and the advice about spending compute that came out of it.

## Finding the straight line

The quantity on the vertical axis is the one you met in 3A: the **test loss**, the average surprise (cross-entropy, in nats per token) the model feels at text it was never trained on. Lower is better, and you already know it can never reach zero.

    Train a ladder of models, from 768 parameters to 1.5 billion, each one on enough data that data is not the limit. Plot test loss against parameter count on ordinary axes.

    Most of the dots are crushed against the left wall, and the big ones trail off almost flat. Is improvement stopping? You cannot tell from this picture, and that is exactly the problem.

    The loss still curves, flattening as it goes. Better, but a curve is still hard to extend.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Log both axes: a straight line">
    Now put loss on a log axis too, and watch. **The points fall on a straight line.**

    A straight line on log-log axes has a name: a <Term t="power-law">**power law**</Term>. Written out, $L(N) = (N_c / N)^{\alpha_N}$: loss is a constant divided by the parameter count, raised to a small power. Taking the log of both sides turns it into $\log L = \alpha_N \log N_c - \alpha_N \log N$, which is a straight line in $\log N$ with slope $-\alpha_N$.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. The slope is the exponent">
    For parameters, Kaplan et al. measured $\alpha_N = 0.076$. The meaning is concrete: every 10× more parameters multiplies the loss by ${}10^{-0.076} = 0.839$, a 16% drop.

    And it is the **same 16% at every scale**: going from 10M to 100M buys the same fraction as going from 1B to 10B. That is what a straight line on log-log axes means.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Data has its own straight line">
    Switch the axis to training tokens. With a model big enough not to be the bottleneck, loss against data is another straight line, with $\alpha_D = 0.095$: every 10× more data multiplies loss by 0.804.

    Two different resources, two clean power laws.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. So does compute">
    Now the one that matters for budgets. For each amount of compute, take the best model you can train with it, and plot its loss: a third straight line, $\alpha_C = 0.050$. Every 10× compute multiplies loss by 0.891, an 11% drop.

    Compute is measured here in <Term t="pf-day">petaflop/s-days</Term>: one is 10¹⁵ FLOP/s for a day, 8.64 × 10¹⁹ FLOPs.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. Extrapolate before you pay">
    Here is the payoff. The line was fitted on models up to 1.5B parameters. Extend it, dashed, to 174B and read off a prediction: about 1.61 nats, 30% below the 1.5B model, **before anyone has trained the big one**.

    GPT-3's own eight models, up to 100 times larger than anything Kaplan trained, kept following a power law in compute. The line held.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. Kaplan’s advice: spend it on parameters">
    If loss falls with both N and D, how should a growing budget be split? Kaplan et al. fitted the best split and found **N ∝ C^0.73 and D ∝ C^0.27**: for 10× compute, a 5.4× bigger model trained on only 1.9× more data.

    This is why GPT-3 has 175B parameters but saw only 300B tokens. Two years later the advice was overturned, which is the next lesson.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Then derive it properly

### Why log-log turns a power law into a line

A power law says one quantity is proportional to a power of another:

$$
L = \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha} = N_c^{\alpha}\, N^{-\alpha}
$$

Take the logarithm of both sides. Logs turn powers into multiplication and products into sums:

$$
\log L = \alpha \log N_c - \alpha \log N
$$

Call $x = \log N$ and $y = \log L$. Then $y = (\alpha \log N_c) - \alpha x$: a straight line with intercept $\alpha \log N_c$ and slope $-\alpha$. No other kind of curve does this. An exponential is straight on a *semi*-log plot; a power law is straight on a *log-log* plot. So when experimental points line up on log-log axes, you have found a power law, and the slope tells you its exponent.

The exponent is a **ratio of ratios**. Multiply $N$ by any factor $k$ and the loss is multiplied by $k^{-\alpha}$, no matter where you start:

$$
\frac{L(kN)}{L(N)} = \frac{(N_c / kN)^{\alpha}}{(N_c / N)^{\alpha}} = k^{-\alpha}
$$

That is why "10× the parameters gives 16% lower loss" is true at 10M and at 100B alike.

### Kaplan's three laws

Kaplan et al. trained transformer language models on WebText2 (the same web text that became part of GPT-3's mix), with sizes from 768 to 1.5 billion non-embedding parameters and datasets from 22 million to 23 billion tokens. Their headline fits, each measured with the other resources large enough not to be the limit:

| Resource | Law | Exponent | 10× more multiplies loss by | 2× more multiplies loss by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parameters $N$ | $L = (8.8 \times 10^{13} / N)^{0.076}$ | $\alpha_N$ = 0.076 | ${}10^{-0.076}$ = 0.839 | ${}2^{-0.076}$ = 0.949 |
| Data $D$ (tokens) | $L = (5.4 \times 10^{13} / D)^{0.095}$ | $\alpha_D$ = 0.095 | ${}10^{-0.095}$ = 0.804 | ${}2^{-0.095}$ = 0.936 |
| Compute $C$ (PF-days) | $L = (3.1 \times 10^{8} / C)^{0.050}$ | $\alpha_C$ = 0.050 | ${}10^{-0.050}$ = 0.891 | ${}2^{-0.050}$ = 0.966 |

Some details matter if you ever use these numbers:

- $N$ counts <Term t="non-embedding-params">**non-embedding**</Term> parameters: the weights in the transformer blocks, leaving out the token and position tables. Kaplan et al. found the trends were much cleaner that way.
- The loss is cross-entropy in nats on their WebText2 test set, with their tokenizer. Change the data or the tokenizer and the constants change. The *shape*, a straight line on log-log axes, is what transfers.
- $C$ is the compute of the best model at each budget (the compute-efficient frontier), in PF-days. Compute was counted with the rule from lesson 1, $C \approx 6ND$ (they wrote it ${}6NBS$: batch size times number of steps is $D$).

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: what the exponents say">
  **Bigger model.** Go from a 1.5B-parameter model to 174B, a factor of 116. Loss is multiplied by

  $$116^{-0.076} = e^{-0.076 \times \ln 116} = e^{-0.076 \times 4.754} = e^{-0.361} = 0.697$$

  so it falls by about 30%. The law predicts 2.30 nats at 1.5B and 2.30 × 0.697 = 1.61 nats at 174B.

  **How steep the road is.** To *halve* the loss with parameters alone you need ${}2^{1/0.076} = 2^{13.2} \approx 9{,}100$ times more parameters. With compute alone, ${}2^{1/0.050} = 2^{20} \approx 1{,}000{,}000$ times more compute. Every step of improvement costs a multiple, not an increment.
</Callout>

That last line is the sobering half of the story. A power law with a small exponent means **progress is reliable but expensive**. Each 10× of compute buys the same 11%, forever (until the law breaks), and each 10× costs ten times more money than the last.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why would a law this clean exist?">
  Nobody fully knows. A useful picture: language contains a vast number of patterns of very different frequency, from "a sentence ends with a period" (everywhere) to the rules of some rare idiom (almost nowhere). A bigger model, or more data, lets the model learn patterns further down that list. If pattern frequencies follow a smooth heavy-tailed distribution, as word frequencies do, each 10× of capacity reaches the next slice of rarer patterns, and each slice is worth a similar fraction of the remaining loss.

  Treat this as intuition, not proof. The empirical fact is what you can build on: the line has held across many orders of magnitude, in many labs, for many model families.
</Callout>

### Fitting your own line

The practical use of all this is cheap prediction: train a few small models, fit the line, read off the big one. Here is the whole procedure, with six "runs" generated from Kaplan's law plus 1% noise:

```python
import math, random

random.seed(0)
Nc, alpha = 8.8e13, 0.076                    # Kaplan et al. 2020, L(N)
sizes = [3e6, 1e7, 3e7, 1e8, 3e8, 1e9]        # your six small training runs
loss = [(Nc / n) ** alpha * random.uniform(0.99, 1.01) for n in sizes]

# a straight line through (log N, log L): least squares by hand
xs = [math.log10(n) for n in sizes]
ys = [math.log10(l) for l in loss]
mx, my = sum(xs) / len(xs), sum(ys) / len(ys)
slope = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
icept = my - slope * mx

predict = lambda n: 10 ** (icept + slope * math.log10(n))
print(f"fitted exponent : {-slope:.3f}")
print(f"predicted loss at 1B  : {predict(1e9):.3f}")
print(f"predicted loss at 100B: {predict(1e11):.3f}  (true law: {(Nc/1e11)**alpha:.3f})")
```

```text
fitted exponent : 0.078
predicted loss at 1B  : 2.367
predicted loss at 100B: 1.656  (true law: 1.674)
```

Notice what 1% noise did. The fitted exponent came out 0.078 instead of 0.076, and the error is invisible inside the range of the data, but extrapolated 100× beyond it the prediction is off by about 1%. In loss terms that sounds small, but at this scale 1% of loss is the difference between models a generation apart. **Extrapolation amplifies small errors in the slope**, so real labs fit on many runs, spanning as many orders of magnitude as they can afford, and they check the prediction with medium-sized runs before betting on the big one.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="A power law has no floor, and real loss does">
  Follow $L = (N_c / N)^{0.076}$ far enough and it heads toward zero. But in 3A you saw that the loss on real text has a floor: language is not fully predictable, so even a perfect model would still be surprised by some tokens. So the straight line *must* eventually bend and flatten toward that floor. Kaplan et al. said as much: the trends cannot continue forever.

  Inside the range anyone could afford in 2020 the bend was not visible. Lesson 4's formula adds the floor explicitly, as a constant $E$.
</Callout>

## The other findings

The paper is called "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models", and the three straight lines are only its headline. Several other results shaped how the next generation of models were built.

- **Shape barely matters.** With the parameter count fixed, changing depth versus width, the number of attention heads, or the MLP width moved the loss by only a few percent across wide ranges. What matters is how many parameters, not how they are arranged (within reason).
- **Overfitting is predictable too.** When both $N$ and $D$ are limited, loss follows one combined formula, and the penalty for too little data depends on the ratio $N^{0.74}/D$. The paper's rule of thumb: each time the model grows 8×, the data needs to grow only about 5× to avoid a penalty, because 8^0.74 ≈ 4.7.
- **Big models learn faster per token.** A larger model reaches any given loss after seeing fewer tokens than a small one. So for a fixed amount of compute, the paper found it better to train a very large model and **stop well before it has converged** than to train a small model to completion.

## How to spend a budget, according to Kaplan

That last finding led to the paper's most influential conclusion. If you have compute $C$ and can choose any $N$ and $D$ with ${}6ND = C$, what split gives the lowest loss? Kaplan et al. fitted it and found that the best model size grows almost as fast as the budget:

$$
N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.73}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.27}
$$

The two exponents add to 1, as they must, because $N \times D$ has to grow in proportion to $C$.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: 10× and 100× more compute, Kaplan's way">
  For 10× compute: parameters grow by ${}10^{0.73} = 5.4\times$, data by ${}10^{0.27} = 1.9\times$. Check: 5.4 × 1.9 = 10.

  For 100× compute: parameters grow by ${}100^{0.73} = 28.8\times$, data by ${}100^{0.27} = 3.5\times$. Check: 28.8 × 3.5 ≈ 100.

  So a lab that grew its budget 100-fold was told to build a model 29 times bigger and feed it less than 4 times more text.
</Callout>

That is very nearly what happened. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens), Gopher (280B, 300B tokens), Jurassic-1 (178B) and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B) were all enormous models trained on a few hundred billion tokens. Parameter counts raced upward; token counts barely moved.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Why the advice turned out to be wrong">
  The exponents in the table above have held up well. The 0.73 has not. Hoffmann et al. (next lesson) showed that Kaplan's experiments used the same learning-rate schedule length for every run regardless of how long it trained, which makes shorter runs look worse than they should and pushes the fit toward big models trained briefly. Later work also pointed to counting only non-embedding parameters and to fitting mostly on small models. Corrected, the best split is close to **equal growth** of $N$ and $D$.

  The general lesson is worth more than the specific number: a fit is only as good as the experiments behind it. Extrapolating a slope over three orders of magnitude multiplies every systematic error in it.
</Callout>

## Why an inference engineer cares

Scaling laws are about training, but they decide what you end up serving. Kaplan's advice produced a generation of very large models trained on relatively little data, models that were expensive to serve for the quality they delivered: every token of a 175B model costs 350 GFLOPs and reads 350 GB of weights. The correction in the next lesson, and the even stronger correction in lesson 5, are what produced the 7B and 8B models that run on one GPU, the models most of this course is about serving.

There is also a habit to take from this lesson into every benchmark you run: **when something spans orders of magnitude, plot it on log axes**. Latency against batch size, cost against model size, throughput against context length: a straight line on log-log axes tells you there is a power law to exploit, and its slope tells you the price of the next 10×.

<KeyNotes>
  1. Test loss against parameters, data or compute is a straight line on log-log axes: a power law, $L = (X_c / X)^{\alpha}$.
  2. On log-log axes the slope is $-\alpha$; multiplying the resource by $k$ multiplies the loss by $k^{-\alpha}$, the same fraction at every scale.
  3. Kaplan et al.: $\alpha_N$ = 0.076 (10× parameters → loss × 0.839), $\alpha_D$ = 0.095 (× 0.804), $\alpha_C$ = 0.050 (× 0.891), with $N$ counting non-embedding parameters.
  4. Small exponents mean progress is reliable but expensive: halving loss with compute alone would take about a million times more compute.
  5. A power law fitted on small runs can predict a big one before it is trained, but errors in the slope grow with every order of magnitude of extrapolation.
  6. A pure power law has no floor; real loss does, so the line must eventually bend.
  7. Kaplan's allocation advice was $N \propto C^{0.73}$, $D \propto C^{0.27}$: for 10× compute, a 5.4× bigger model on 1.9× more data. It produced GPT-3, Gopher and other huge models trained on ~300B tokens.
  8. That allocation was later corrected toward equal growth of N and D, partly because every run used the same learning-rate schedule length.
</KeyNotes>
