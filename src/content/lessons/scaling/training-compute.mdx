In Module 01 you derived a rule that has served you ever since: a forward pass costs about **2 FLOPs per parameter per token**. One multiply and one add for every weight, because every token passes through every weight once.

So here is a question that sounds like it should be easy. GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters and was trained on 300 billion tokens. How many FLOPs did that take? The obvious answer is to reuse the rule: 2 × 175 × 10⁹ × 300 × 10⁹ = **1.05 × 10²³ FLOPs**. Then you open the GPT-3 paper and find, in an appendix table, **3.14 × 10²³**. You were off by a factor of three.

That factor is not a rounding error, and it is not a detail. It is the difference between a \$200,000 training run and a \$600,000 one, and between a run that finishes in three days and one that finishes in nine. This lesson finds where the missing two thirds went, and by the end you will be able to price any training run on the back of an envelope, which is exactly what the rest of this module needs.

## The missing two thirds

<Scrolly>
  <TrainingCompute client:visible slot="stage" />

  <Step title="1. One layer, twelve weights">
    Shrink the problem until you can see every part of it. A layer with **4 inputs and 3 outputs** has 4 × 3 = **12 weights**, one per line in the picture. Call the number of weights $N$, so here $N = 12$.

    One token arrives as a vector of 4 numbers. Our job is to count every FLOP this layer does while *learning* from that one token.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. Forward: 2 FLOPs per weight">
    The forward pass is the matrix multiply you already know: each weight multiplies one input and adds the result into one output. **One multiply plus one add is 2 FLOPs**, so the forward pass costs 2 × 12 = 24 FLOPs.

    For a whole model this is the familiar ${}2N$ per token. If training stopped here, your guess would have been right.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Backward, part 1: blame flows back">
    But training does not stop at the output. In Module 03 you saw how a model learns: compute the loss, then work out how much every number contributed to it. That second half is the <Term t="backward-pass">backward pass</Term>.

    First, the error at each output has to be sent back to the inputs, so the layer before this one can learn too. It travels back along the **same 12 weights**, in the other direction, and each one does another multiply and add. That is another ${}2N$.
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Backward, part 2: each weight’s gradient">
    Second, each weight needs its own gradient: how much the loss would change if *this* weight moved a little. For the weight joining input $i$ to output $j$ it is simply input $i$ times the error at output $j$.

    One multiply, and (summed over the tokens of a batch) one add, per weight: **another ${}2N$**.
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. 6 FLOPs per weight per token">
    Add the three: ${}2N + 2N + 2N = 6N$ FLOPs to learn from one token, **three times** what it costs to run the model on that token. Our 12 weights cost 72 FLOPs.

    The optimizer then nudges every weight, but it does that **once per batch**, and a batch holds millions of tokens. Spread over them, its cost per token rounds to zero.
  </Step>

  <Step title="6. Times every training token: C ≈ 6ND">
    Nothing about a token changes the arithmetic: the first token of training and the last both pay ${}6N$. So a run over $D$ tokens costs

    $$C \approx 6ND$$

    This is the <Term t="training-compute">training compute</Term> of the run.

    Drag the sliders. Compute grows **in proportion to both**: twice the parameters is twice the bill, twice the data is twice the bill.
  </Step>

  <Step title="7. GPT-3: 3.15 × 10²³ FLOPs">
    Now the check. 6 × 175 × 10⁹ × 300 × 10⁹ = **3.15 × 10²³ FLOPs**. The paper reports 3.14 × 10²³, because it counts 174.6 billion parameters, not a rounded 175.

    Notice where the 2024 models sit on the ladder: Llama-3.1-8B, a model **22 times smaller** than GPT-3, used more training compute, because it read 50 times more text. Hold that thought until lesson 5.
  </Step>

  <Step title="8. From FLOPs to GPU-hours and dollars">
    FLOPs are not a bill yet. Divide by what one GPU really sustains, about **40% of the H100’s 989 TFLOP/s**, and you get GPU-seconds. Divide by 3,600 for <Term t="gpu-hour">GPU-hours</Term>; multiply by an hourly rate for dollars.

    For GPT-3 on H100s that is about **221,000 GPU-hours**: nine days on 1,024 GPUs, roughly \$550,000 at \$2.50 per GPU-hour. Switch the GPU and watch the same FLOPs cost a different amount of time.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Then derive it properly

The twelve-weight picture is the whole idea. Now let us do it with real shapes, so that you can trust the rule for a 400-billion-parameter model as much as for a toy.

### One linear layer, one batch

Take one weight matrix $W$ of shape `[k × n]`, so it holds $kn$ parameters. A batch of $b$ tokens arrives as a matrix $X$ of shape `[b × k]`, one row per token. During training three matrix multiplies touch $W$.

**1. Forward.** The output is $Y = XW$, with shape `[b × n]`. From Module 01, a `[b × k] × [k × n]` multiply costs

$$
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{fwd}} = 2bkn
$$

**2. Backward, gradient for the input.** The loss sends back an error for every output number, a matrix $\delta = \partial L / \partial Y$ of the same shape as $Y$, `[b × n]`. The previous layer needs the error at *its* outputs, which are this layer's inputs. The chain rule says it is

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial X} = \delta\, W^{\top}, \qquad [b \times n] \times [n \times k] \to [b \times k]
$$

Same three sizes, multiplied in a different order: ${}2bnk$ FLOPs.

**3. Backward, gradient for the weights.** Each weight $W_{ij}$ connected input $i$ to output $j$. Nudge it, and output $j$ of every token moves by that token's input $i$. So its gradient is the sum, over the batch, of input $i$ times error $j$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = X^{\top}\delta, \qquad [k \times b] \times [b \times n] \to [k \times n]
$$

Again the same three sizes: ${}2kbn$ FLOPs.

Add them up:

$$
\text{FLOPs per training step for this matrix} = 2bkn + 2bkn + 2bkn = 6\,(kn)\,b = 6 \times \text{parameters} \times \text{tokens}
$$

That is the rule, with nothing approximate about it for a single matrix. A transformer is almost entirely such matrices (you counted them for Llama-3.1-8B in Module 01), and every token passes through every one of them, so summing over the model gives ${}6N$ per token, and over a whole run, ${}6ND$.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why the backward pass costs twice the forward">
  Every weight is used three times per token. Once to compute the answer, once to pass blame back to the layer below, and once to work out its own correction. The first use is the forward pass; the other two are the backward pass. Each use is one multiply and one add.

  So backward = 2 × forward is not a coincidence of implementation. It falls out of the fact that a weight sits between two things that both need a gradient: the input it read, and the weight itself.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: the toy layer, every FLOP">
  Our layer has $k = 4$ inputs, $n = 3$ outputs, so $kn = 12$ weights. One token, $b = 1$.

  - Forward $Y = XW$: 2 × 1 × 4 × 3 = **24** FLOPs.
  - Input gradient $\delta W^{\top}$: 2 × 1 × 3 × 4 = **24** FLOPs.
  - Weight gradient $X^{\top}\delta$: 2 × 4 × 1 × 3 = **24** FLOPs.

  Total **72 FLOPs** = 6 × 12 weights × 1 token. Now make it $b = 1{,}000$ tokens: every line scales by 1,000, total 72,000 = 6 × 12 × 1,000.
</Callout>

You can check the counting by brute force. Here is a matrix multiply written as three loops, which adds 2 to a counter for every multiply-add, then the forward pass and both halves of the backward pass for a batch of 5 tokens:

```python
import random

flops = 0
def matmul(A, B):
    """[m x k] times [k x n], counting one multiply and one add per term."""
    global flops
    m, k, n = len(A), len(B), len(B[0])
    C = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(m)]
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(n):
            for t in range(k):
                C[i][j] += A[i][t] * B[t][j]
                flops += 2
    return C

def T(M):  # transpose
    return [list(r) for r in zip(*M)]

b, k, n = 5, 4, 3                      # 5 tokens, 4 inputs, 3 outputs
X = [[random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(k)] for _ in range(b)]
W = [[random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(k)]

Y = matmul(X, W);          fwd = flops                 # forward
delta = [[1.0] * n for _ in range(b)]                   # error arriving at Y
dX = matmul(delta, T(W));  bwd_in = flops - fwd         # gradient for the inputs
dW = matmul(T(X), delta);  bwd_w = flops - fwd - bwd_in # gradient for the weights

print(f"forward {fwd}, input grads {bwd_in}, weight grads {bwd_w}")
print(f"total {flops} = 6 x {k*n} weights x {b} tokens = {6*k*n*b}")
```

```text
forward 120, input grads 120, weight grads 120
total 360 = 6 x 12 weights x 5 tokens = 360
```

Three equal thirds, exactly as derived. Real frameworks (PyTorch's `FlopCounterMode`, for example) count the same three matrix multiplies on a real layer and get the same 6 × parameters × tokens.

### What 6ND leaves out

The rule counts the weight matrices. Three other things happen during training, and it is worth knowing how big each one is.

| Left out | Cost | Why it is usually small |
|---|---|---|
| Attention scores, $QK^{\top}$ and the weighted sum of $V$ | about ${}2\,n_{\text{layer}}\,n_{\text{ctx}}\,d_{\text{model}}$ per token forward, ×3 for training | grows with context, not parameters |
| Embedding lookup | a memory read, no multiplies | 0 FLOPs |
| Optimizer step (Adam) | about a dozen FLOPs per parameter **per batch** | a batch holds millions of tokens |

For GPT-3 the attention term is 2 × 96 layers × 2,048 tokens × 12,288 = 4.8 × 10⁹ FLOPs per token, against ${}2N$ = 3.5 × 10¹¹. That is **1.4%**. The optimizer is even smaller: GPT-3's batch held 3.2 million tokens, so a dozen FLOPs per parameter per batch is about 12 ÷ 3,200,000 ≈ 0.000004 FLOPs per parameter per token.

Attention does stop being small when the context is long compared to the model width: the ratio is roughly $n_{\text{ctx}} / (12\,d_{\text{model}})$ in Kaplan et al.'s accounting (for GPT-3, 2,048 ÷ (12 × 12,288) = 1.4%, the same number). A small model trained at 32K context can spend a large fraction of its compute on attention. For the big models in this module it is a percent or two, and we ignore it.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Two different “utilizations”">
  Big training runs often save memory by **throwing away activations** during the forward pass and recomputing them during the backward pass (<Term t="activation-checkpointing">activation checkpointing</Term>). The GPU then really does a second forward pass: about ${}8N$ FLOPs per token instead of ${}6N$.

  That extra work is real, but it is not *useful*. So the standard metric, <Term t="mfu">**MFU**</Term> (model FLOPs utilization, which you met in Module 01), counts only the ${}6N$ the model needs, divided by the GPU's peak. A run that does recomputation can show 50% "hardware utilization" and 38% MFU at the same time. When you budget a training run, use ${}6ND$ and an MFU, never the hardware number.
</Callout>

## From FLOPs to GPU-hours

A FLOP count is a quantity of work. To turn it into time you need a rate, and the rate of a real training run is never the spec-sheet peak. Over a long run, a GPU spends time waiting for data, for other GPUs to finish (Module 18 shows why), for gradients to be summed across the cluster, and for checkpoints to be written. Well-run large jobs sustain roughly **35–50%** of peak.

$$
\text{GPU-seconds} = \frac{C}{\text{peak FLOP/s} \times \text{MFU}}, \qquad
\text{GPU-hours} = \frac{\text{GPU-seconds}}{3600}
$$

<Callout type="math" title="Worked: GPT-3 on H100s">
  $C = 3.15 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs. One H100 sustains 989 × 10¹² × 0.40 = 3.956 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s.

  $$
  \frac{3.15\times 10^{23}}{3.956\times 10^{14}} = 7.96\times 10^{8}\ \text{GPU-seconds} = \frac{7.96\times 10^{8}}{3600} \approx 221{,}000\ \text{GPU-hours}
  $$

  On 1,024 GPUs: 221,183 ÷ 1,024 = 216 hours = **9.0 days**. At \$2.50 per GPU-hour: 221,183 × 2.50 ≈ **\$553,000**.

  (GPT-3 itself was trained in 2020 on V100s, a much slower card, so the real run took far more GPU-hours than this. The FLOPs, though, are the same on any hardware. That is the beauty of counting work instead of time.)
</Callout>

The same function prices every run in this module:

```python
def train_cost(N, D, peak=989e12, mfu=0.40, price=2.50):
    flops = 6 * N * D                       # forward 2N + backward 4N, per token
    gpu_seconds = flops / (peak * mfu)      # what one GPU really sustains
    gpu_hours = gpu_seconds / 3600
    return flops, gpu_hours, gpu_hours * price

runs = {
    "GPT-3 175B":      (175e9, 300e9),
    "Chinchilla 70B":  (70e9, 1.4e12),
    "Llama-3.1-8B":    (8e9, 15e12),
    "Llama-3.1-405B":  (405e9, 15.6e12),
}
for name, (N, D) in runs.items():
    C, h, usd = train_cost(N, D)
    print(f"{name:15s} C = {C:.2e} FLOPs {h:12,.0f} GPU-hours  ${usd/1e6:6.2f}M")
```

```text
GPT-3 175B      C = 3.15e+23 FLOPs      221,183 GPU-hours  $  0.55M
Chinchilla 70B  C = 5.88e+23 FLOPs      412,875 GPU-hours  $  1.03M
Llama-3.1-8B    C = 7.20e+23 FLOPs      505,561 GPU-hours  $  1.26M
Llama-3.1-405B  C = 3.79e+25 FLOPs   26,617,796 GPU-hours  $ 66.54M
```

Read the table slowly, because the rest of the module is hiding in it. Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) and GPT-3 (175B, 300B tokens) cost about the same. An 8B model costs more than both. The parameter count alone tells you almost nothing about what a model cost to make; **the product $ND$ does**. Lessons 3 and 4 are about how to split a fixed $ND$ between the two.

<Callout type="real" title="Checking the rule against a published run">
  Meta's Llama 3 paper reports about 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs for Llama-3.1-405B on 15.6T tokens. The rule gives 6 × 405 × 10⁹ × 15.6 × 10¹² = 3.79 × 10²⁵. Same number.

  The model card reports **30.84 million H100-hours** for that model. At 100% of peak the work would take 3.8 × 10²⁵ ÷ 989 × 10¹² ÷ 3,600 = 10.7 million hours, so the whole run averaged 10.7 ÷ 30.84 ≈ **35%** of peak. The paper reports 38–43% MFU while the job is running; the gap is everything else a real run lives through: restarts after hardware failures, checkpoints, evaluation. Our 40% assumption lands within 15% of what actually happened.
</Callout>

## Why an inference engineer cares

You will not train a 405B model. You will, constantly, be asked questions that this lesson answers.

- **"Should we finetune or pretrain?"** Finetuning a Llama-3.1-8B on 100 million tokens costs 6 × 8 × 10⁹ × 10⁸ = 4.8 × 10¹⁸ FLOPs, about 3.4 H100-hours at 40% MFU. Pretraining it cost 150,000 times more. That ratio is why almost nobody pretrains.
- **"What did this open model really cost?"** Parameters × tokens × 6 is the first sentence of every honest answer.
- **"Training versus serving."** A token trained costs ${}6N$; a token served costs ${}2N$. Serve three times as many tokens as you trained on and you have spent as much compute running the model as making it. Lesson 5 turns that into the most important design decision in this module.

<KeyNotes>
  1. Training a model on one token costs about **6 FLOPs per parameter**: 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward pass.
  2. The backward pass is two matrix multiplies with the same shapes as the forward one: $\delta W^{\top}$ sends the error to the layer below and $X^{\top}\delta$ gives each weight its gradient.
  3. A whole run costs $C \approx 6ND$, with $N$ the parameters and $D$ the training tokens. Double either and the bill doubles.
  4. GPT-3: 6 × 175 × 10⁹ × 300 × 10⁹ = **3.15 × 10²³ FLOPs**, matching the paper's 3.14 × 10²³.
  5. GPU-hours = $C$ ÷ (peak FLOP/s × MFU) ÷ 3,600. With an H100 at 989 TFLOP/s and 40% MFU, GPT-3 is about 221,000 GPU-hours.
  6. 6ND ignores attention scores (about 1–2% for large models at 2K context), embeddings (no FLOPs) and the optimizer (once per batch, so negligible per token).
  7. MFU counts only the model's ${}6N$; recomputed activations make the hardware busier without making MFU higher.
  8. Serving costs ${}2N$ per token, a third of training, which is why heavy traffic eventually costs more than the training run itself.
</KeyNotes>
