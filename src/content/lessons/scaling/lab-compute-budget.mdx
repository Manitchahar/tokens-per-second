Five lessons of formulas: $C \approx 6ND$, GPU-hours from MFU, power laws, IsoFLOP valleys, $N = \sqrt{C/120}$, and the lifetime bill ${}6ND + 2NT$. This lab makes you use all of them at once, the way someone planning a real training run would, and checks your arithmetic against a simulator.

No GPU needed: everything runs in your browser, and every number the simulator shows is one of this module's formulas, so you can (and should) reproduce each one by hand.

## What the simulator computes

Everything is one of the module's formulas, chained:

| Readout | Formula | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Training FLOPs | $C = \text{hours} \times 3600 \times \text{peak FLOP/s} \times \text{MFU}$ | 3B.1 |
| Training tokens | $D = C / 6N$ | 3B.1 |
| Predicted loss | $L = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}$ | 3B.4 |
| Bottom of the valley | $N_{\text{opt}} = G\,(C/6)^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$; rule of thumb $\sqrt{C/120}$ | 3B.4 |
| Compute efficiency | smallest budget whose compute-optimal model reaches your loss, divided by your budget | 3B.4 |
| Serving FLOPs | ${}2N \times \text{tokens per day} \times \text{days}$ | 3B.5 |
| Serving GPU-hours | serving FLOPs ÷ (peak × 25%) ÷ 3600 | 3B.5 |
| Dollars | GPU-hours × hourly rate (illustrative: A100 \$1.60, H100 \$2.50, H200 \$3.50, B200 \$5.50) | 3B.1 |

The loss fit defaults to the **2024 refit** of the Chinchilla data (Besiroglu et al.), because its optimum agrees with Approaches 1 and 2 and the 20-tokens rule. Switch to the **printed 2022** constants to see how much the exact optimum depends on the fit (lesson 4's pitfall). The target quality is Chinchilla's own, the loss of 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens under whichever fit is selected.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Two simplifications to keep in mind">
  Serving is priced in FLOPs at an assumed 25% of peak. Real decode is memory-bound (Module 05), so its cost tracks the bytes of the weights and the batch size, not just FLOPs. Module 25 prices it properly. The FLOP estimate gets the direction and the rough size right, which is all these tasks need.

  And the loss is a *fitted* prediction, in nats on DeepMind's data. Differences of a few thousandths of a nat are inside the fit's own uncertainty. Read the readouts as "clearly better", "about the same", "clearly worse", not to the third decimal.
</Callout>

## Tasks

Do each task in order. **Write your prediction down before you touch a slider**, then check it. The expected outputs below use the default fit (2024 refit), 40% MFU, and 730 days unless a task says otherwise.

### Task 1. Turn hours into FLOPs

You have 100,000 H100-hours at 40% MFU.

1. Predict the training FLOPs by hand.
2. Predict the compute-optimal model size with the rule of 20, and its training tokens.
3. Set the budget slider to 100k, the GPU to H100, and slide $N$ to the bottom of the valley. Compare.

**Expected:** $C$ = 10⁵ × 3,600 × 989 × 10¹² × 0.40 = 1.42 × 10²³ FLOPs. $N = \sqrt{1.42 \times 10^{23} / 120}$ ≈ 34B, $D$ ≈ 20 × 34B ≈ 690B tokens. The green "bottom" marker should sit close to 34B with the default fit.

**Check yourself:** switch the GPU to A100 without changing the hours. By what factor do the FLOPs fall, and by what factor does the optimal $N$ fall? (FLOPs fall by 989 ÷ 312 = 3.17×; since $N_{\text{opt}} \propto \sqrt{C}$, $N$ falls by √3.17 = 1.78×.)

### Task 2. Reproduce Gopher's mistake

Set the budget to Gopher's: 404,000 H100-hours (step 1 of the simulator does this for you).

1. Set $N$ = 280B. Read the tokens per parameter, the loss and the efficiency.
2. Move $N$ to 70B. Read them again.
3. Switch the fit to "printed 2022" and repeat both.

**Expected (2024 refit):** 280B gives about 1.2 tokens per parameter, loss ≈ 1.993, efficiency ≈ 53%. 70B gives 20 per parameter, loss ≈ 1.975, efficiency ≈ 100%.
**Expected (printed 2022):** 280B gives loss ≈ 1.984, about 0.05 above the bottom, efficiency ≈ 27%. 70B gives loss ≈ 1.938, about 0.007 above the bottom (the printed fit's bottom is near 32B).

**Check yourself:** under both fits, which is closer to optimal, 280B or 70B? Does the answer depend on the fit? (70B under both. The fits disagree about *where* the bottom is, not about which side Gopher fell off.)

### Task 3. Feel how flat the bottom is

Keep Gopher's budget and the default fit. Slide $N$ from 15B to 300B and watch the loss.

**Expected:** the loss stays within about 0.01 nats of the bottom from roughly 30B to 200B (50B and 100B are both within 0.002), then climbs: about 0.02 above at 300B and 0.025 at 15B. Keep going and the walls get steeper.

**Check yourself:** if you were unsure whether the optimum is 50B or 100B, how much would guessing wrong cost you in loss, compared with Gopher's choice? (A few thousandths of a nat either way, versus about 0.02 for 280B. Getting the order of magnitude right matters far more than the exact number.)

### Task 4. Change the hardware, not the hours

Set 1,000,000 hours. Find the optimal $N$ on A100, H100 and B200 (40% MFU each).

**Expected:** about 61B (A100), 109B (H100) and 164B (B200). Each is $\sqrt{C/120}$ with $C$ = 10⁶ × 3,600 × peak × 0.4.

**Check yourself:** the B200 costs 2.2× the H100 per hour here (\$5.50 vs \$2.50) and delivers 2.28× the FLOPs. Which gives more FLOPs per dollar? (B200, slightly: 2,250 ÷ 5.50 = 409 TFLOP/s per \$/hour versus 989 ÷ 2.50 = 396. With these illustrative prices they are close; with real prices, check before you buy.)

### Task 5. Price the users

Go back to Gopher's budget, $N$ = 70B, and set traffic to 100 billion tokens a day for 730 days (step 4 does this).

1. Predict the serving FLOPs by hand.
2. Predict how many tokens the model can serve before serving costs as much as training, in FLOPs.

**Expected:** $T$ = 10¹¹ × 730 = 7.3 × 10¹³ tokens. Serving FLOPs = 2 × 70 × 10⁹ × 7.3 × 10¹³ = 1.02 × 10²⁵, about 18 times the training FLOPs. Break-even (lesson 5) at $T = 3D$ = 3 × 1.37T ≈ 4.1T tokens, which this traffic reaches in 41 days. In dollars the gap is wider (about 28×) because serving runs at an assumed 25% of peak instead of 40%.

**Check yourself:** the readout shows serving at about \$28.7M and training at about \$1.0M. Where does the extra factor of 1.6 between the FLOP ratio (18×) and the dollar ratio (28×) come from? (0.40 ÷ 0.25 = 1.6: each serving FLOP costs 1.6× a training FLOP at these utilizations.)

### Task 6. Find the cheapest plan that meets the target

Keep the traffic from Task 5. Your job: **meet the target quality** (the predicted-loss readout must say "meets") **at the lowest lifetime cost**. You may change the budget and $N$ freely.

Work it like an engineer, not by random sliding:

1. Pick a model size. Raise the budget until the loss just meets the target. Note the total cost.
2. Repeat for 50B, 35B, 25B, 20B, 15B, 12B.
3. Plot or tabulate total cost against $N$. Where is the minimum?

**Expected:** the minimum is near **20B parameters trained on about 15T tokens** (about 1.29 million H100-hours), roughly \$3.2M of training plus \$8.4M of serving, **about \$11.6M** in total, against about \$29.7M for the 70B Chinchilla plan. Below roughly 12B the budget needed explodes: you are approaching the minimum size that can ever reach the target.

**Check yourself:**
- Your winning plan's efficiency readout is far below 100%. Explain why that is not a contradiction. (Efficiency measures training compute only. The plan deliberately spends extra training compute to make every served token cheaper.)
- Halve the traffic to 50 billion tokens a day. Does the best $N$ get bigger or smaller? (Bigger: with less serving, the training term weighs more, so the optimum moves back toward Chinchilla.)
- Your best plan is around 700 tokens per parameter. Which real model family made a similar choice? (Llama 3, at about 1,875 for the 8B, with even more expected traffic.)

### Task 7. Where the formula should not be trusted

Set $N$ = 3B and push the budget until it meets the target (it may not be able to).

**Expected:** with the default fit, a 3B model cannot reach Chinchilla's quality at any budget on the slider; the loss stops improving long before it gets there. This is the minimum-size wall from lesson 5, near 11B for this target.

**Check yourself:** the fit says a 15B model meets the target at a few thousand tokens per parameter. Why should you be cautious about that number? (The fit was made on runs near 20 tokens per parameter; at extreme ratios gains continue but more slowly than predicted, so the real budget needed is likely higher. Check with a real medium-sized run before betting on it.)

## What you should take away

You can now answer, with arithmetic you can show, the first questions anyone asks about a model: what it cost to make, what it will cost to run, and whether its size made sense for how it will be used. When Module 04 has you pick an open model to serve, ask these questions of it: how many tokens per parameter did it see, and was it built to be cheap to train or cheap to serve?

<KeyNotes>
  1. A budget in GPU-hours becomes FLOPs only with a GPU and an MFU: $C = \text{hours} \times 3600 \times \text{peak} \times \text{MFU}$.
  2. Given $C$ and a model size $N$, the training tokens are fixed at $D = C/6N$; the compute-optimal size is near $\sqrt{C/120}$.
  3. Faster GPUs at the same hours mean more FLOPs, so the optimal model grows with the square root of the speedup.
  4. The IsoFLOP valley is flat near its bottom and steep on its walls: a factor of two off the optimum costs little, a factor of four or more costs a lot.
  5. For a model with real traffic, serving cost quickly dwarfs training cost; break-even is at three times the training tokens.
  6. The lifetime-cheapest plan for heavy traffic is a smaller model trained far past 20 tokens per parameter: a low training "efficiency" is the price of cheap serving.
  7. Every model size has a minimum quality it can reach; below it no budget helps, and the fitted laws are least reliable near that edge.
</KeyNotes>
